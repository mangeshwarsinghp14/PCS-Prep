**Q1.** 'More number of constitutional amendments in India were carried out because of political necessity than of constitutional need'. Elucidate. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Amendments and basic structure are core polity; new bills (One Nation One Election, 2024) keep it live.

*Intro:* Article 368 lets Parliament amend the Constitution; 106 amendment Acts had been passed by 2023 (106th: women's reservation).

*Points:*
- Genuine constitutional need: 7th (1956) state reorganisation; 73rd/74th (1992) local bodies; 86th (2002) right to education; 101st (2016) GST — these responded to real structural gaps.
- Political necessity: 24th, 25th (1971) and 39th (1975) amendments sought to override courts and protect the PM's election.
- 42nd Amendment (1976, Emergency) — 'mini-Constitution', curbed judicial review; largely reversed by the 44th (1978).
- Ninth Schedule (1st Amendment, 1951) was used later to shield many laws from judicial review — curbed in I.R. Coelho (2007).
- Anti-defection (52nd, 1985; 91st, 2003) and EWS quota (103rd, 2019) — responses to political pressures as much as constitutional gaps.
- Courts as check: Kesavananda Bharati (1973) basic structure; Minerva Mills (1980); NJAC (99th Amendment) struck down in 2015.
- Counter-view: frequent amendments also show the Constitution's flexibility; many were technical or needed for new states/fiscal reform.
- Concern: hurried passage with little debate or committee scrutiny; one-party majorities make amendment easy.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab-related: Punjab Reorganisation (1966) used ordinary law under Article 3, not amendment; Article 371-type demands and river-water issues show political pressures on constitutional design.

*Conclusion:* Amendments mix necessity and politics; basic-structure review and wider consultation keep politics within constitutional limits.

*Diagram:* Two columns: 'need-based' vs 'politics-driven' amendments with numbers.

**Q2(a).** 'Probity in public life is an internally induced behaviour than an externally administered action'. Explain. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Probity is a syllabus phrase; ethics-in-governance questions recur.

*Intro:* Probity means integrity, uprightness and honesty in public conduct; it flows mainly from an official's own values.

*Points:*
- Laws (Prevention of Corruption Act 1988, Lokpal Act 2013) and conduct rules can punish, but cannot create honesty.
- Internal conscience works even where no one is watching — the gap that rules cannot cover.
- Values come from family, education and role models; civil-service training must build them.
- External checks (audit, RTI, vigilance) are still needed as a safety net and deterrent.
- Best result: internalised values + supportive institutional culture + transparent systems.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's Vigilance Bureau and anti-corruption helpline work best when officers are personally committed to integrity.

*Conclusion:* External rules set the floor; inner values set the ceiling of probity.

**Q2(b).** Examine any of the two recent E-Governance initiatives to illustrate how they have enhanced transparency and efficiency in government services. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* E-governance is asked nearly every year; new platforms keep appearing.

*Intro:* E-governance cuts discretion, delay and middlemen by putting services and money flows online.

*Points:*
- Direct Benefit Transfer with JAM (Jan Dhan–Aadhaar–Mobile): subsidies and pensions go straight to bank accounts, removing ghost beneficiaries.
- Government e-Marketplace (GeM, 2016): online, open procurement with price comparison, reducing collusion and delay.

- Limits: digital divide, Aadhaar authentication failures, data privacy (DPDP Act 2023 now applies).

*Punjab:*
- Punjab: Sewa Kendras deliver hundreds of citizen services; doorstep delivery of services via helpline 1076 (from December 2023); e-PunjabSchool and online land records.

*Conclusion:* E-governance improves transparency when backed by digital literacy and grievance redress.

**Q3.** Analyse the major issues creating tension in relationship between the Centre and the States with reference to recent controversies. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Centre–State friction is a perennial and very current theme for Punjab (Governor, RDF, BBMB, SYL).

*Intro:* India is a 'Union of States' with a strong Centre; recent years have seen sharper Centre–State friction.

*Points:*
- Governor's role: delays on bills and summoning of sessions; Supreme Court in State of Punjab v. Principal Secretary to the Governor (November 2023) held that a Governor cannot sit on bills indefinitely; Tamil Nadu case (April 2025) went further and was revisited through a 2025 Presidential reference.
- Fiscal issues: end of GST compensation (June 2022), rising cesses and surcharges outside the divisible pool, conditions on borrowing.
- Legislating on state subjects: the three farm laws (2020) on agriculture/markets, repealed in 2021.
- Central agencies (CBI, ED) and withdrawal of general consent by several states.
- Security jurisdiction: BSF's area of operation extended to 50 km from the border in Punjab, West Bengal and Assam (October 2021).
- All-India services deputation rules, use of Article 356 in the past, and delays in centrally sponsored scheme funds.
- Institutions: Inter-State Council meets rarely; Sarkaria (1988) and Punchhi (2010) recommendations largely unimplemented.
- Way forward: cooperative federalism via NITI Aayog and GST Council, timelines for Governors, regular ISC meetings.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab: withholding of Rural Development Fund (RDF), BBMB membership rules, Chandigarh's status, SYL canal dispute and Punjab University governance.
- Punjab's 2023 Supreme Court win on Governor's assent is a landmark.

*Conclusion:* Trust-based cooperative federalism, not unilateralism, is needed to keep the Union strong.

**Q4.** The phenomenon of political defections in India has assumed large proportions in recent years. Bring out the factors responsible for this. Is it right to say that ban on defection will amount to suppression of freedom of thought and action? *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Defections keep toppling state governments; Tenth Schedule reform is a recurring question.

*Intro:* The Tenth Schedule (52nd Amendment, 1985) disqualifies legislators who voluntarily give up party membership or defy the whip; yet defections continue.

*Points:*
- Recent cases: Karnataka (2019), Madhya Pradesh (2020), Maharashtra (2022) — governments fell through mass resignations or 'mergers'.
- Factors: lure of office and money, weak inner-party democracy, personality-centric parties, Speaker's partisan delays, 2/3 merger loophole (split clause removed by 91st Amendment, 2003).
- Resignation route bypasses disqualification; by-elections reward defectors.
- Kihoto Hollohan (1992): upheld the law and made Speaker's decision subject to judicial review.
- Keisham Meghachandra Singh (2020): Speaker should decide within about three months; Subhash Desai (2023) on the Maharashtra crisis.
- Freedom of thought argument: whips apply on all votes, so MPs cannot vote by conscience even on non-critical issues — a real concern.
- Counter: voters elect on party tickets; stability and mandate need protection.
- Balance: Dinesh Goswami Committee (1990) and Law Commission (170th report, 1999) suggested limiting whips to confidence/money bills and giving decisions to the President/Governor on EC advice.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab has seen defections before elections (2022) rather than mid-term government toppling; local body defections are common.

*Conclusion:* The ban is not suppression but needs reform — narrow whips, time-bound neutral adjudication — to protect both stability and conscience.

*Diagram:* Flowchart: defection → Speaker petition → time limit → judicial review.

**Q5.** "The Representation of the People Act, 1951 is not an effective preventive law against criminalization of politics and corruption in public life". Analyse. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* RPA and criminalisation are perennial; ADR data after each election keeps it live.

*Intro:* The RPA 1951 governs elections, disqualifications and corrupt practices, but criminals continue to enter legislatures.

*Points:*
- Section 8 disqualifies only on conviction (2 years or more for most offences); trials take years, so charge-sheeted candidates contest.
- Lily Thomas (2013) struck down Section 8(4) that protected sitting legislators — a step forward.
- ADR analysis: about 46% of Lok Sabha members elected in 2024 declared criminal cases.
- Public Interest Foundation (2018) and Rambabu Singh Thakur (2020): parties must publish candidates' criminal records and reasons for selection — weak enforcement.
- Election expenditure limits (Section 77) ignore party spending; electoral bonds were struck down in ADR v. Union of India (February 2024).
- ECI cannot deregister parties for such conduct; no inner-party democracy rules.
- Corrupt practices (Section 123) need an election petition — slow.
- Reforms: disqualify at charge-framing for heinous offences (Law Commission 244th report, 2014), fast-track courts for MPs/MLAs, state funding, ECI power to deregister.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's drug-money and sand-mining networks in politics show the need for financial transparency in elections.

*Conclusion:* RPA is necessary but not sufficient; political will and speedy justice are the real cure.

*Diagram:* Funnel: entry (nomination) → campaign money → conviction → disqualification — mark the leak points.

**Q6.** The strength of any modern democracy is based on the deliberative functions of the Parliament. How do you evaluate the role of Indian Parliament as a deliberative body in the light of its working in recent years? *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Decline of parliamentary deliberation is a recurring governance theme.

*Intro:* Parliament's job is not just to pass laws but to debate, scrutinise and hold the executive accountable.

*Points:*
- Sittings fell: the 17th Lok Sabha (2019–24) sat 274 days, the lowest for a full term (PRS).
- Bills sent to committees fell sharply — only about 16% in the 17th Lok Sabha vs much higher shares in the 14th and 15th (PRS).
- Frequent disruptions; many bills passed with little debate, some by voice vote (e.g., farm bills in Rajya Sabha, September 2020).
- Use of Money Bill route for non-money matters (Aadhaar Act 2016) — contested in courts.
- Deputy Speaker post stayed vacant through the 17th Lok Sabha.
- Positives: Question Hour, department-related standing committees, record productivity in some sessions; new building (2023).
- Reforms: minimum sitting days, mandatory committee scrutiny, opposition day, pre-legislative consultation policy (2014) to be followed.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab Vidhan Sabha also meets for short sessions; special sessions on SYL and BBMB show debate is often symbolic.

*Conclusion:* Parliament must recover its deliberative role through more sittings and committee scrutiny.

*Diagram:* Bar chart: sittings and % bills to committees, 14th–17th Lok Sabha.

**Q7.** Independence of the Election Commission is a pre-condition for holding free and fair elections. Critically analyse the constitutional safeguards for the impartial functioning of Election Commission. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* ECI appointments and credibility (2023 Act, roll revisions) are hot topics.

*Intro:* Article 324 vests superintendence of elections in the Election Commission of India (ECI).

*Points:*
- CEC can be removed only like a Supreme Court judge; other ECs only on CEC's recommendation.
- Service conditions cannot be varied to the disadvantage after appointment.
- Gaps: Constitution does not fix qualifications, tenure or appointment method; no bar on post-retirement jobs.
- Anoop Baranwal v. Union of India (March 2023): selection by PM, Leader of Opposition and CJI until Parliament makes a law.
- Chief Election Commissioner and Other Election Commissioners Act, 2023 replaced the CJI with a Union Cabinet Minister — criticised as executive-dominated.
- ECI depends on the government for staff and budget (not charged on the Consolidated Fund).
- Strengths: Model Code of Conduct, EVM-VVPAT, T.N. Seshan-era reforms show what an assertive ECI can do.
- Reforms: equal removal protection for all ECs, collegium-type selection, independent secretariat, charged expenditure (Goswami Committee, 2nd ARC, Law Commission 255th report).

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's Chief Electoral Officer runs elections under ECI; smooth 2022 Assembly and 2024 Lok Sabha polls show administrative capacity.

*Conclusion:* Constitutional safeguards are partial; a neutral appointment system is essential for public trust.

**Q8.** The Companies Act, 2013 has made Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) mandatory for business corporates in India. However, the impact of CSR contributions towards social welfare and environmental protection still remains inadequate. Comment. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* CSR is asked in governance and ethics; moderate recurrence.

*Intro:* Section 135 of the Companies Act 2013 made India the first country to mandate CSR spending.

*Points:*
- Applies to companies with net worth ₹500 crore, or turnover ₹1,000 crore, or net profit ₹5 crore; they must spend 2% of average net profit of the last three years.
- Schedule VII lists eligible areas: health, education, environment, sanitation, rural development, etc.
- 2019 amendments: unspent money must go to an Unspent CSR Account or a Schedule VII fund; penalties later decriminalised.
- Achievements: tens of thousands of crores spent annually; support to PM CARES and Covid relief.
- Problems: geographic concentration in industrial states (Maharashtra, Karnataka, Gujarat); neglect of backward districts and the North-East.
- Sectoral skew to education and health; little for environment and rural livelihoods; projects near company plants.
- Weak impact assessment; CSR used for brand building; NGO capacity issues.
- Way forward: aspirational district focus, outcome-based reporting, impact audits, pooling through CSR exchanges.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab gets a small share of CSR; companies could fund stubble management machinery, drug de-addiction and groundwater recharge.

*Conclusion:* CSR has mobilised funds, but strategic, need-based targeting is needed for real impact.

**Q9.** India's democratic governance confronts the challenge of manipulation by private interests. Critically Analyse. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Crony capitalism and lobbying are recurrent ethics/governance themes.

*Intro:* Democratic governance can be captured when policy serves narrow private interests over the public good.

*Points:*
- Opaque political funding: electoral bonds were struck down in February 2024 for violating voters' right to information.
- Regulatory capture and 'revolving door' between regulators and industry.
- Crony capitalism in allocation of natural resources (2G and coal block cases, struck down/cancelled 2012 and 2014).
- Lobbying is unregulated in India, unlike the US/EU registers.
- Media concentration and paid news; data and digital platforms shaping opinion.
- Counter-view: private sector participation is essential for growth; PPPs and consultations bring expertise.
- Safeguards: RTI, CAG, CVC, Lokpal, competition law (CCI), auction-based allocation since 2012.
- Reforms: lobbying law, state funding of elections, cooling-off periods, beneficial ownership disclosure.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab examples: debates over sand-mining and private transport monopolies, and power purchase agreements with private thermal plants (renegotiated/acquired, e.g., PSPCL's purchase of the Goindwal Sahib plant in 2024).

*Conclusion:* Transparency and strong institutions can let private enterprise thrive without capturing the state.

**Q10.** In 2019, the Supreme Court ruled that the office of the Chief Justice of India is a public authority under the Right to Information (RTI) Act. Discuss the significance of this judgement by explaining the implications of bringing office of CJI under the RTI. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* RTI and judicial transparency recur; collegium transparency debate continues.

*Intro:* In CPIO, Supreme Court v. Subhash Chandra Agarwal (13 November 2019), a five-judge bench held that the office of the CJI is a public authority under the RTI Act, 2005.

*Points:*
- Upheld the Delhi High Court's 2010 view; transparency strengthens judicial independence rather than weakening it.
- Judges' asset declarations can be disclosed, subject to privacy balancing.
- Collegium decisions: names may be disclosed but reasons can be withheld to protect privacy and confidentiality.
- Information is to be tested under Section 8(1)(j) (personal information) and Section 11 (third-party) with a proportionality test.
- Significance: judiciary is not above accountability; boosts public trust.
- Concerns: risk of fishing inquiries; chilling effect on candid collegium discussion; slow implementation.
- Later amendment: DPDP Act 2023 amended Section 8(1)(j), widening the personal-information exemption — critics fear dilution of RTI.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab and Haryana High Court and Punjab's State Information Commission apply similar standards to court administration records.

*Conclusion:* The ruling affirmed that transparency and independence go together, but its promise depends on how exemptions are applied.

*Punjab:*

**Q16.** What is the mandate of WHO and how binding are its decisions? Examine the role played by WHO in context of COVID-19 Pandemic. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* International institutions are asked regularly; WHO's role changed with the 2025 Pandemic Agreement and US withdrawal.

*Intro:* The World Health Organization (founded 7 April 1948) is the UN agency that directs and coordinates international health.

*Points:*
- Mandate: 'attainment by all peoples of the highest possible level of health' — setting norms, technical help, surveillance, emergency response.
- Binding power: under Articles 21–22 of its Constitution, regulations adopted by the World Health Assembly bind members unless they opt out — e.g., International Health Regulations (2005).
- But WHO has no enforcement power; it relies on member cooperation; most guidance is advisory.
- Covid-19 role: declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (30 January 2020) and called it a pandemic (11 March 2020); issued technical guidance; co-led COVAX and the ACT Accelerator.
- Criticism: delayed warnings, dependence on Chinese information, weak access for investigations, funding dominated by voluntary earmarked contributions.
- Reforms after Covid: IHR amendments (2024) and the WHO Pandemic Agreement adopted by the World Health Assembly in May 2025.
- The US announced withdrawal from WHO in January 2025, cutting its largest funding source.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab used WHO/ICMR protocols for testing and vaccination; Punjab's health system gained from Covid-era oxygen plants and labs.

*Conclusion:* WHO is indispensable but under-powered; sustainable funding and stronger compliance tools are needed.
