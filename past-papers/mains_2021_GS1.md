**Q1(a).** "Gupta sculpture is the logical outcome of the early classical sculpture of Amravati and Mathura." Discuss with examples. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Ancient art appears in most PPSC GS-I papers; Gupta 'classical age' is a favourite UPSC/PPSC theme.

*Intro:* Gupta sculpture (c. 4th–6th century CE) fused the spiritual idealism of Mathura with the grace and narrative skill of Amaravati, producing India's 'classical' style.

*Points:*
- Mathura school (Kushana period, 1st–3rd c. CE): spotted red sandstone, robust indigenous figures, Buddha with shaven head, smiling face, frontal pose; also Jain and Brahmanical images.
- Amaravati school (Satavahana, Andhra, 2nd c. BCE–3rd c. CE): white limestone/marble, slender figures in dynamic poses, crowded narrative relief panels of Jataka stories.
- Gupta artists kept Mathura's iconography (halo, mudras, sanghati robe) but refined it: transparent 'wet' drapery, inward calm and spiritual expression.
- From Amaravati they took fluid body lines, tribhanga postures and story-telling panels.
- Examples: seated Buddha in Dharmachakra mudra from Sarnath (5th c.); standing Buddha from Mathura (5th c.) with clinging drapery.
- Brahmanical works: Varaha panel at Udayagiri caves (Vidisha, c. 401 CE); Vishnu Anantashayana and Nara-Narayana panels at Deogarh Dashavatara temple.
- Canons of proportion (tala system) and iconography were standardised, which later medieval schools followed.
- So Gupta art was evolution, not a break: it synthesised earlier schools into balanced, idealised forms.

*Punjab:*
- Gandhara-Mathura links ran through the Punjab region (Taxila was part of greater Punjab), showing how north-western traditions fed Gupta art.
- Sanghol (Fatehgarh Sahib) Kushana-period sculptures in the Sanghol Museum show the Mathura idiom in Punjab.

*Conclusion:* Gupta sculpture is the mature fruit of Mathura's strength and Amaravati's grace, and it set the classical standard for Indian art.

*Diagram:* Three-column table: Mathura | Amaravati | Gupta — material, features, example.

**Q1(b).** Critically examine the cultural contribution of Pallavas. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Dynasty-specific; PPSC asks South Indian culture only occasionally.

*Intro:* The Pallavas of Kanchipuram (c. 6th–9th century CE) laid the foundation of Dravidian temple architecture and patronised Sanskrit and Tamil learning.

*Points:*
- Architecture moved from rock-cut to structural: Mahendravarman I's cave temples (e.g., Mandagapattu) → Narasimhavarman I 'Mamalla's' monolithic rathas at Mahabalipuram → Rajasimha's structural Shore Temple and Kailasanatha temple, Kanchi.
- Mahabalipuram group of monuments is a UNESCO World Heritage Site (1984); 'Arjuna's Penance'/'Descent of the Ganga' is among the world's largest open-air reliefs.
- Sculpture: elegant, slender figures; Somaskanda panels.
- Literature: Mahendravarman I wrote the Sanskrit farce Mattavilasa Prahasana; Bharavi and Dandin are associated with Pallava courts.
- Bhakti: Nayanar and Alvar saints flourished, taking Tamil devotional hymns to the people.
- Education: Kanchi was a centre of learning (ghatika); the Grantha script developed and spread to South-East Asia.
- Overseas influence: Pallava art and script influenced Cambodia, Java and Champa.
- Critique: patronage was largely Brahmanical and elite; long wars with Chalukyas drained resources; some Jain/Buddhist decline followed.

*Punjab:*
- Comparison: like the Pallavas, Punjab's rulers (e.g., Maharaja Ranjit Singh's patronage of the Golden Temple's gilding) used temple/gurdwara building for legitimacy.

*Conclusion:* Pallava contribution was foundational: Cholas built their great temples on Pallava experiments.

**Q2(a).** Analyse the role of Pre-Congress Political Associations in rise of political consciousness in India. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Pre-1885 nationalism is a standard modern-history topic; asked by UPSC and PPSC.

*Intro:* Before 1885 educated Indians formed regional associations that trained a generation in petitioning, public meetings and press campaigns.

*Points:*
- Bengal: Landholders' Society (1838) and British Indian Association (1851) — early landlord interests but first organised political action.
- Bombay Association and Madras Native Association (1852) petitioned the British Parliament before the 1853 Charter renewal.
- East India Association (1866, London) by Dadabhai Naoroji took Indian grievances to British public opinion.
- Poona Sarvajanik Sabha (1870): studied famine and land revenue; Indian Association (1876, S.N. Banerjea and Ananda Mohan Bose) — middle-class, all-India outlook.
- Campaigns: ICS age limit and simultaneous exams (1877–78), against Vernacular Press Act (1878) and Arms Act, support for Ilbert Bill (1883).
- Indian National Conference (1883, 1885) and Madras Mahajan Sabha (1884), Bombay Presidency Association (1885) were direct forerunners of the Congress.
- Limits: urban, elite, region-specific and moderate; masses largely outside.
- Impact: created a common political language, all-India leadership and the idea of constitutional agitation.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab: Lahore's Indian Association branch and the Arya Samaj (Lahore, 1877) and Singh Sabha (1873) spread public debate and the vernacular press, e.g., The Tribune (Lahore, 1881).

*Conclusion:* These associations were the nursery of Indian nationalism; the Congress of 1885 grew out of their networks.

*Diagram:* Timeline 1838 → 1851 → 1866 → 1870 → 1876 → 1883 → 1885 (INC).

**Q2(b).** The Gurudwara Reform Movement of Punjab made an important contribution towards India's freedom struggle. Discuss. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Akali movement is asked very often in PPSC GS-I; Punjab-specific freedom struggle is a syllabus focus.

*Intro:* The Gurdwara Reform (Akali) Movement of 1920–25 sought to free Sikh shrines from corrupt mahants backed by the British and became a mass, non-violent anti-colonial campaign.

*Points:*
- Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee (SGPC) formed in November 1920; Shiromani Akali Dal in December 1920.
- Nankana Sahib massacre (February 1921): over a hundred reformers killed by the mahant's men, which turned Sikh opinion against the government.
- Keys affair (1921–22): government returned the keys of the Golden Temple toshakhana; Gandhi hailed it as the first decisive battle of India's freedom won.
- Guru-ka-Bagh morcha (1922): disciplined non-violent jathas endured police beatings, impressing national leaders and C.F. Andrews.
- Jaito morcha (1923–25) over the deposition of the Maharaja of Nabha linked religious reform to princely-state politics.
- Outcome: Sikh Gurdwaras Act, 1925 placed shrines under elected SGPC management.
- Contribution: proved the power of mass non-violence alongside Non-Cooperation; drew peasants and ex-soldiers into politics; weakened the loyalist image the British cultivated among Sikhs.
- Limitations: largely community-specific; later strains between Akali and Congress politics.

*Punjab:*
- Sites to quote: Nankana Sahib (now Pakistan), Guru-ka-Bagh (Amritsar), Jaito (Faridkot).

*Conclusion:* The Akali movement fused religious reform with anti-imperialism and gave Punjab a mass base in the national movement.

*Diagram:* Timeline: Nov 1920 SGPC → Feb 1921 Nankana → Jan 1922 keys → Aug 1922 Guru-ka-Bagh → 1923–25 Jaito → 1925 Act.

**Q3.** What were the circumstances that led Mahatma Gandhi to start the Non-cooperation Movement? Examine its contribution to India's struggle for freedom. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Gandhian mass movements are asked almost every year in PPSC/UPSC; strong Punjab link (Jallianwala).

*Intro:* The Non-Cooperation Movement (1920–22) was Gandhi's first all-India mass movement, launched after a series of shocks in 1919–20.

*Points:*
- Rowlatt Act (March 1919): detention without trial; Gandhi's Rowlatt Satyagraha.
- Jallianwala Bagh massacre, Amritsar (13 April 1919) and martial law in Punjab; the Hunter Committee report and House of Lords' support for Dyer angered Indians.
- Khilafat question: harsh treatment of the Ottoman Sultan after WWI gave a chance for Hindu-Muslim unity.
- Montagu-Chelmsford reforms (1919) were seen as inadequate (dyarchy); post-war price rise and economic hardship.
- Programme approved at the special Calcutta session (Sept 1920, presided by Lala Lajpat Rai) and Nagpur session (Dec 1920): boycott of titles, councils, courts, schools, foreign cloth; swadeshi, khadi, national schools.
- Contribution: turned Congress into a mass party (reorganised Provincial Congress Committees on linguistic lines, low membership fee).
- National institutions: Jamia Millia Islamia, Kashi Vidyapith, Gujarat Vidyapith; Tilak Swaraj Fund.
- Fear of British rule broke; peasants, workers, women and students joined; Hindu–Muslim unity at its peak.
- Withdrawn after Chauri Chaura (February 1922); criticism of sudden withdrawal, but it set the template for Civil Disobedience (1930) and Quit India (1942).

*Punjab:*
- Jallianwala Bagh was the trigger — Punjab became the moral centre of the movement.
- Akali movement ran in parallel and Gandhi praised its non-violent jathas.

*Conclusion:* NCM transformed nationalism from elite petitioning into a disciplined mass struggle, even though Swaraj within a year was not achieved.

*Diagram:* Flowchart: Rowlatt + Jallianwala + Khilafat + inadequate reforms → NCM (1920) → mass mobilisation → Chauri Chaura (1922) → lessons for 1930.

**Q4.** Describe the Scramble of Africa by European Colonial Masters. What were its consequences? *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* World history from the 18th century — colonisation/decolonisation questions recur in PPSC GS-I.

*Intro:* The 'Scramble for Africa' (c. 1880–1914) was the rapid partition of Africa among European powers; by 1914 only Ethiopia and Liberia remained independent.

*Points:*
- Causes: industrial demand for raw materials and markets; surplus capital; strategic rivalry (Suez Canal 1869); nationalism and prestige; missionary and 'civilising mission' ideas.
- Enablers: quinine, steamships, railways, telegraph and the Maxim gun.
- Berlin Conference (1884–85, Bismarck): rules of 'effective occupation'; Leopold II's Congo Free State recognised.
- Main players: Britain (Cape to Cairo), France (West & North Africa), Germany, Belgium, Portugal, Italy, Spain.
- Flashpoints: Fashoda incident (1898), Boer War (1899–1902), Moroccan crises (1905, 1911); Ethiopia defeated Italy at Adwa (1896).
- Consequences for Africa: artificial borders splitting ethnic groups (root of later conflicts), extraction economies based on mines and cash crops, forced labour (Congo atrocities), loss of sovereignty.
- Some modern infrastructure, Western education and Christian missions, but designed for extraction.
- Consequences for Europe: rivalry fed alliance tensions before World War I.
- Long-term: nationalist movements after WWII and decolonisation (Ghana 1957 onwards); legacy of dependency and border disputes.

*Punjab:*
- Indian link: Indian indentured labour and traders went to East and South Africa; Gandhi's satyagraha began in South Africa — many Punjabis served in British-Indian forces in East Africa in WWI.

*Conclusion:* The scramble remade Africa's map in a generation, and its economic and political scars persist.

*Diagram:* Sketch map of Africa shaded by colonial power in 1914.

**Q5.** Discuss Maharaja Ranjit Singh's role in consolidation of smaller territories (misls) into a powerful state of Punjab. What sort of reforms did he introduce in the field of civil and military administration? *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Ranjit Singh is a named syllabus topic and asked in almost every PPSC GS-I paper.

*Intro:* Ranjit Singh (1780–1839), leader of the Sukerchakia misl, united the quarrelling misls into the Sarkar-i-Khalsa, the strongest state in north India.

*Points:*
- Captured Lahore (1799); proclaimed Maharaja (1801); took Amritsar (1802).
- Methods: marriage alliances (Kanhaiya misl via Sada Kaur; Nakai misl), diplomacy, subordinate alliances and conquest of rival misls.
- Treaty of Amritsar (1809) with the British fixed the Sutlej as boundary, freeing him to expand north and west: Multan (1818), Kashmir (1819), Peshawar (1834).
- Civil administration: Maharaja as head with ministers (e.g., Dhian Singh as wazir); provinces (subas) under nazims, parganas under kardars, villages with panchayats.
- Land revenue: batai and kankut systems; jagirs; revenue was the main income; Nanakshahi coins issued in the name of the Gurus.
- Justice: largely customary, with the Adalat-i-Ala at Lahore; fines common; no death penalty in ordinary practice.
- Military: Fauj-i-Ain (regular army) trained on European lines by Ventura, Allard, Court and Avitabile; Fauj-i-Khas (1822) as elite brigade.
- Strong artillery (topkhana) and a regularly paid army; secular recruitment of Sikhs, Hindus, Muslims and Europeans.
- Limits: personal rule, weak succession system, rising power of Dogra chiefs and army panchayats led to collapse after 1839.

*Punjab:*
- Legacy places: Gobindgarh Fort (Amritsar), Ram Bagh summer palace (Amritsar), gilding of Harmandar Sahib.

*Conclusion:* Ranjit Singh turned a confederacy into a centralised, secular kingdom; its institutions were strong but over-dependent on him.

*Diagram:* Map: Sutlej–Indus region with dates of conquest (Lahore 1799, Multan 1818, Kashmir 1819, Peshawar 1834).

**Q6.** "High mountain areas are prone to land slides." Suggest a suitable strategy to mitigate such natural disaster phenomenon. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Himalayan landslides make news every monsoon (Joshimath 2023, Wayanad 2024); disaster topics recur.

*Intro:* Steep slopes, young fragile rocks, heavy rain, earthquakes and unplanned construction make the Himalaya and Western Ghats highly landslide-prone.

*Points:*
- Causes: natural (steep relief, weathered rock, cloudbursts, seismicity, glacial retreat) and human (road cutting, deforestation, quarrying, hydro projects, overloaded slopes).
- Mapping: Landslide Atlas of India (ISRO-NRSC, 2023) and GSI's national landslide susceptibility mapping — use them for zoning.
- Land-use regulation: ban building on high-hazard slopes; enforce building codes and drainage rules in hill towns.
- Engineering: retaining walls, rock bolting, wire mesh, gabions, surface and sub-surface drainage, slope grading.
- Bio-engineering: afforestation, vetiver and native grasses, contour trenching.
- Early warning: rain-gauge thresholds, slope sensors and community alerts (GSI's early-warning pilots).
- Institutions: NDMA Landslide Guidelines (2009) and National Landslide Risk Management Strategy (2019); SDMAs and district plans.
- Community preparedness: evacuation drills, safe shelters, insurance, restraint in tourism carrying capacity.
- Lessons: Joshimath land subsidence (January 2023) and Wayanad landslides (July 2024) show the cost of ignoring carrying capacity.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's Shivalik/Kandi belt (Pathankot's Dhar Kalan, Hoshiarpur) faces slope failure and soil erosion; roads to Ranjit Sagar Dam need slope protection.
- 2023 and 2025 monsoon floods in Punjab followed heavy rain and landslides upstream in Himachal.

*Conclusion:* Landslide risk can be cut sharply by combining hazard zoning, engineering and nature-based solutions with community preparedness.

*Diagram:* Cycle diagram: hazard mapping → regulation → engineering & bio-engineering → early warning → response → review.

<a id="q7"></a>

**Q7.** Present a comparative account of Temperate & Tropical Cyclones on global scale. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Cyclone basics are standard geography; recurrence moderate.

*Intro:* Both are low-pressure systems with converging winds, but they differ in origin, structure and behaviour.

*Points:*
- Location: tropical — 5°–30° N/S over warm oceans; temperate (extra-tropical) — 35°–65° N/S over land and sea.
- Origin: tropical needs sea-surface temperature above about 26.5°C, Coriolis force and low wind shear; temperate forms along fronts where warm and cold air masses meet.
- Structure: tropical has a calm eye, warm core, no fronts; temperate has warm and cold fronts and a cold core.
- Size and intensity: tropical smaller but far more violent (winds can exceed 250 km/h); temperate covers a larger area with milder winds.
- Movement: tropical moves east to west with trade winds and recurves; temperate moves west to east with the westerlies.
- Life: tropical weakens over land; temperate persists over land and sea.
- Names: hurricanes, typhoons, cyclones, willy-willies (tropical); western disturbances, mid-latitude depressions (temperate).

*Punjab:*
- Western disturbances (temperate origin) bring winter rain to Punjab, vital for rabi wheat; they can also cause hailstorms at harvest.

*Conclusion:* Tropical cyclones are destructive coastal hazards; temperate cyclones shape day-to-day weather of mid-latitudes, including Punjab's winter rain.

*Diagram:* Two-column comparison table plus sketch of a frontal cyclone and a cyclone eye.

**Q8.** Covid-19 pandemic has led to a shift in the recent trends of international migration. Analyse. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Migration and diaspora remain live in Punjab (study visas, remittances, deportations) though the Covid frame is dated.

*Intro:* Covid-19 (2020–21) disrupted cross-border mobility and changed who migrates, how and where.

*Points:*
- Immediate shock: border closures and travel bans; mass return of migrants, e.g., India's Vande Bharat Mission (from May 2020) and Gulf returnees.
- Job losses in host economies (construction, hospitality) hit low-skilled migrants first; wage theft and stranded workers.
- Remittances proved resilient: India remained the top recipient; by 2022 its inflows crossed USD 100 billion (World Bank).
- Shift to skilled and health-worker migration: rich countries fast-tracked nurses, doctors and IT professionals.
- Rise of remote work reduced some need to move physically; digital nomad visas emerged.
- Student mobility rebounded strongly after 2022, then host countries tightened rules (e.g., Canada's cap on study permits, 2024).
- Health checks, vaccine passports and digital IDs became part of migration governance.
- Policy: Global Compact for Safe, Orderly and Regular Migration (2018) gained relevance; India signed migration and mobility partnerships with several countries.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab is a major source of student and family migration to Canada, UK, Australia; IELTS centres and remittances shape the Doaba economy.
- Canada's 2024 study-permit cap and US deportation flights (2025) hit Punjabi aspirants; illegal 'donkey route' risks highlighted.

*Conclusion:* The pandemic did not stop migration but made it more selective, skill-based and regulated.

*Diagram:* Arrow chart: shock (2020) → return migration → recovery (2022) → selective/skilled migration and tighter controls (2024–25).

**Q9(a).** Define the term 'Chos'. Discuss their associated problems with special reference to Punjab. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Punjab physiography (Kandi, chos, Shivaliks) is asked often by PPSC.

*Intro:* Chos are seasonal torrents that descend from the Shivalik hills; dry for most of the year, they flood suddenly in the monsoon.

*Points:*
- Found mainly in the Kandi belt of Hoshiarpur, Nawanshahr (SBS Nagar), Ropar (Rupnagar), Mohali and Pathankot.
- Problems: soil erosion and gully formation in the Shivaliks due to deforestation and grazing.
- Sand and gravel deposits spread over fertile fields, reducing productivity.
- Flash floods damage crops, roads and villages; channels shift course.
- Low groundwater recharge in the Kandi area; drinking water scarcity in summer.
- Illegal mining of sand from cho beds worsens erosion.
- Remedies: Punjab Land Preservation (Chos) Act, 1900 regulates land use; check dams and small dams (e.g., Kandi watershed projects), afforestation, vegetative barriers, channelisation, Kandi Canal for irrigation.

*Punjab:*
- Hoshiarpur district historically had the most chos; Kandi Area Development schemes target them.

*Conclusion:* Integrated watershed management in the Shivaliks is the only lasting answer to the cho problem.

*Diagram:* Cross-section: Shivalik hills → Kandi belt (cho) → Sirowal → alluvial plain.

**Q9(b).** Explain the salient features of physiography of Upper Bari Doab region. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Punjab doabs are a classic PPSC topic; Bari Doab is the Majha heartland.

*Intro:* Bari Doab lies between the Beas and the Ravi; its upper part covers Pathankot, Gurdaspur, Amritsar and parts of Tarn Taran (Majha).

*Points:*
- North: Shivalik foothills and Kandi tract in Pathankot (Dhar Kalan) with steep slopes and seasonal streams.
- Centre and south: a flat, fertile alluvial plain of Himalayan sediments sloping gently to the south-west.
- Rivers: Ravi on the west (international border in parts) and Beas on the east; floodplains (bet) along both.
- Wetlands (chhambs): Keshopur-Miani (Gurdaspur) Community Reserve, a Ramsar site, and Kahnuwan chhamb.
- Irrigation: Upper Bari Doab Canal from Madhopur headworks on the Ravi (opened in the 1850s–60s), one of India's oldest canal systems; Ranjit Sagar (Thein) Dam upstream.
- Soils: loamy alluvium; waterlogging near chhambs, sandy soils near rivers.
- Strategic: border with Pakistan along much of its western edge.

*Punjab:*
- Majha's agriculture (wheat–paddy, basmati in Amritsar–Gurdaspur) depends on this physiography.

*Conclusion:* Upper Bari Doab combines hills, plains and wetlands, making it agriculturally rich but flood- and border-sensitive.

*Diagram:* Sketch map: Ravi–Beas with Pathankot, Gurdaspur, Amritsar, Madhopur headworks, Keshopur chhamb.

**Q10(a).** Write brief note on: Folding & Faulting. *(5 marks, 50 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Basic geomorphology; asked as short notes.

*Intro:* Folding and faulting are results of endogenic (internal) forces acting on rocks.

*Points:*
- Folding: compressional forces bend rock layers into anticlines (up-folds) and synclines (down-folds); forms fold mountains like the Himalaya, Alps.
- Faulting: tension or compression breaks rocks and displaces them along a fault plane; forms rift valleys (graben) and block mountains (horst) such as the Narmada rift valley and Vosges/Black Forest.

*Punjab:*


*Conclusion:* Both shape major relief features and are linked to plate movements.

**Q10(d).** Write brief note on: Corporate Farming. *(5 marks, 50 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Farm-sector reform and corporate role remain hot in Punjab after the 2020–21 farm-law protests.

*Intro:* Corporate farming is large-scale agriculture owned or managed by companies, using capital, technology and hired labour.

*Points:*
- Pros: investment, technology, assured markets, value chains; cons: small-farmer displacement, land concentration, monoculture risk.
- India mostly allows contract farming rather than corporate land ownership; the 2020 farm laws that eased corporate trade were repealed in 2021.

*Punjab:*

**Q11.** Analyse the changing trends of Caste System prevalent in Indian Society. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Caste is a perennial Indian-society topic; Punjab has India's highest SC share.

*Intro:* Caste remains a key social institution, but its rituals have weakened while its political and economic roles have grown.

*Points:*
- Weakening of purity-pollution and occupational rigidity due to urbanisation, education, industrial jobs and migration.
- Constitutional change: Article 15 (non-discrimination), Article 17 (abolition of untouchability), Protection of Civil Rights Act 1955, SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act 1989.
- Reservation and affirmative action created an SC/ST/OBC middle class; Indra Sawhney (1992) set the 50% ceiling; EWS quota by the 103rd Amendment (2019), upheld in 2022.
- Politicisation: caste as vote bank and identity politics ('dominant caste' idea of M.N. Srinivas); caste census demands.
- Sanskritisation and westernisation as mobility routes.
- Persistence: endogamy (most marriages within caste), honour killings, manual scavenging, atrocities.
- New forms: caste associations online, sub-categorisation of SCs allowed by the Supreme Court (State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh, August 2024).
- Rural–urban divide: anonymity in cities but caste-based housing and workplace discrimination continue.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab has the highest proportion of Scheduled Castes in India — 31.9% (Census 2011).
- Dera movements and Ad-Dharm reflect Dalit assertion in Doaba; the Davinder Singh case arose from Punjab's sub-quota for Balmikis and Mazhabi Sikhs.

*Conclusion:* Caste is changing from a ritual hierarchy into a tool of identity and political bargaining; inclusive growth and education must finish the work of equality.

**Q15.** Critically evaluate the role of New Education Policy 2020 in context of inclusive education. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* NEP 2020 implementation remains a live debate; education questions recur in PPSC.

*Intro:* NEP 2020 replaced the 1986 policy and makes equity and inclusion a core principle.

*Points:*
- Defines Socio-Economically Disadvantaged Groups (SEDGs) — girls, SC/ST/OBC, minorities, children with disabilities, rural and urban poor.
- Gender Inclusion Fund and Special Education Zones for disadvantaged regions.
- Early childhood care (5+3+3+4 structure) and foundational literacy-numeracy mission (NIPUN Bharat, 2021).

- Targets: 100% school GER by 2030; 50% higher-education GER by 2035; public spending of 6% of GDP.
- Concerns: digital divide (seen in Covid-era online classes), low public spending, teacher shortages, privatisation pressure, language debate and Centre–State friction (e.g., PM-SHRI disputes).
- Way forward: adequate funding, community participation, scholarships and hostels, accessible infrastructure.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's 'Schools of Eminence' (from 2023) and PSEB reforms aim at quality; challenges include border-district school access and girls' transition to higher classes.

*Conclusion:* NEP 2020 offers a strong inclusive vision; its success depends on money, teachers and Centre–State cooperation.
