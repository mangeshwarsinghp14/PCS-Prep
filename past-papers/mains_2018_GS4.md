**Q1.** "Delhi blanketed in toxic haze has become a gas chamber." List the major causes and possible mitigation measures. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Air pollution and stubble burning are asked every year and directly concern Punjab.

*Intro:* Every October–January, Delhi-NCR faces 'severe' air quality from a mix of local emissions and regional sources trapped by winter weather.

*Points:*
- Local sources: vehicles, road and construction dust, industries, power plants, open waste burning, diesel generators.
- Regional: paddy stubble burning in Punjab and Haryana in October–November; its share on peak days can be large.
- Meteorology: low wind speed, temperature inversion and low mixing height trap pollutants; Diwali firecrackers add spikes.
- Health: respiratory and heart disease, lower life expectancy.
- Measures in place: Graded Response Action Plan (GRAP), Commission for Air Quality Management Act 2021, National Clean Air Programme (2019), BS-VI fuel nationwide from April 2020.
- Stubble: subsidised crop-residue machines (Happy Seeder, Super Seeder), bio-decomposer, ex-situ use in biomass power, CBG and boilers.
- Further steps: EVs and public transport, dust control, waste processing, cleaner industrial fuel, regional airshed management, incentives for paddy alternatives.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab farm fires fell from about 36,600 (2023) to about 10,900 (2024) as per satellite counts, through machinery, enforcement and awareness.

*Conclusion:* Clean air needs year-round, regional action — not emergency measures only in winter.

*Diagram:* Pie: sources of PM2.5 — transport, dust, industry, biomass burning, others (qualitative).

**Q2.** What is active and passive immunization and also discuss the role of second and third generation vaccines in controlling hepatitis? *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Immunity and vaccination are a named syllabus item.

*Intro:* Immunisation gives protection against disease either by stimulating the body's own immunity or by supplying ready-made antibodies.

*Points:*
- Active immunisation: vaccine (antigen) stimulates the body to make antibodies and memory cells; slow onset, long-lasting (e.g., measles, hepatitis B vaccines).
- Passive immunisation: ready-made antibodies given (immunoglobulin, antisera, maternal antibodies); quick but short-lived (e.g., hepatitis B immunoglobulin for newborns of infected mothers, anti-rabies serum).
- First-generation hepatitis B vaccine (early 1980s) was made from plasma of infected people.
- Second-generation vaccines (from mid-1980s) are recombinant: the hepatitis B surface antigen produced in yeast — safe, cheap, used worldwide and in India's Universal Immunisation Programme (including birth dose).
- Third-generation hepatitis B vaccines add pre-S1/pre-S2 antigens, made in mammalian cells — stronger response in poor responders (elderly, diabetics, dialysis patients).
- Hepatitis A and E vaccines exist; no vaccine yet for hepatitis C (curable by antiviral drugs).
- India's National Viral Hepatitis Control Programme (2018) targets elimination of hepatitis C and control of B (SDG 3.3 by 2030).

*Punjab:*
- Punjab has a high burden of hepatitis C and runs free testing and treatment under the Mukh Mantri Punjab Hepatitis C Relief Fund (2016).

*Conclusion:* Vaccines, especially recombinant hepatitis B vaccine, make hepatitis control and elimination possible.

**Q3.** What were the major causes of Kerala floods and what could be the possible mitigation measures? *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Floods are the most common disaster question; Punjab's own 2023 and 2025 floods make it very relevant.

*Intro:* In August 2018 Kerala suffered its worst floods in nearly a century, with about 480 deaths and huge damage.

*Points:*
- Extreme rainfall: very heavy, continuous monsoon rain over a short period across the state.
- Reservoir management: many dams were near full and had to release water simultaneously (e.g., Idukki, Idamalayar), adding to floods.
- Ecological degradation: quarrying, deforestation and construction on fragile Western Ghats slopes caused landslides.
- Encroachment on floodplains, paddy fields and wetlands reduced natural water storage.
- Mitigation: integrated reservoir operation with rule curves and early releases; real-time forecasting.
- Flood-zone mapping and regulation of construction; protection of wetlands and the Western Ghats.
- Early warning and community-based disaster preparedness; resilient housing (Rebuild Kerala Initiative).
- Use of NDMA flood guidelines and insurance.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab floods of July 2023 and August–September 2025 (severe statewide, with all districts affected) show similar issues — heavy upstream rain, dam releases from Bhakra/Pong/Ranjit Sagar, breached dhussi bundhs and floodplain encroachment.

*Conclusion:* Floods are natural, disasters are often man-made; ecosystem-based planning and smart reservoir management cut the damage.

*Diagram:* Flowchart: heavy rain + full dams + degraded slopes → floods/landslides → mitigation layers.

**Q4.** What are monoclonal antibodies and what role they can play in diagnosis and therapy of various diseases? *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Biotechnology applications recur.

*Intro:* Monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) are identical antibodies made by one clone of cells, all binding to the same specific target (antigen).

*Points:*
- Produced by hybridoma technique (Köhler and Milstein, 1975; Nobel 1984) — fusing antibody-producing B cells with myeloma cells; now also by recombinant methods.
- Diagnosis: pregnancy tests, ELISA and rapid diagnostic kits (malaria, dengue, COVID), blood typing, imaging of tumours.
- Cancer therapy: target cancer cells (e.g., trastuzumab for HER2-positive breast cancer, rituximab for lymphoma); antibody–drug conjugates; checkpoint inhibitors.
- Autoimmune diseases: rheumatoid arthritis, psoriasis (anti-TNF agents).
- Infectious diseases: RSV, rabies, Ebola; COVID-19 antibody cocktails were used during the pandemic.
- Transplant: preventing organ rejection.
- Limits: high cost, need for cold chain, side effects; biosimilars from Indian firms are lowering costs.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's cancer burden makes affordable biosimilar mAbs important (e.g., via Mukh Mantri Punjab Cancer Raahat Kosh).

*Conclusion:* mAbs are precision tools of modern medicine; cheaper biosimilars can widen access in India.

**Q6(b).** Write short note on: What are cybrids? How do they differ from hybrids? *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Plant biotechnology term; rarely repeated.

*Intro:* A cybrid (cytoplasmic hybrid) has the nucleus of one parent but cytoplasm from two (or from the other) parents.

*Points:*
- Made by protoplast fusion where the nucleus of one protoplast is inactivated or lost.
- Used in plants to transfer cytoplasmic traits like cytoplasmic male sterility (useful for hybrid seed) and disease resistance.
- Hybrid: combines the nuclear genes of both parents (by sexual crossing or somatic fusion).
- So cybrids differ in mixing only cytoplasmic (mitochondrial/chloroplast) genomes, not nuclear genomes.

*Punjab:*


*Conclusion:* Cybrids are a precise tool for transferring organelle-based traits.

**Q7.** What is IRS (Indian Remote Sensing) satellite system and what role can it play in conservation of biodiversity and in drought monitoring and assessment. How far remote sensing has been successful in doing so? *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Space applications for resource management recur.

*Intro:* The IRS system is India's constellation of earth-observation satellites, started with IRS-1A (1988); data is processed by ISRO's National Remote Sensing Centre (NRSC).

*Points:*
- Satellites: Resourcesat, Cartosat, Oceansat, RISAT (radar), EOS series; NASA-ISRO NISAR radar satellite launched in July 2025.
- Biodiversity: forest-cover mapping (Forest Survey of India's biennial reports use IRS data), habitat and corridor mapping, forest-fire alerts, wetland inventories, landscape-level biodiversity characterisation.
- Drought: vegetation indices (NDVI), soil moisture, rainfall and reservoir monitoring; agricultural drought assessment (NADAMS and its successors) used for drought declaration.
- Also crop acreage and yield estimation (FASAL), groundwater prospect maps.
- Success: national-scale, repeatable data; fire alerts and crop estimates are used routinely.
- Limits: cloud cover for optical sensors, resolution limits for small habitats, need for ground truthing, slow use by local administration.

*Punjab:*
- Satellite monitoring of stubble-burning fires in Punjab (using ISRO/NASA data) is a prime example; also crop diversification mapping.

*Conclusion:* Remote sensing has become indispensable; integration with ground data and local decision-making will raise its value.

**Q8.** What is the current status of tuberculosis in India? How drug resistance against this disease is impacting its control measures? *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* TB elimination is a flagship health goal; asked repeatedly.

*Intro:* India carries the largest share of the world's TB cases (about a quarter of the global burden, WHO).

*Points:*
- Incidence has fallen — WHO's Global TB Report 2024 estimated a decline of about 18% in India between 2015 and 2023; deaths fell faster.
- National TB Elimination Programme targets 'TB Mukt Bharat' ahead of the SDG 2030 target.
- Measures: free diagnosis (CBNAAT/TrueNat), Ni-kshay portal, Ni-kshay Poshan Yojana nutrition support (raised to ₹1,000/month in 2024), Pradhan Mantri TB Mukt Bharat Abhiyaan (Ni-kshay Mitra, 2022), 100-day intensified campaign (December 2024).
- Drug resistance: MDR-TB (resistant to isoniazid and rifampicin) and XDR-TB need long, toxic, costly treatment; lower cure rates.
- Causes: incomplete treatment, private-sector irregular prescriptions, poor adherence.
- Response: universal drug-susceptibility testing, shorter all-oral regimens (BPaLM approved in 2024), bedaquiline, private-sector engagement.
- Gaps: under-nutrition, stigma, missing cases, post-TB disability.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab: drug users and prisoners are high-risk groups; Ni-kshay Mitra support from NGOs and gurdwaras.

*Conclusion:* Fighting drug resistance through early testing and shorter regimens is key to ending TB.

**Q9.** "District administration in India has lost its credibility, trust and importance in the modern times and it requires massive structural reforms and alternative arrangements." Comment. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* District administration is a named syllabus item; PCS officers serve here.

*Intro:* The district Collector/Deputy Commissioner remains the pivot of field administration, but the role is under strain.

*Points:*
- Concerns: overload of functions, frequent transfers, political interference, rise of parallel agencies and societies, weak coordination.
- Panchayati Raj and municipal bodies after the 73rd/74th Amendments shifted some functions, but devolution is incomplete.
- Public perception: delays, corruption at lower levels, poor grievance redress.
- Counter-view: districts still deliver elections, disaster response, law and order, welfare; Covid-19 and floods showed their importance.
- Reforms: 2nd ARC (15th report) — strengthen local bodies, district councils; Aspirational Districts Programme (2018) — data-driven ranking; DISHA committees.
- E-governance, e-office, single-window service centres and social audits improve trust.
- Need: stable tenure, specialisation, delegation to SDMs and tehsildars, accountability to citizens.

*Punjab:*
- In Punjab, the DC leads flood relief, stubble-burning control and anti-drug drives; Sewa Kendras and SDM-level camps improved service delivery.

*Conclusion:* District administration needs reform, not replacement — modernised, decentralised and accountable.

- Foreign policy: India supported Bangladesh with aid under Operation Insaniyat (2017) and engages Myanmar for safe return.
- Way forward: a national refugee law, case-by-case screening, regional solutions with Bangladesh and Myanmar.

*Conclusion:* India's stance balances security and humanity; a clear legal framework would make it more consistent.
