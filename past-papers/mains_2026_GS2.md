**Q1.** "While statutory and regulatory authorities are justified on grounds of expertise and efficiency, they have weakened democratic accountability and transparency in governance." Discuss. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Regulatory bodies are a syllabus node; debates on independence of regulators and tribunals recur in UPSC/PPSC.

*Intro:* Statutory regulators (SEBI, TRAI, CERC, FSSAI, state electricity commissions) are created by law to take technical decisions away from day-to-day politics.

*Points:*
- Justification: domain expertise, speed, policy stability, credible commitment to investors, insulation from populism.
- Accountability deficit: unelected members make rules with wide social impact (tariffs, prices, market access).
- Weak parliamentary scrutiny of delegated legislation and regulations; few regulators report to dedicated committees.
- Transparency gaps: limited public consultation, opaque appointments dominated by the executive; post-retirement jobs create 'revolving door'.
- Overlaps and turf wars; tribunalisation (Tribunal Reforms Act 2021) raised concerns of executive control.
- Counter-view: regulators are subject to judicial review, RTI, CAG audit and appellate tribunals; many publish consultation papers.
- Reform: a common regulatory accountability law (as suggested by the Financial Sector Legislative Reforms Commission, 2013), parliamentary committee review, transparent selection, cooling-off rules.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab State Electricity Regulatory Commission sets power tariffs and subsidy accounting; Punjab RERA and Punjab Pollution Control Board face similar accountability questions.

*Conclusion:* Expertise and accountability are not opposites; regulators need independence from government but answerability to Parliament and the public.

**Q2.** Critically examine the limitations of SHGs as instruments of development. Do they reinforce existing social hierarchies? Explain in the context of SHGs in Punjab. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* SHGs asked in GS-I and GS-II of the same exam — signals PPSC interest; DAY-NRLM and Lakhpati Didi are live schemes.

*Intro:* SHGs (under DAY-NRLM and NABARD's SHG–Bank Linkage) aim at financial inclusion and women's empowerment, but their impact depends on local social structure.

*Points:*
- Limitations: small, consumption-oriented loans; weak market linkages and low-value micro-enterprises.
- Groups often become dormant after subsidies end; poor book-keeping and training.
- Debt risk from multiple borrowing (SHG plus MFIs).
- Women's workload rises without change in household power.
- Hierarchy: groups tend to form along caste and kin lines; leaders often from better-off/dominant families (elite capture).
- Landless Dalit women find it harder to start viable enterprises; benefits skew to those with assets.
- Positive side: SHGs also give poor women collective voice, bank access and public roles.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's SHG movement (PSRLM) is weaker than in South India; groups in Malwa villages are often caste-segregated; Dalit women's groups have been used to claim panchayat common land and wages.

*Conclusion:* SHGs work when linked to markets, skills and rights-based mobilisation; otherwise they can mirror existing hierarchies.

**Q3.** "Parliamentary neutrality rests on the impartial functioning of the Speaker of the Lok Sabha." Discuss. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Speaker's role (anti-defection, money bill certification, no-confidence motions) is a perennial polity theme.

*Intro:* The Speaker (Art. 93) is the presiding officer and guardian of the rights of the House; impartiality is the foundation of fair debate and legislative supremacy.

*Points:*
- Powers: maintains order, decides points of order, certifies Money Bills (Art. 110(3)), decides disqualification under the Tenth Schedule, refers bills to committees, casting vote only (Art. 100).
- Safeguards: salary charged on Consolidated Fund; removal only by effective majority with 14 days' notice (Art. 94); conduct not discussed except on substantive motion.
- Concerns: delays in deciding defection petitions; partisan Money Bill certification (Aadhaar Act 2016 debate); mass suspensions of MPs; Deputy Speaker post left vacant through the 17th Lok Sabha (2019–24).
- Judicial view: Kihoto Hollohan (1992) — Speaker acts as a tribunal subject to judicial review; Keisham Meghachandra (2020) — decide defection pleas within about three months; Subhash Desai (2023) on Speaker's duties in split cases.
- Reform: UK convention ('once Speaker, always Speaker'; Speaker resigns party), independent tribunal for defection cases (suggested by SC in 2020), time-bound decisions.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab Vidhan Sabha Speaker faces similar issues in defection and privilege matters.

*Conclusion:* A neutral Speaker turns majority rule into deliberative democracy; institutional safeguards should not depend only on the individual.

**Q4.** Analyse the role of private institutions in implementing Punjab government schemes through the Public-Private Partnership (PPP) model. Discuss their contributions and challenges in service delivery. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* PPP is asked in GS-II, GS-III and GS-IV of this exam — high-frequency theme for PPSC.

*Intro:* Under PPP, private firms or non-profits finance, build or run public services while government retains policy control and pays through user charges or annuities. Punjab set up the Punjab Infrastructure Development Board under the Punjab Infrastructure (Development & Regulation) Act, 2002.

*Points:*
- Health: private hospitals empanelled under Ayushman Bharat–Mukh Mantri Sarbat Sehat Bima Yojana provide cashless treatment; PPP dialysis units.
- Transport: PPP bus terminals (e.g. ISBT Mohali), toll roads and bridges.

- Challenges: weak contract design and monitoring, delayed payments by the state, disputes and terminated contracts (municipal waste contracts in Ludhiana), user-charge resistance, profit-driven exclusion of poor and remote areas, fraud in insurance claims.
- Fixes: independent regulator, output-based contracts, social audit, grievance redress.

*Punjab:*
- As above: AB-MMSBY in Punjab, PIDB projects, municipal waste contracts.

*Conclusion:* PPP can extend Punjab's limited fiscal capacity, but only with strong contracts, regulation and equity safeguards.

**Q5.** Analyse whether Parliament's amending power under Article 368 brings India closer to parliamentary sovereignty rather than constitutional supremacy. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Basic structure/amending power is a classic; comparative questions (UK vs India) recur.

*Intro:* Article 368 gives Parliament wide power to amend the Constitution by special majority (and state ratification for federal provisions), but judicial review limits it.

*Points:*
- Wide power: over 100 amendments since 1950; most need only special majority — flexibility like a sovereign Parliament.
- Early view: Shankari Prasad (1951) and Sajjan Singh (1965) upheld unlimited power.
- Golaknath (1967): Fundamental Rights cannot be amended; 24th Amendment (1971) reasserted the power.
- Kesavananda Bharati (1973): Parliament can amend any part but not the 'basic structure'.
- 42nd Amendment (1976) clauses barring review struck down in Minerva Mills (1980); NJAC (99th Amendment) struck down in 2015.
- Hence India has constitutional supremacy: Parliament is a creature of the Constitution, unlike the UK where Parliament is sovereign.
- Balance: flexibility of amendment + judicial guardianship of core values.

*Punjab:*
- Federal amendments affecting states (e.g. GST, 101st Amendment) need ratification by half the states, including Punjab's legislature.

*Conclusion:* Article 368 makes India's Constitution flexible, not Parliament sovereign; the basic structure doctrine keeps constitutional supremacy.

**Q6.** Discuss the "Accommodative Spirit of Federalism" inherent in the 101st Constitutional Amendment Act. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* GST federalism is live (GST 2.0 of Sept 2025, compensation ending, state revenue worries in Punjab).

*Intro:* The 101st Amendment (2016) introduced the Goods and Services Tax from 1 July 2017, pooling the taxing powers of Centre and States.

*Points:*
- Concurrent power: Art. 246A lets both Parliament and State legislatures levy GST — a break from strict separation of tax lists.
- GST Council (Art. 279A): Union Finance Minister + State Finance Ministers; Centre has one-third vote weight, States two-thirds; decisions need three-fourths majority — neither side can act alone.
- States surrendered sales tax/VAT, entry tax etc. for a common market — 'pooled sovereignty'.
- Compensation for revenue loss for five years (GST Compensation to States Act 2017).
- Mohit Minerals (2022): Council recommendations are persuasive, not binding — legislatures retain autonomy.
- Continuing consensus: rate rationalisation in September 2025 (two main slabs, 5% and 18%) was agreed in the Council.
- Tensions: end of compensation, delays in dues, loss of fiscal autonomy for producing states.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab, a consuming but agrarian state with low tax base, has sought extended compensation and fair IGST settlement.

*Conclusion:* GST shows cooperative federalism in action: shared sovereignty, collective bargaining and consensus decision-making.

**Q7.** Explain how the recent RTI amendments might compromise the Information Commission's "Check-and-Balance" function. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* RTI changes via DPDP Act 2023 and Rules 2025 are live; transparency is a stable GS-II theme.

*Intro:* Information Commissions under the RTI Act 2005 are the appellate watchdogs that force public authorities to disclose information.

*Points:*
- RTI (Amendment) Act 2019: tenure, salaries and service conditions of the Chief Information Commissioner and ICs (Centre and States) now decided by the Central Government (rules fixed a 3-year term) instead of being equal to Election Commissioners.
- Effect: reduced security of tenure and status; perception of executive control; states lose say over their own commissions.
- DPDP Act 2023, Sec. 44(3): amends Sec. 8(1)(j) to exempt all 'personal information' — removing the public-interest override that allowed disclosure of officials' assets, attendance etc.
- This can shield misuse of public office (beneficiary lists, contractor details) from scrutiny.
- Other weaknesses: large vacancies and pendency in commissions; SC in Anjali Bhardwaj (2019) directed timely appointments.
- Counter-view: privacy (Puttaswamy 2017) needs protection; uniform conditions bring parity.
- Way forward: restore public-interest test, statutory fixed tenure, transparent appointments.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab State Information Commission has faced vacancies and backlog; RTI is widely used for land and recruitment records.

*Conclusion:* An Information Commission that depends on the executive cannot check the executive; amendments should not dilute the RTI's spirit.

**Q8.** Justify the filing of large numbers of Public Interest Litigations (PILs) in the context of Separation of Powers. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Judicial activism vs separation of powers is a recurring GS-II theme.

*Intro:* PIL relaxes locus standi so that any public-spirited person can approach courts (Art. 32/226) for those unable to do so; it grew from S.P. Gupta (1981) and Hussainara Khatoon (1979).

*Points:*
- Justification: executive inaction and legislative gaps leave rights unprotected — PIL fills governance deficits.
- Access to justice for poor and voiceless (bonded labour – Bandhua Mukti Morcha 1984; undertrials).
- Landmark outcomes: Vishaka guidelines (1997), M.C. Mehta environment cases, Right to Food case (PUCL, 2001) leading to mid-day meals.
- Separation of powers in India is functional, not rigid (Ram Jawaya Kapur 1955); courts enforce constitutional limits, not usurp.
- Concerns: frivolous/publicity PILs clog courts; judicial overreach into policy (e.g. liquor ban near highways).
- Safeguards: SC guidelines in State of Uttaranchal v. Balwant Singh Chaufal (2010), costs on frivolous petitions, judicial restraint.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab & Haryana High Court PILs on stubble burning, drug menace and illegal mining pushed state action.

*Conclusion:* PILs are justified as a corrective when other organs fail — provided courts use restraint and filter frivolous petitions.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's exports (hosiery, bicycles, sports goods, basmati) are exposed to tariff changes.

*Conclusion:* The world is not de-globalising fully but re-globalising on national-security terms; India's path is strategic autonomy with selective openness.

**Q11.** Evaluate the argument that the Government of India Act 1935 served as the "blueprint" of the Indian Constitution. Identify areas of continuity and change. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Historical underpinnings of the Constitution are a basic syllabus item; asked repeatedly.

*Intro:* The Government of India Act 1935 (321 sections, 10 schedules) was the longest Act of the British Parliament and heavily influenced the structure of the Indian Constitution.

*Points:*
- Continuity – federal scheme: three lists of subjects (Federal, Provincial, Concurrent) became the Seventh Schedule.
- Office of Governor and his role; emergency provisions (basis of Arts. 352, 356).
- Federal Court (1937) → Supreme Court; Public Service Commissions; Comptroller and Auditor-General.
- Administrative details: centre–state relations, bicameral legislatures in some provinces.
- Change – sovereignty: the Constitution flows from 'We the People', not British Parliament.
- Fundamental Rights (Part III) and Directive Principles (Part IV) absent in 1935 Act.
- Universal adult franchise replaced limited property-based franchise; separate electorates abolished.
- Full parliamentary responsible government at the Centre; Governor's discretion curtailed; Preamble and secular, socialist values; independent judiciary with judicial review.
- Assessment: the 1935 Act was the administrative skeleton (Ambedkar said the Constitution borrowed from it), but the spirit was transformed.

*Punjab:*
- Provincial autonomy under the 1935 Act led to the Unionist Party ministry in Punjab (1937) under Sikander Hayat Khan.

*Conclusion:* The 1935 Act was the blueprint of structure, not of philosophy; the Constitution added rights, democracy and sovereignty.

*Diagram:* Two-column table: continuity vs change.

**Q12.** The Indian Constitution envisages an independent state legislature but political centralization often curtails it. Critically examine how political dynamics and coalition politics influence legislative autonomy in states. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Governor–state friction (Punjab, Tamil Nadu, Kerala) and the Nov 2025 SC opinion keep this central.

*Intro:* Under the Seventh Schedule, State legislatures have exclusive power over the State List, yet political centralisation reduces their real autonomy.

*Points:*
- Governor's role: delays in assent (Art. 200), reservation of bills for the President (Art. 201), summoning/proroguing disputes.
- Judicial view: State of Tamil Nadu v. Governor (April 2025) set timelines; but the Presidential Reference opinion (20 Nov 2025) held courts cannot fix timelines and there is no 'deemed assent', while allowing limited review of prolonged, unexplained inaction.
- Party high commands: when the same party rules Centre and State, legislative agendas follow central directions; CM selection by high command.
- Fiscal centralisation: cesses and surcharges outside divisible pool; centrally sponsored schemes with conditions.
- Use of Art. 356 historically (curbed by S.R. Bommai, 1994).
- Coalition era (1989–2014): regional parties gained bargaining power, strengthening federalism (e.g. Inter-State Council activated).
- Single-party majority periods: return of centralising trends; Concurrent List laws (farm laws 2020) seen as intrusive.
- State assemblies sit few days a year, weakening deliberation.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab: disputes with the Governor over summoning the Budget session and assent to bills (SC judgment, Nov 2023 – Governor cannot sit on bills indefinitely); Punjab Assembly resolutions against farm laws (2020) and on BBMB/Chandigarh.

*Conclusion:* Healthy federalism needs the Sarkaria/Punchhi recommendations implemented — a neutral Governor, consultation and fiscal space for states.

**Q13.** Explain the key components of the Sevottam Model and discuss how it improves efficiency, accountability, and citizen satisfaction in government services. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Citizen-centric governance is a repeated PPSC theme; Punjab's Right to Service Act makes it relevant.

*Intro:* Sevottam ('seva' + 'uttam') is a service-delivery excellence framework developed by the Department of Administrative Reforms and Public Grievances (DARPG) around 2006, recommended by the 2nd ARC; it is based on IS 15700:2005.

*Points:*
- Component 1 – Citizen's Charter: publicly declared service standards, time limits and costs.
- Component 2 – Public Grievance Redress: easy lodging, time-bound disposal, analysis of complaints (now CPGRAMS).
- Component 3 – Service Delivery Capability: leadership, strategy, people (staff), resources, infrastructure and continuous improvement.
- Seven-step cycle: define services and clients, set standards, develop capability, perform, monitor, evaluate, improve.
- Efficiency: standard processes, measured timelines, reduced discretion.
- Accountability: published commitments make officials answerable; grievance data shows weak points.
- Citizen satisfaction: feedback loops, single-window and online services.
- Limitations: charters often generic, no penalties, poor awareness; needs statutory backing (Right to Services laws).

*Punjab:*
- Punjab Right to Service Act, 2011 and Sewa Kendras (single-window citizen service centres); Punjab Transparency and Accountability in Delivery of Public Services Act, 2018.

*Conclusion:* Sevottam shifts administration from rules to results; with legal right-to-service backing it becomes enforceable.

*Diagram:* Triangle diagram: Citizen's Charter – Grievance Redress – Service Delivery Capability.

**Q16.** Analyse the impact of the Right to Privacy judgment (Puttaswamy) on the State's e-governance architecture. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Data protection (DPDP Rules 2025), Aadhaar and digital public infrastructure keep privacy central.

- Positive: privacy-by-design builds trust in digital public infrastructure (UPI, CoWIN, DigiLocker).

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's e-governance (Sewa Kendras, land records, Aadhaar-linked ration cards) must follow DPDP norms; exclusion of elderly due to biometric mismatch needs offline alternatives.

*Conclusion:* Puttaswamy made the citizen the owner of data; e-governance must be efficient and rights-respecting at the same time.

**Q17.** India's 'neighbourhood first' policy needs a perceptible overhaul in the light of the prevailing anti-India sentiment in South Asian countries such as Nepal and Bangladesh. Comment. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Rapid political change in Bangladesh (2024–26) and Nepal (2025–26) makes neighbourhood policy a top IR theme.

*Intro:* Neighbourhood First (2014) seeks connectivity, trade and people-to-people ties with South Asian neighbours, but anti-India sentiment has risen in several countries.

*Points:*
- Bangladesh: after Sheikh Hasina's ouster (Aug 2024) and her stay in India, ties cooled; a new BNP government under Tarique Rahman took office after the Feb 2026 election.
- Nepal: map dispute (Lipulekh–Kalapani, 2020), memory of 2015 blockade; Gen-Z protests (Sept 2025) toppled the Oli government; new government after March 2026 election.
- Other cases: Maldives 'India Out' campaign (2023) followed by repair; Sri Lanka's debt crisis aid (2022) improved image.
- Causes: perception of big-brother attitude, interference charges, delayed projects, trade/water disputes (Teesta), China's BRI offers.
- Overhaul ideas: engage all political actors, not one party; deliver projects faster; asymmetric trade concessions; people-centric diplomacy (scholarships, health, digital public goods); respect sovereignty.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's own border with Pakistan shows how hostility hurts trade (Attari–Wagah trade suspended).

*Conclusion:* Neighbourhood policy must move from government-to-government to society-to-society trust; generosity and non-interference are India's best tools.

**Q20.** 'Indian diaspora today constitutes the largest migrant population in the world with its economic ties to the homeland remaining unsurpassed in terms of remittances. However, its role in the shaping of Indian foreign policy is limited at best.' Do you agree with this statement? Give reasons for your answer. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Diaspora is a recurring IR theme and is especially relevant for Punjab (Canada, UK, US).

*Intro:* India has the world's largest diaspora (about 3.5 crore NRIs and PIOs as per MEA) and is the top recipient of remittances — a record USD 135.46 billion in FY 2024-25 (RBI).

*Points:*
- Agree – limited role: diaspora rarely shapes core decisions on security, China or Pakistan; foreign policy remains state-led.
- Host-country loyalties and politics differ; diaspora is divided by region, religion and politics.
- Some diaspora activism (e.g. separatist groups in Canada) has strained ties rather than helped.
- Disagree – significant influence: Indian-American lobbying helped the India–US nuclear deal (2008).
- Diaspora politicians in the UK, US, Canada, Mauritius shape bilateral agendas.
- Economic diplomacy: remittances, investment, technology transfer; soft power through culture and yoga.
- Institutions: Pravasi Bharatiya Divas, OCI card, evacuation missions (Operation Ganga 2022, Operation Kaveri 2023) show two-way bond.
- Balanced view: diaspora is an indirect, enabling force — influence is growing but not decisive.

*Punjab:*
- Punjabi diaspora in Canada is large and politically active; India–Canada ties nosedived in 2023–24 over the Nijjar case and have been gradually restored since 2025.
- Remittances support Doaba villages; NRI Sabha Punjab and NRI affairs department.

*Conclusion:* The diaspora is more an asset of soft power and economic ties than a driver of policy — India should engage it as partner, not lobby.
