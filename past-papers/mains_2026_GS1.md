**Q1.** Give the salient features of the Gandhara Art. How was it different from the Mathura Art? *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Art & culture appears in almost every PPSC/UPSC GS-I paper; Gandhara–Mathura comparison is a classic repeat theme.

*Intro:* Gandhara and Mathura were the two great schools of sculpture that flourished under the Kushanas (c. 1st–3rd century CE), and both gave the Buddha a human image.

*Points:*
- Gandhara (Peshawar valley, Taxila, Swat): Indo-Greek/Greco-Roman influence on Buddhist themes.
- Material: blue-grey schist and later stucco/lime plaster.
- Features: realistic anatomy, wavy hair, moustache, heavy draped robes with thick folds, spiritual calm, halo plain; Buddha often looks Apollo-like.
- Mathura (Braj region): indigenous school with roots in yaksha–yakshi folk images.
- Material: spotted red sandstone (Sikri).
- Mathura images: fleshy, energetic body, smiling face, shaven or snail-shell curls, thin transparent drapery, decorated halo.
- Themes: Gandhara mainly Buddhist; Mathura served Buddhist, Jain and Brahmanical (Vishnu, Shiva) faiths.
- Patronage: both under Kushanas (Kanishka); Mathura later fed into Gupta classical art (Sarnath).

*Punjab:*
- Sanghol (Fatehgarh Sahib) yielded Kushana-era carved railing pillars in Mathura-style red sandstone (excavated 1985).
- Punjab region (Taxila, Gandhara frontier) was the meeting ground of Greek and Indian art.

*Conclusion:* Gandhara gave the Buddha a Hellenistic body, Mathura an Indian soul; together they shaped the classical Gupta idiom.

*Diagram:* Two-column table: Gandhara vs Mathura — region, stone, influence, religion, body/face, drapery, halo.

*Punjab:*
- Bibi Gulab Kaur (Ghadar), Bibi Amar Kaur and women of the Gurdwara Reform (Akali) movement held protests and jathas in the 1920s.

**Q3.** Examine the contribution of the Arya Samaj in the evolution of education in British India. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Socio-religious reform with Punjab focus is named in the PPSC syllabus; Arya Samaj was strongest in Punjab.

*Intro:* The Arya Samaj, founded by Swami Dayanand Saraswati in Bombay in 1875 and strongest in Punjab after the Lahore Samaj (1877), made education its chief instrument of reform.

*Points:*
- DAV movement: Dayanand Anglo-Vedic School, Lahore (1886) under Lala Hansraj — combined Vedic learning with English and modern science.
- Gurukul stream: Swami Shraddhanand founded Gurukul Kangri, Haridwar (1902) on ancient residential lines with Hindi as medium.
- Women's education: Kanya Mahavidyalaya, Jalandhar (1896) — pioneering girls' institution.
- Promoted Hindi/Devanagari and vernacular instruction; mass network of schools and colleges.
- Opened education to lower castes through shuddhi and anti-untouchability work.
- Created self-reliant Indian-funded institutions, reducing dependence on missionary and government schools.
- Limitation: revivalist tone and shuddhi sharpened communal competition (Singh Sabha and Anjumans also built rival school networks).

*Punjab:*
- DAV College Lahore (1889) and today's DAV colleges in Jalandhar, Amritsar, Bathinda, Chandigarh; KMV Jalandhar.

*Conclusion:* The Samaj indigenised modern education and built an institutional network that still educates lakhs, though its revivalism had communal side-effects.

**Q4.** The Amritsar massacre left a scar on the Indo-British relations which could never be erased. Analyse. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Jallianwala Bagh is the single most-asked Punjab freedom-struggle event; anniversaries keep it live.

*Intro:* On 13 April 1919 (Baisakhi), troops under Brig.-Gen. Reginald Dyer fired without warning on an unarmed gathering in Jallianwala Bagh, Amritsar, protesting the Rowlatt Act and the arrest of Dr Satyapal and Dr Saifuddin Kitchlew.

*Points:*
- Official toll 379 killed (Hunter Committee); Indian estimates far higher.
- Martial law, the 'crawling order' and public floggings in Punjab deepened humiliation.
- Hunter Committee (1919–20) only censured Dyer; House of Lords praised him and a fund was raised for him — Indians saw British justice as racial.
- Rabindranath Tagore renounced his knighthood; Gandhi called cooperation with a 'satanic' government a sin — leading to the Non-Cooperation Movement (1920).
- Moderates lost faith in British fair play; the Congress Punjab Inquiry Committee report hardened opinion.
- Udham Singh shot Michael O'Dwyer, Punjab's Lt Governor, in London (13 March 1940).
- Even later, Britain stopped short of a formal apology (Theresa May expressed 'deep regret' in 2019).

*Punjab:*
- Jallianwala Bagh National Memorial (Trust Act 1951) at Amritsar; renovated memorial reopened in 2021.
- Udham Singh of Sunam remains a Punjab icon.

*Conclusion:* The massacre turned loyal subjects into nationalists; the wound remains part of India–UK memory despite a strong modern partnership.

**Q5.** Write a short note on cloud burst as hydro-meteorological disaster in India. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Repeated Himalayan cloudbursts (2013, 2023, 2025) and their spill-over floods into Punjab make it a live theme.

*Intro:* A cloudburst is extreme, short-duration rainfall over a small area; IMD treats about 100 mm or more of rain in an hour over roughly 20–30 sq km as a cloudburst.

*Points:*
- Cause: orographic lifting of moist monsoon air on steep slopes; convective cumulonimbus clouds; interaction of monsoon with western disturbances.
- Where: Himalayan states (Uttarakhand, Himachal, J&K, Ladakh), the north-east and Western Ghats.
- Effects: flash floods, debris flows, landslides, damage to roads, hydro projects and homes.
- Examples: Kedarnath (2013), Leh (2010), Himachal (2023), Dharali–Uttarkashi and Kishtwar (August 2025).
- Aggravators: construction on river banks, deforestation, road cutting, climate warming increasing extreme rain.
- Management: Doppler radars and nowcasting, early-warning sirens, river-bank zoning, slope stabilisation, NDMA guidelines on landslides and flash floods.

*Punjab:*
- Cloudbursts and heavy rain in the Sutlej, Beas and Ravi catchments in Himachal/J&K swell inflows to Bhakra and Pong dams and trigger floods downstream in Punjab (2023, 2025).

*Conclusion:* Cloudbursts cannot be prevented, but risk-sensitive land use and local early warning can turn disasters into manageable events.

*Diagram:* Flowchart: moist monsoon air → forced up slope → rapid condensation → cloudburst → flash flood/landslide → downstream flood.

*Intro:* India is called the 'pharmacy of the world': it is the third-largest producer of medicines by volume and supplies a large share of the world's generic medicines and vaccines.

*Points:*
- Clusters: Hyderabad (Genome Valley, bulk drugs), Ahmedabad–Vadodara–Ankleshwar (Gujarat), Mumbai–Pune, Baddi–Barotiwala–Nalagarh (HP), Visakhapatnam, Sikkim and Goa.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab has units in Mohali–Derabassi and Ludhiana and is close to the Baddi cluster; NIPER Mohali supports research.

*Conclusion:* Moving from volume to value — APIs at home, quality and innovation — will decide whether India stays the world's pharmacy.

**Q7.** Assess the status of water resources in Punjab. What are the key ecological and socio-economic challenges emerging from their overexploitation? *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Groundwater crisis is the most repeated Punjab theme across PPSC papers (asked again as GS-IV Q12 in the same exam).

*Intro:* Punjab, the 'land of five rivers', now has only three (Sutlej, Beas, Ravi) and relies heavily on groundwater for irrigation.

*Points:*
- Groundwater: the latest state assessment (2025-26) reports extraction of about 26.3 BCM against about 17.3 BCM available — stage of extraction about 152%; 110 of 153 blocks over-exploited.
- Surface water: canal systems (Bhakra, Sirhind, Upper Bari Doab) fed by Bhakra, Pong and Ranjit Sagar dams; canal share fell for decades but is now being revived.
- Ecological: falling water table, deeper submersible pumps, drying wetlands, salinity and waterlogging in south-west (Muktsar, Fazilka).
- Quality: arsenic, uranium, fluoride and nitrates in parts of Malwa; industrial pollution of Buddha Nullah and Kali Bein.
- Socio-economic: rising cost of deep tubewells pushes small farmers into debt; free power encourages over-pumping; health burden (cancer concerns in Malwa).
- Inter-state disputes: SYL canal, BBMB water sharing with Haryana and Rajasthan.
- Paddy (water-intensive) on over 30 lakh ha drives most of the extraction.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab Preservation of Subsoil Water Act, 2009 (delayed paddy transplanting).
- Canal revival: state reports restoring over 100 defunct canals and reviving the Kandi canal (2025-26).

*Conclusion:* Water security needs crop diversification, metered/power reforms and canal revival — otherwise Punjab's agriculture faces a hydrological cliff.

**Q8.** Discuss the issues affecting agricultural productivity and sustainability in Punjab. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Punjab agrarian crisis is asked in almost every PPSC mains; core to state policy debates 2026–29.

*Intro:* Punjab led the Green Revolution and still contributes heavily to central wheat and rice pools, but yields have plateaued and the system is ecologically stressed.

*Points:*
- Yield plateau: wheat and paddy yields among the highest in India but growth has slowed since the 2000s.
- Monoculture: wheat–paddy rotation reduces soil health and biodiversity.
- Soil: low organic carbon, micronutrient (zinc, manganese) deficiency, excess urea use.
- Water: over-extraction of groundwater (stage about 150%+).
- Residue burning: paddy stubble fires each Oct–Nov harm soil microbes and air quality.
- Economics: small holdings, high input cost, rising debt, dependence on MSP for two crops.
- Climate: terminal heat stress on wheat (e.g. March 2022 heatwave) and floods (2023, 2025).
- Pests: pink bollworm and whitefly in cotton belt; shrinking cotton area.

*Punjab:*
- PAU varieties (PR-126 short-duration paddy), Direct Seeded Rice incentives, Happy Seeder/super-seeder subsidies, crop diversification towards maize, cotton, basmati.

*Conclusion:* Sustainable productivity needs diversification, soil-health and water-saving technology with price support beyond wheat and paddy.

**Q9.** In the era of Digital Technology and Artificial Intelligence, ethical challenges have become increasingly complex. Critically analyse the significance of ethics in public sphere. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* AI ethics is a dominant theme across 2025–26 papers (also in this Essay paper); 'ethics and society' is in GS-I syllabus.

*Intro:* Ethics in the public sphere means that decisions affecting citizens rest on values such as fairness, transparency, accountability and respect for dignity — even more vital when algorithms make or shape those decisions.

*Points:*
- New challenges: privacy and mass data collection; algorithmic bias in welfare, policing and hiring; deepfakes and misinformation; opacity of 'black box' AI; accountability gaps.
- Ethics ensures technology serves people: consent, purpose limitation and data minimisation (Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023; Rules notified 2025).
- Human oversight: officials must not hide behind 'the system decided'.
- Trust: public trust in institutions depends on visible fairness and grievance redress.
- Inclusion: avoiding digital exclusion of the poor, elderly and rural citizens.
- Frameworks: Puttaswamy (2017) privacy test of legality, necessity, proportionality; India AI Governance Guidelines (MeitY, Nov 2025).
- Critique: ethics without law and enforcement remains voluntary; regulation must not choke innovation.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's online land records and e-Sewa services need data protection and human help-desks for digitally weak citizens.

*Conclusion:* Technology changes the tools of governance, not its moral purpose; ethics keeps AI accountable to citizens.

**Q11.** Bhagat Singh was not merely a martyr but also a thinker who left behind a rich intellectual legacy. Elucidate. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Bhagat Singh is Punjab's central freedom-struggle figure; thinker-angle questions recur across PPSC and UPSC.

*Points:*
- Socialist vision: renamed HRA as Hindustan Socialist Republican Association (1928) — freedom meant ending exploitation of man by man, not just transfer of power.

- Slogans: popularised 'Inquilab Zindabad' and the idea of mass mobilisation over individual terror.
- Assembly bomb (8 April 1929) aimed 'to make the deaf hear' — symbolic, not to kill.

*Punjab:*
- Born at Banga (Lyallpur, now Pakistan); ancestral village Khatkar Kalan (SBS Nagar); Hussainiwala memorial (Ferozepur); Mohali airport named after him.

*Conclusion:* Bhagat Singh's legacy is an argument for a secular, rational, egalitarian India — which is why his ideas remain relevant to youth today.

**Q12.** Adam Smith illustrated the market as an invisible hand which can pave the path to prosperity and well-being for all, but Marx pointed its gloomy side. Critically analyse their respective ideologies. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* World history/ideologies (capitalism, socialism) appear in the syllabus; ideology comparison is a UPSC favourite.

*Intro:* Adam Smith's 'The Wealth of Nations' (1776) founded classical liberal economics; Karl Marx (Communist Manifesto 1848 with Engels, Das Kapital 1867) offered its most powerful critique.

*Points:*
- Smith: self-interest in a competitive market, guided by the 'invisible hand', allocates resources efficiently and raises wealth.
- Smith: division of labour raises productivity; limited state — defence, justice, public works.
- Smith also wrote 'Theory of Moral Sentiments' (1759): markets need moral sympathy and rules.
- Marx: capitalism exploits labour — surplus value goes to the capitalist; workers become alienated.
- Marx: recurring crises, concentration of capital and class conflict lead to revolution and a classless society.
- Assessment of Smith: markets did produce growth (Industrial Revolution, post-1991 India) but also inequality, monopolies and market failures (externalities, 1929 and 2008 crises).
- Assessment of Marx: correctly foresaw inequality and labour struggles, but command economies (USSR) failed on efficiency and freedom.
- Synthesis: welfare state, Keynesian demand management and mixed economy; India's Directive Principles and post-1991 'market with safety nets'.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's Green Revolution (state-supported market) and current MSP debate show the mix of market and state.

*Conclusion:* Neither the pure invisible hand nor the pure planned economy works; modern states blend Smith's efficiency with Marx's concern for justice.

**Q13.** Maharaja Ranjit Singh was the most accomplished ruler of the first half of the nineteenth century India. Do you think that he could have done more to save Punjab from the yoke of British Imperialism? *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Ranjit Singh and British relations is a named syllabus node and a near-annual PPSC theme.

*Intro:* Maharaja Ranjit Singh (1780–1839) united the misls, took Lahore in 1799 and built a sovereign Sikh state stretching from the Sutlej to Peshawar and Kashmir.

*Points:*
- Achievements: modern Fauj-i-Khas trained by European officers (Ventura, Allard); strong artillery; conquest of Multan (1818), Kashmir (1819), Peshawar (1834).

- Counter-view: the Company had overwhelming resources; avoiding war kept Punjab independent for 40 years — realism, not weakness.

*Punjab:*
- Legacy: Lahore Darbar, Gobindgarh Fort (Amritsar), gold plating of Harmandar Sahib; Koh-i-Noor.

*Conclusion:* Ranjit Singh's realism preserved Punjab in his lifetime; his failure lay in not institutionalising the state, which made annexation easier after him.

**Q14.** Explain the major physiographic regions of Punjab. Discuss their role in shaping soil distribution, irrigation patterns, and cropping systems. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Punjab physiography is a named syllabus node; questions on regions/soils/crops recur.

*Intro:* Punjab (50,362 sq km) is mostly a flat alluvial plain built by the Sutlej, Beas and Ravi, sloping gently from north-east to south-west.

*Points:*
- Shivalik hills (north-east: Pathankot, Hoshiarpur, Ropar, Mohali): low, eroded hills; thin, gravelly soils; rain-fed maize and wheat; agroforestry.
- Kandi belt (foot of Shivaliks): porous gravelly soils, deep water table, seasonal streams (chos) cause erosion; Kandi canal and small dams for irrigation.
- Bet / flood plains (river beds of Sutlej, Beas, Ravi): new alluvium (khadar), fertile but flood-prone; paddy, sugarcane, vegetables.
- Upland alluvial plain (Majha, Doaba, central Malwa): old alluvium (bangar), loamy fertile soils; dense tubewell and canal network; wheat–paddy core.
- South-western sandy plain (Bathinda, Mansa, Fazilka, Muktsar): sandy, dune-affected soils; earlier gram, bajra; cotton–wheat after canals (Sirhind, Bhakra); waterlogging and salinity in parts.
- Irrigation link: central plains use groundwater; south-west depends on canals because groundwater is saline.
- Cropping link: rice–wheat in fresh-water plains; cotton–wheat in south-west; maize and fodder in Kandi.

*Punjab:*
- Kinnow belt of Abohar–Fazilka, basmati in Majha, potatoes in Doaba (Jalandhar–Hoshiarpur).

*Conclusion:* Physiography explains why Punjab has distinct agro-zones; diversification must follow these natural differences.

*Diagram:* Sketch map of Punjab: Shivaliks (NE), Kandi strip, river Bet zones, central plain, SW sandy plain; arrows for slope NE→SW.

**Q15.** Discuss the revised Seismic Zonation Map of India released by the Bureau of Standards in Nov 2025 and its implications. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Very topical; the map was withdrawn in March 2026, so a question on the old vs new code and policy reversal is plausible.

*Intro:* In November 2025 the Bureau of Indian Standards notified the seventh revision of IS 1893 (Part 1):2025, the earthquake-resistant design code, with a new seismic zonation map.

*Points:*
- Basis: Probabilistic Seismic Hazard Assessment (PSHA) using active-fault mapping, historical seismicity and GPS strain data, instead of mainly past-earthquake records.
- New Zone VI (highest hazard) created; the entire Himalayan arc (J&K to Arunachal) placed in it, ending the old split between Zones IV and V.
- About 61% of India's land was shown in moderate-to-high hazard zones, where roughly 75% of people live.
- Settlements on or near a zone boundary to be treated as being in the higher zone.
- Code changes: updated design spectra, soil–structure interaction, liquefaction checks, stricter rules for tall and irregular buildings.
- Implications: safer buildings, hospitals, dams and bridges; higher construction cost (estimated 10–30% higher design forces); retrofitting needs; urban planning in Himalayan towns.
- Policy reversal: after MoHUA objections on cost and lack of consultation (metro projects), BIS withdrew the 2025 code in March 2026; IS 1893:2016 with four zones (II–V) is again in force.
- Lesson: balance scientific risk with costs through phased adoption and stakeholder consultation.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's northern districts near the Shivalik foothills lie in high-hazard areas (Zone IV under the 2016 code); Bhakra and Ranjit Sagar dams and Chandigarh tri-city high-rises need seismic safety audits.

*Conclusion:* Science says India's seismic risk is higher than earlier assumed; the reversal shows the need to align building safety with costs, not abandon it.

*Diagram:* Outline map of India showing Himalayan arc, Kutch and North-East as highest hazard, Indo-Gangetic plain high, peninsula low.

**Q16.** What is the importance of glaciers and ice caps? Explain glacial retreat changes in glaciers and their impact on environment. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Climate-cryosphere links (UN International Year of Glaciers' Preservation 2025) and Punjab's glacier-fed rivers make it relevant.

*Intro:* Glaciers and ice caps hold about 69% of the world's fresh water; the Hindu Kush–Himalaya is called the 'Third Pole'.

*Points:*
- Importance: perennial water source for rivers (Indus, Ganga, Brahmaputra); regulate sea level and earth's albedo; climate archives (ice cores); support hydropower and agriculture.
- Retreat evidence: most Himalayan glaciers (Gangotri, Siachen region, Chhota Shigri) are thinning and retreating; ICIMOD (2023) warned HKH glaciers could lose up to 80% of volume by 2100 under high emissions.
- Causes: global warming, black carbon and dust deposition, less winter snowfall.
- Impact – water: short-term higher melt, long-term fall in summer flows ('peak water').
- Impact – hazards: glacial lake outburst floods (South Lhonak, Sikkim, Oct 2023), landslides.
- Impact – sea level and ecosystems: polar ice loss raises sea levels; loss of habitats; changes in river ecology.

**Q20.** Caste in India functions as a system of hierarchy and exploitation rather than merely a form of social stratification. Critically analyse. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Caste is a perennial GS-I theme; Punjab has India's highest SC share, giving a strong state angle.

*Intro:* Every society is stratified, but caste is distinctive because rank is fixed by birth, linked to purity–pollution, occupation and endogamy.

*Points:*
- Hierarchy: graded inequality (Ambedkar) — each caste is above some and below others, which blocks unity.
- Exploitation: untouchability, forced and bonded labour, landlessness of Dalits, manual scavenging.
- Economic dimension: land ownership concentrated among upper/dominant castes; Dalits largely agricultural labourers.
- Violence and discrimination: atrocities, social boycott, separate cremation grounds in some villages.
- Counter-view: caste also works as social network, identity and mobilisation tool (Sanskritisation – M.N. Srinivas; dominant caste; caste associations).
- Change: constitutional safeguards (Art. 15, 16, 17, 46), reservation, SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act 1989, urbanisation and education weaken ritual hierarchy.
- Persistence: endogamy, honour crimes, caste in politics show it remains a system of power.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab has the highest share of Scheduled Castes in India — 31.9% (Census 2011) — but they own a very small share of farmland; Dalit struggles over panchayat common land (ZPSC agitation in Sangrur).
- Sikh and Ad-Dharm movements opposed caste, yet separate gurdwaras and cremation grounds persist in some villages.

*Conclusion:* Caste is more than stratification — it is unequal distribution of dignity and resources; constitutional morality and economic empowerment are the remedy.
