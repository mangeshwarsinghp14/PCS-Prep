**Q1.** Economics is too Important to be left to the Economists. *(50 marks, 800 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Philosophical economy topics recur in PPSC essays.

*Intro:* Open with a policy that looked good on paper but hurt people (e.g., a rigid model ignoring farmers' realities).

*Points:*
- Meaning: economic choices shape lives, so they need inputs from society, ethics, ecology and politics, not just models.
- Limits of economics: assumptions, GDP fixation, failure to foresee crises (2008).
- Role of economists: evidence, cost-benefit, forecasting — essential but not sufficient.
- Other voices: sociologists, environmentalists, farmers, workers, women — e.g., behavioural economics, Amartya Sen's capability approach.
- Democracy: budgets and policies need public debate and accountability.
- Indian examples: demonetisation (2016) debate; farm laws (2020) and lack of consultation; GST design.
- Counter-view: populism can ignore sound economics — balance expertise with participation.
- Way forward: evidence-based, participatory policy making.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's free power and wheat–paddy cycle show how economics, ecology and politics intertwine.

*Conclusion:* Economics must stay grounded in human welfare and democratic choice.

**Q2.** Indian Democracy is Increasingly becoming Hostage to Party Politics. *(50 marks, 800 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Democracy and party politics essays recur; defections and polarisation keep it live.

*Intro:* Start with a scene of a state government falling overnight due to defections.

*Points:*
- Role of parties in democracy — aggregation of interests, choice, accountability.
- Symptoms: defections, high command culture, dynasties, lack of inner-party democracy.
- Money and muscle power; criminalisation; opaque funding (electoral bonds struck down 2024).
- Polarisation and identity politics; parliamentary disruptions; partisan use of institutions.
- Freebies and short-termism over long-term reforms.
- Counter-view: parties still give voice to the marginalised; high voter turnout shows democratic vitality.
- Reforms: inner-party democracy, Tenth Schedule reform, state funding, RTI for parties, empowered ECI.
- Citizens' role: informed voting, civil society, free media.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's shift from two-party politics to new parties (2022) shows voters can punish entrenched parties.

*Conclusion:* Parties are essential, but democracy must not be their hostage; reform and vigilant citizens can restore balance.

**Q3.** Pandemic Times: Experiments with Different Models of Technology and Sociality. *(50 marks, 800 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Covid-specific; future essays more likely on technology and society in general.

*Intro:* Begin with images of online classes, telemedicine and family Zoom calls during lockdown.

*Points:*
- Technology as lifeline: work from home, e-learning, telemedicine, digital payments, CoWIN.
- New sociality: virtual gatherings, mutual-aid groups, langar and gurdwara relief.
- Digital divide: rural students without devices; elderly excluded.
- Surveillance and privacy concerns (contact-tracing apps).
- Mental health and isolation; blurred work-life boundaries.
- Hybrid models after the pandemic: hybrid work, telemedicine policy, ed-tech boom and bust.
- Lessons: tech must be inclusive; community remains irreplaceable.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's 'Mission Fateh' and gurdwara langars combined tech and community during Covid.

*Conclusion:* The pandemic showed technology can connect us, but human solidarity holds society together.

**Q4.** Agrarian Crisis in Punjab: Need for Sagacious Agro-Economic Policies. *(50 marks, 800 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Punjab agriculture is the single most repeated Punjab theme across PPSC papers.

*Intro:* Open with the paradox: India's food bowl, yet farmers in debt and groundwater falling.

*Points:*
- Green Revolution legacy: wheat–paddy monoculture, MSP-assured procurement.
- Ecological crisis: groundwater extraction above 150% of recharge; soil health decline; stubble burning.
- Economic crisis: stagnant yields, rising costs, small holdings, indebtedness, farmer suicides.
- Social: migration, drugs, rural youth unemployment.
- Policy gaps: free power, limited diversification support, weak agro-processing.
- Sagacious policies: crop diversification with assured markets (maize, pulses, basmati, horticulture), direct seeding of rice, water pricing reform, FPOs, agro-processing clusters, crop insurance, research (PAU).
- Centre–state cooperation on procurement and incentives.
- Farmers' voice: consultation lessons from 2020–21 protests.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab Economic Survey 2025-26: agriculture about 23% of GSVA, contracted 2.5% after 2025 floods.

*Conclusion:* Punjab needs a new agrarian compact — ecologically sustainable, economically viable and socially just.

**Q5.** Ethics and Politics of Environment. *(50 marks, 800 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Environment-related essays appear almost every year.

*Intro:* Start with a contrast: a farmer burning stubble to survive vs a city child breathing smoke.

*Points:*
- Ethics: intergenerational equity, rights of nature, Gandhi's 'need not greed', Indian traditions (Bishnois).
- Politics: development vs environment; who pays and who benefits.
- Global: common but differentiated responsibilities, climate finance, Paris Agreement (2015).
- National: EIA dilution debates, forest clearances, pollution control.
- Environmental justice: tribals, fishers and the poor bear the brunt.
- Green politics and movements: Chipko, Narmada, youth climate movements.
- Way forward: ethical consumption, green growth, participatory governance, Mission LiFE.

*Punjab:*
- Stubble burning, Buddha Nullah pollution and groundwater depletion are Punjab's ethical-political test.

*Conclusion:* Environment is a moral question first; good politics must follow good ethics.

**Q6.** Indian Diaspora's Nativist Glance and Contribution. *(50 marks, 800 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Diaspora is central to Punjab; migration questions recur.

*Intro:* Begin with a Punjabi village with 'NRI kothis' and a Canadian flag on a rooftop water tank.

*Points:*
- Size and spread: one of the world's largest diasporas.
- Contributions: remittances (India the top recipient globally), investment, knowledge and technology transfer, philanthropy.
- Soft power and lobbying for India abroad; Pravasi Bharatiya Divas.
- 'Nativist glance': emotional attachment to homeland, culture, religion; sometimes long-distance nationalism and separatist fringe.
- Challenges: brain drain, exploitation of migrant workers, illegal migration, racism abroad.
- Policy: OCI cards, MADAD portal, e-Migrate, Emigration Bill.
- Balance: engage diaspora constructively while countering hostile elements.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's diaspora in Canada, UK, US, Australia funds schools, hospitals, gurdwaras; also issues of illegal 'donkey route' migration and deportations (2025).

*Conclusion:* The diaspora is a bridge between India and the world; its love for the homeland should be channelled into development.
