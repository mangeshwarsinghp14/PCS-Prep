**Q1.** Critically explain the financial relationship between the Centre and the States against the backdrop of the recommendations of 14th Finance Commission. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Fiscal federalism is asked repeatedly; the 16th Finance Commission period (2026–31) keeps it live.

*Intro:* The 14th Finance Commission (chaired by Y.V. Reddy; award period 2015–20) reshaped Centre–State finances.

*Points:*
- Raised states' share in the divisible pool of central taxes from 32% to 42% — the largest jump ever.
- Horizontal formula: population 1971 (17.5%), population 2011 (10%), area (15%), forest cover (7.5%), income distance (50%).
- Revenue-deficit grants for states; grants to local bodies; ended the plan/non-plan distinction (Planning Commission replaced by NITI Aayog, 2015).
- Gains: more untied funds and autonomy for states.
- Offsets: Centre cut its share in several centrally sponsored schemes; rising cesses and surcharges (not shared with states) reduced the effective divisible pool.
- GST (2017) pooled indirect-tax powers; compensation for five years ended in June 2022.
- 15th FC kept devolution at 41% (after J&K's reorganisation); debates on using 2011 population penalising southern states.
- Issues: vertical imbalance, conditional borrowing limits, delays in releases; the 16th Finance Commission covers 2026–31.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's high debt and revenue deficit make it dependent on revenue-deficit grants; it seeks compensation for border-state costs and loss of GST compensation.

*Conclusion:* Fiscal federalism improved after the 14th FC, but cess growth and conditionalities need correction for true cooperative federalism.

**Q2.** Explain the main aspects of the role of the Reserve Bank of India under the present financial regime compared to the regime prior to demonetization. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* RBI's role and monetary framework recur in economy questions.

*Intro:* RBI's role changed around 2016 with the inflation-targeting framework and demonetisation (8 November 2016).

*Points:*
- Before: multiple-indicator approach; Governor decided policy rates; less formal inflation mandate.
- After the RBI Act amendment (2016): six-member Monetary Policy Committee; flexible inflation target of 4% (±2%).
- Demonetisation tested currency management — withdrawal and replacement of about 86% of currency in circulation by value.
- Push to digital payments: UPI growth, payment-system regulation, later the digital rupee pilots (2022).
- Stronger supervision: Prompt Corrective Action for weak banks, Asset Quality Review (2015), IBC support; co-operative banks under fuller RBI regulation (2020 amendment).
- Government–RBI relationship debates: surplus transfer (Bimal Jalan Committee, 2019), autonomy issues (2018).
- Financial inclusion and consumer protection: Integrated Ombudsman (2021).

*Punjab:*


*Conclusion:* RBI has moved to a rule-based, more transparent role focused on price and financial stability, with digital payments at its core.

**Q3.** Despite subsidies and minimum support price, the state of farmers has not changed drastically. Discuss the reasons for the failure. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Farm distress, MSP and subsidies are asked almost every year — central to Punjab.

*Intro:* Huge subsidies and MSP have not lifted most farmers out of distress.

*Points:*
- NSO Situation Assessment Survey (2019): average farm household monthly income about ₹10,218 and average debt about ₹74,121 (2018-19).
- MSP reaches few: effective procurement is mainly for wheat and paddy in a few states; Shanta Kumar Committee (2015) estimated only about 6% of farmers sell at MSP.
- Subsidies on fertiliser, power and water favour bigger farmers and encourage overuse (urea, groundwater).
- Small and fragmented holdings; low investment in irrigation and research; climate shocks.
- Weak markets: few regulated mandis, middlemen, poor storage and processing.
- Rising input costs and stagnant yields; little crop diversification.
- Way forward: income support (PM-KISAN since 2019), crop insurance, FPOs, market reforms, research, shift from input subsidy to direct support.

*Punjab:*
- Even in Punjab, with near-universal MSP procurement, small farmers face debt and stagnant incomes because of high costs and wheat–paddy lock-in.

*Conclusion:* Price support alone cannot fix farm distress; productivity, markets and risk protection must improve together.

**Q4.** Discuss the role of education and health in enhancing the human capital. Explain its importance in economic development. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Human capital and social sector are recurring.

*Intro:* Human capital is the knowledge, skills and health that make people productive (Schultz, Becker).

*Points:*
- Education raises productivity, innovation, adaptability and earnings; women's education improves child health and lowers fertility.
- Health increases working years, energy and learning ability; reduces lost workdays and medical poverty.
- Endogenous growth theory sees human capital as a key driver of long-run growth; East Asian economies are examples.
- India: demographic dividend depends on skilling; India's HDI rank was 130 of 193 in UNDP's 2025 report.
- Challenges: low learning levels, low public health spending, malnutrition, skill mismatch.
- Policies: NEP 2020, Ayushman Bharat (2018), POSHAN, Skill India.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's high literacy but drug abuse and youth emigration reduce its human-capital dividend.

*Conclusion:* Investing in health and education is investing in growth itself.

**Q5.** The Punjab economy has failed to remain competitive with many other developed states. Do you agree? Justify. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Punjab economy is asked every year.

*Intro:* Punjab was India's richest state in per-capita terms in the 1980s but has slipped behind several states.

*Points:*
- Growth has lagged: real GSDP growth 6.1% in 2025-26 (Punjab Economic Survey), roughly near or below the national rate in many years.
- Per capita income ₹2,30,523 (2025-26) is well below Haryana, Gujarat, Maharashtra, Karnataka and Tamil Nadu.
- Agriculture-heavy (about 23% of GSVA) with stagnant wheat–paddy yields; industry (29%) dominated by small units.
- Fiscal stress: high debt and committed spending on salaries, pensions, interest and power subsidy leave little for capital spending.
- Disadvantages: landlocked border state, closed Pakistan trade route, insurgency legacy, earlier tax incentives to hill states (2003).
- Brain drain and emigration; drug abuse among youth.
- Strengths: good infrastructure, high literacy, entrepreneurial diaspora, strong agro base, Mohali IT hub.
- Agree with qualification: competitiveness can be regained via agro-processing, logistics, MSME upgrading, fiscal reform and skills.

*Punjab:*
- Ludhiana (bicycles, hosiery, auto parts), Jalandhar (sports goods), Mandi Gobindgarh (steel) need cluster modernisation.

*Conclusion:* Punjab has lost competitiveness, but its human and entrepreneurial base allows a comeback with reform.

*Conclusion:* Technology has reduced leakages, but targeting and nutrition quality remain issues.

**Q6(b).** What are buffer norms? What are its objectives? Is any change in buffer norms in pipeline? *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Buffer stocks and food security are a syllabus item.

*Intro:* Buffer norms are the minimum food-grain stocks the Centre must hold in the central pool at the start of each quarter.

*Points:*
- Current norms (2015): about 21.41 million tonnes on 1 January, 21.04 MT on 1 April, 41.12 MT on 1 July and 30.77 MT on 1 October (including strategic reserve).
- Objectives: meet PDS and welfare needs, emergencies (droughts, pandemics), price stabilisation through open-market sales.
- Actual stocks have often been far above norms, raising storage costs and wastage.
- Changes discussed: periodic review of norms, including pulses/other crops, more use of OMSS to release excess and private storage.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's storage capacity (silos, covered godowns) is stretched every season due to excess wheat and paddy stocks.

*Conclusion:* Norms should reflect current consumption and be managed to cut costly excess stocks.

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Liberalisation and inequality is a syllabus item.

*Points:*

**Q8.** Do you think GST has facilitated the Indian business environment? Discuss. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* GST, especially after the 2025 rate rationalisation, remains live.

*Intro:* GST (from 1 July 2017, via the 101st Amendment) replaced a web of central and state indirect taxes.

*Points:*
- Facilitation: one national market, end of check-post queues (e-way bill), input-tax credit removing cascading, single registration.
- Formalisation: more registered taxpayers, digital invoices and e-invoicing.
- Revenue: gross GST collections were about ₹22 lakh crore in 2024-25.
- GST Council as a model of cooperative federalism.
- Problems at start: multiple rates, frequent changes, inverted duty structure, complex returns, refund delays — hurting MSMEs.
- Reform: rate structure simplified in September 2025 to mainly two slabs (5% and 18%) plus a higher rate for sin/luxury goods.
- Continuing issues: petroleum and electricity outside GST, compliance burden, state revenue concerns after compensation ended (2022).

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's trading and MSME sectors benefit from smooth inter-state movement; the state lost revenue autonomy and seeks compensation for its consumption-based losses.

*Conclusion:* GST has improved the business environment, especially after simplification; further rationalisation and easier compliance will complete the reform.

**Q9.** What is China Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC)? What are its implications on security of India? *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* CPEC and China–Pakistan collusion are recurring security themes.

*Intro:* CPEC is the flagship project of China's Belt and Road Initiative, launched in 2015, linking Kashgar (Xinjiang) to Gwadar port in Pakistan through roads, railways, pipelines, power plants and special economic zones.

*Points:*
- Investment pledges grew from about USD 46 billion to around USD 60 billion.
- Passes through Gilgit-Baltistan in Pakistan-occupied Kashmir — violates India's sovereignty; India has refused to join BRI.
- Gwadar may give China a naval/strategic foothold near the Strait of Hormuz — part of 'string of pearls' concerns.
- Deepens China–Pakistan military collusion — two-front threat.
- Chinese presence and investments make PoK issue more complex; China shields Pakistan-based terrorists at the UN.
- Counter-view: Pakistan's debt stress and attacks on Chinese workers slow CPEC.
- India's response: Chabahar port (Iran), INSTC, IMEC corridor (2023), strengthening border infrastructure and maritime presence.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab is a frontline state on the western border; closer China–Pakistan ties raise drone, narcotics and weapons risks.

*Conclusion:* CPEC is as much strategic as economic; India must counter with connectivity diplomacy and military preparedness.

**Q10.** Write a short note on recent initiatives of Comprehensive Integrated Border Management System. Also mention the challenges linked with border management for India particularly in view of the long porous border India shares with Pakistan, Bangladesh and China. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Border management is a named syllabus item and vital for Punjab.

*Intro:* CIBMS integrates manpower, sensors, networks and command-and-control to plug gaps where physical fencing is not possible.

*Points:*
- Uses thermal imagers, radars, underground sensors, fibre-optic sensors, CCTV and a common command centre.
- Pilot 'smart fence' stretches were inaugurated in Jammu (2018); Project BOLD-QIT covered the riverine Brahmaputra stretch in Dhubri, Assam (2019).
- Pakistan border: infiltration, cross-border firing, tunnels, drones dropping drugs and arms, riverine gaps.
- Bangladesh border: porous, densely populated, riverine and char areas, illegal migration, cattle and fake-currency smuggling, unfenced stretches.
- China border (LAC): undefined boundary, difficult terrain, Chinese infrastructure advantage; standoffs (Galwan 2020).
- Other challenges: coordination among BSF/ITBP/Army/state police, weather, land acquisition for fencing, local population's livelihoods.
- Measures: border infrastructure (Vibrant Villages Programme, 2023), anti-drone systems, intelligence integration.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab has a 553 km international border with Pakistan; BSF's jurisdiction extended to 50 km in 2021; anti-drone systems deployed against narco-drones.

*Conclusion:* Technology-led integrated management, with local community support, is essential for porous borders.

**Q14.** Study the data given in the following multiple bar chart which shows the production, export and import (in million kg) of various commodities by Country X and answer the questions given below.
