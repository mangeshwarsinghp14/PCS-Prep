**Q1.** Mention two traditional water conservation methods practised in Punjab. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Water conservation is a Punjab favourite; short factual items on traditional practices may recur.

*Intro:* Before tubewells, Punjab's villages relied on community structures that stored rain and runoff and recharged groundwater.

*Points:*
- Village ponds (chhapparh/toba): every village had one or more ponds that collected rainwater and runoff, recharged aquifers and served cattle; managed by the panchayat/community.
- Baolis (step-wells): stepped wells reaching groundwater, often attached to gurdwaras and serais (e.g. Baoli Sahib at Goindwal Sahib).
- Also: open wells with Persian wheel (harat), and kacha embankments/bunds in the Kandi area to hold runoff from choes.
- Relevance today: revival of village ponds (e.g. Seechewal/Thapar models), roof-top harvesting and recharge pits.

*Punjab:*
- Baoli Sahib, Goindwal; village ponds revived under MGNREGA and Amrit Sarovar (2022).

*Conclusion:* Reviving these community systems can complement canals and reduce pressure on groundwater.

**Q2.** What measures can be taken in terms of sustainable development to reduce vulnerability to natural hazards? *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Disaster risk reduction is a named node; Punjab's 2023 and 2025 floods make it highly likely.

*Intro:* Vulnerability is reduced when development itself is risk-informed — the core idea of the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction (2015–2030).

*Points:*
- Risk-sensitive land use: no construction in flood plains, river beds and active-fault/landslide zones; flood-zone mapping.
- Resilient infrastructure: building codes, earthquake-resistant design, Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure (India-led, 2019).
- Ecosystem-based approaches: wetlands, mangroves, forests and riparian buffers absorb floods and storm surges.
- Climate adaptation: heat-action plans, drought-resistant crops, water harvesting.
- Early warning systems and last-mile communication (Common Alerting Protocol, cell broadcast).
- Social protection: crop insurance (PMFBY), livelihood diversification, SHG savings — reduce economic vulnerability.
- Community-based disaster management, Aapda Mitra volunteers, school safety; mainstream DRR in all schemes and budgets (Finance Commission DRR windows).

*Punjab:*
- Punjab: strengthening dhussi bundhs, desilting choes, ban on illegal sand mining in river beds, flood zoning of Sutlej–Beas–Ghaggar plains.

*Conclusion:* Sustainable development and disaster risk reduction are the same agenda: build so that hazards do not become disasters.

**Q3.** Explain petroplants with examples. What is their role in sustainable development? *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Fairly niche topic; bioenergy may recur in a different form (CBG, ethanol).

*Intro:* Petroplants (petro-crops) are plants whose latex or seeds contain hydrocarbons or oils that can be converted into fuel substitutes for petroleum; the idea was popularised by Nobel laureate Melvin Calvin.

*Points:*
- Latex-yielding: Euphorbia lathyris, Euphorbia tirucalli, Calotropis procera (aak), Copaifera langsdorfii (the 'diesel tree' of Brazil).
- Oil-seed bio-diesel plants: Jatropha curcas, Pongamia pinnata (karanja), Simarouba.
- Grow on wasteland and dry, saline areas — do not compete with food crops.
- Renewable and nearly carbon-neutral fuel; reduces oil imports and emissions.
- Rural employment, land reclamation and soil conservation.
- Policy: National Policy on Biofuels 2018 (amended 2022); India reached 20% ethanol blending in petrol in 2025.
- Limits: low yields, high processing cost; the Jatropha mission of the 2000s largely failed.

*Punjab:*
- Calotropis grows wild in Punjab's sandy south-west; Punjab's bigger bioenergy opportunity is paddy straw to compressed biogas (Verbio CBG plant, Lehragaga, Sangrur).

*Conclusion:* Petroplants are a useful supplement, not a substitute, for fossil fuels — viable when grown on degraded land with assured buy-back.

**Q4.** What are the possible impacts of Climate Change on agriculture and water resources in the State of Punjab? *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Climate impact on Punjab farming/water is a top state issue; asked in many forms.

*Intro:* Punjab's intensive, irrigation-dependent farming makes it highly sensitive to rising temperatures and changing rainfall.

*Points:*
- Heat stress: terminal heat in March reduces wheat grain filling (2022 heatwave cut yields).
- Erratic monsoon: dry spells raise irrigation demand; extreme rain causes floods (July 2023; Aug–Sept 2025 when all 23 districts were declared flood-affected).
- Water: higher evapotranspiration and pumping; uncertain snow- and glacier-fed inflows to Bhakra and Pong.
- Groundwater recharge falls with intense, short rains; extraction already about 152% (2025-26 state assessment).
- Pests and diseases: new pest outbreaks (whitefly, pink bollworm, locust 2020).
- Soil: loss of organic matter, salinity in south-west.
- Livestock heat stress lowers milk yield.
- Adaptation: heat-tolerant varieties (PAU), direct-seeded rice, crop diversification, micro-irrigation, weather-based advisories, crop insurance, Punjab State Action Plan on Climate Change.

*Punjab:*
- Floods of 2023 and 2025 along Sutlej, Beas, Ravi and Ghaggar; March 2022 heatwave impact on wheat.

*Conclusion:* Climate change multiplies Punjab's existing water and monoculture stress; adaptation must be built into farm policy now.

**Q5.** What is the objective of National Quantum Mission? Mention two practical uses of quantum technology for India. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Frontier technologies (quantum, AI, semiconductors) are asked every year in S&T.

*Intro:* The National Quantum Mission (NQM), approved by the Union Cabinet in April 2023 with an outlay of ₹6,003.65 crore for 2023-24 to 2030-31, is run by the Department of Science and Technology.

*Points:*
- Objective: seed and scale up R&D and build an innovation ecosystem in quantum technology.
- Targets: quantum computers with 50–1,000 physical qubits in about 8 years; satellite-based secure quantum communication over 2,000 km between ground stations; multi-node quantum networks; magnetometers and atomic clocks for precision sensing; quantum materials.

- Use 1 – Secure communication: Quantum Key Distribution makes eavesdropping detectable — for defence, banking and government networks.
- Use 2 – Quantum sensing: ultra-precise clocks and magnetometers for GPS-independent navigation, mineral exploration and medical imaging.
- Also: quantum computing for drug discovery, new materials, weather and logistics optimisation.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's universities (IISER Mohali, Punjab University) can join the quantum research ecosystem.

*Conclusion:* NQM aims to make India one of the leading quantum nations and secure its digital future.

**Q6.** UPI is seen as India's IT achievement that affects daily life. Explain what UPI is and write two benefits and one major cyber risk. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Digital public infrastructure is a stable S&T theme; UPI frauds keep it topical.

*Intro:* Unified Payments Interface (UPI), launched by NPCI in April 2016, is a real-time, interoperable payment system that links multiple bank accounts to a mobile app using a virtual payment address or QR code.

*Points:*
- Scale: 18,586.6 crore transactions worth ₹260.56 lakh crore in FY 2024-25, and about 24,162 crore transactions worth ₹314.23 lakh crore in FY 2025-26 (Government data).
- Benefit 1 – Convenience and speed: instant 24×7 transfers, free for users, works across banks and apps.
- Benefit 2 – Inclusion and formalisation: small vendors accept QR payments; UPI Lite and UPI 123PAY for feature phones; creates digital credit history; used abroad in Bhutan, Singapore, UAE, France, Nepal, Sri Lanka and others.
- Major cyber risk – Fraud through social engineering: fake collect requests, phishing links, QR code scams, screen-sharing apps and SIM-swap lead to unauthorised debits.
- Safeguards: UPI PIN never needed to receive money; transaction limits, device binding, fraud-risk monitoring, cybercrime helpline 1930.

*Punjab:*
- Widely used by Punjab's mandi traders, kirana shops and dhabas; cyber police stations handle UPI fraud complaints.

*Conclusion:* UPI is a global model of digital public infrastructure; user awareness is the key defence against fraud.

*Points:*

- Aims to raise India's low R&D spending (under 1% of GDP).

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's universities (PAU, PU, GNDU, Punjabi University) can access ANRF grants for agri-tech and industry-linked research.

*Conclusion:* ANRF can become India's NSF-style engine if it widens the research base and genuinely draws in private investment.

*Why it matters:* Municipal services (waste, sanitation) are a named problem-solving node; Punjab's dumpsites are a live issue.

**Q11.** Discuss the causes and impact of floods in districts situated along the Sutlej and Beas rivers. What structural and non-structural measures can be adopted to mitigate this hazard? *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Repeated floods (2019, 2023, 2025) make this the most likely Punjab disaster question.

*Intro:* Districts along the Sutlej and Beas (Rupnagar, Ludhiana, Jalandhar, Kapurthala, Ferozepur, Fazilka, Hoshiarpur, Gurdaspur, Tarn Taran) face recurring floods; in Aug–Sept 2025 all 23 districts of Punjab were declared flood-affected.

*Points:*
- Causes – extreme rainfall and cloudbursts in Himachal catchments; heavy local rain.
- Large releases from Bhakra and Pong dams when reservoirs fill (reservoir operation and rule-curve issues).
- Breaches of weak earthen embankments (dhussi bundhs); silted river beds and choes.
- Encroachment of flood plains for farming and housing; illegal sand mining weakens banks.
- Poor drainage and blocked natural drains; backflow from Harike.
- Impact – loss of lives and livestock; standing paddy damaged (over 4 lakh acres submerged by early Sept 2025); houses, roads and power lines destroyed; silt deposition on farmland; health risks; debt for farmers.

- Non-structural: flood-plain zoning and ban on construction/mining in river beds; real-time inflow forecasting and better dam release coordination (BBMB–state–IMD); early warnings; crop insurance and quick compensation; community preparedness and SDRF training; restoration of wetlands.

*Punjab:*
- 2025 floods: about 1,400 villages hit and over 4 lakh acres submerged by 2 Sept 2025 (Tribune); Centre announced ₹1,600 crore assistance.

*Conclusion:* Floods in Punjab are partly natural, partly man-made; integrated basin management with dam operation reform is essential.

**Q12.** What are the causes of depletion of ground water in Punjab? Suggest the mitigation strategy to overcome it. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Groundwater depletion is the single most repeated Punjab question across papers.

*Intro:* Punjab is the most groundwater-stressed state: the latest state assessment (2025-26) shows extraction of about 26.3 BCM against about 17.3 BCM available, a stage of extraction of about 152%, with 110 of 153 blocks over-exploited.

*Points:*
- Causes – paddy cultivation on over 30 lakh ha in summer; about 3,000–5,000 litres of water per kg of rice.
- Free electricity for farm tubewells (since 1997) removes the cost of pumping.
- Assured MSP procurement of wheat and paddy discourages diversification.
- Decline of canal irrigation and maintenance; tail-ends get little water.
- Over 14 lakh tubewells; shift to deep submersible pumps.

- Mitigation – crop diversification (maize, cotton, pulses, basmati) with assured procurement and income support.
- Water-saving techniques: Direct Seeded Rice, short-duration varieties (PR-126), laser land levelling, drip and sprinkler, tensiometers.
- Paddy Water Act (Punjab Preservation of Subsoil Water Act 2009) to delay transplanting; 'Pani Bachao Paisa Kamao' type direct benefit for saving power.
- Canal revival and lining, conjunctive use; recharge structures, pond renovation, rooftop harvesting.
- Regulation: Punjab Water Resources (Management and Regulation) Act 2020; metering; Atal Bhujal-type community water budgeting.

*Punjab:*
- State reports reviving over 100 defunct canals and the Kandi canal; groundwater improved in many blocks (2025).

*Conclusion:* Without changing what Punjab grows and how it prices power, recharge alone cannot save the aquifers.

**Q13.** What is the Seechewal Model which transformed a sewage-filled drain back into a rejuvenated flowing river? *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Punjab success stories in water/environment management are favoured by PPSC.

*Intro:* The Seechewal Model refers to the community-led cleaning of the Kali Bein — a rivulet of about 160 km in Hoshiarpur and Kapurthala districts associated with Guru Nanak Dev Ji at Sultanpur Lodhi — started in 2000 by environmentalist Sant Balbir Singh Seechewal.

*Points:*
- Problem: the Bein had become a choked drain carrying sewage from villages and towns along its course, choked with weeds and silt.
- Kar sewa (voluntary labour): thousands of volunteers desilted the bed, removed water hyacinth and built banks and paths — with community money, not government funds.
- Village sewage system: underground sewer pipes in Seechewal village; wastewater passes through a series of settling wells/ponds where solids settle and water is naturally treated.
- Treated water reused for irrigation through underground pipes — no untreated discharge into the river.
- Revival of flow: springs reopened; river now flows with clean water; tree plantation along banks.
- Social mobilisation through religious motivation (Guru Nanak's link) and panchayats.
- Recognition: model replicated in many Punjab villages for pond cleaning; praised nationally; Seechewal was later elected to the Rajya Sabha (2022).
- Lessons: low-cost decentralised treatment, community ownership, reuse of wastewater for farms, faith-based environmental action.

*Punjab:*
- Kali Bein, Sultanpur Lodhi (Kapurthala); village pond renovation under the Seechewal and Thapar models across Punjab.

*Conclusion:* The Seechewal Model shows that people's participation and simple technology can rejuvenate rivers where big projects fail.

*Diagram:* Flow diagram: household sewage → pipes → settling well 1 → well 2 → pond → treated water → irrigation channels.

**Q15.** Compare somatic gene therapy, gene editing, and germline editing. Explain the expected benefits and discuss the ethical issues involved with such practices. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Genetic engineering is a named node; India's CAR-T and gene therapy milestones keep it topical.

*Intro:* Modern biotechnology allows correction of disease-causing genes, but the type of cell targeted decides whether changes stay with one person or pass to future generations.

*Points:*
- Somatic gene therapy: adds or replaces a gene in body (non-reproductive) cells; effects limited to the patient (e.g. CAR-T cell therapy; India's NexCAR19 approved in 2023; haemophilia gene-therapy trial at CMC Vellore).
- Gene editing: precise change in DNA using tools like CRISPR-Cas9, TALENs, base and prime editing; may be somatic (e.g. Casgevy for sickle-cell disease, approved in UK and US in 2023) or germline.
- Germline editing: changes in eggs, sperm or embryos — heritable to all future generations; prohibited for clinical use in India (ICMR guidelines on gene therapy, 2019) and most countries.
- Benefits: cure for monogenic diseases (sickle-cell, thalassaemia, haemophilia), cancers; reduce disease burden; agriculture and research uses.
- Ethical issues – safety: off-target effects, unknown long-term risks.
- Consent: future generations cannot consent to germline changes.
- Equity: very high cost (crores per patient) widens inequality.
- 'Designer babies' and eugenics fears; He Jiankui's gene-edited babies (China, 2018) were condemned worldwide.
- Regulation: strict oversight, ethics committees, transparency, public dialogue.

*Punjab:*
- Thalassaemia is common in Punjab; affordable somatic therapies could transform care.

*Conclusion:* Somatic therapies are acceptable with safeguards; germline editing should stay off-limits until safety and social consensus exist.

*Diagram:* Comparison table: target cells, heritability, examples, regulation in India.

**Q16.** What is CRISPR-Cas9 technology? How can it contribute towards crop improvement? *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Genome-edited crops (India's first two rice varieties, May 2025) make this a likely repeat for agri-biotech.

*Intro:* CRISPR-Cas9 is a genome-editing tool adapted from a bacterial immune system: a guide RNA leads the Cas9 enzyme to a specific DNA sequence where it makes a cut, and the cell's repair process disables or changes the gene. Its developers, Jennifer Doudna and Emmanuelle Charpentier, won the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

*Points:*
- Precise, cheap and fast compared with conventional breeding and transgenic GM methods.
