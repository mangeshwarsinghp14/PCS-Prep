**QA1.** Social Media as a Tool of Empowerment or Surveillance: A Governance Paradox. *(50 marks, 800 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Technology-and-governance essays dominated this paper (all of Part A); digital themes will recur.

*Intro:* Open with a contrast: the same smartphone that lets a farmer livestream a protest also lets the state or a platform map his network.

*Points:*
- Define the paradox: social media empowers voice, but creates data that can be used to watch and control.
- Empowerment: free expression, citizen journalism, grievance redress (e-governance handles on X), mobilisation (#MeToo, farm protests), small-business marketing.
- Surveillance: state monitoring, internet shutdowns, spyware controversies, platform data harvesting and algorithmic profiling.
- Harms beyond surveillance: misinformation, hate speech, deepfakes, polarisation, cyberbullying.
- Legal framework: Art. 19(1)(a) with reasonable restrictions, Puttaswamy (2017) privacy test, IT Rules 2021, DPDP Act 2023.
- Governance balance: transparency in takedown orders, independent oversight, proportional surveillance with judicial warrants, platform accountability.
- Citizen side: digital literacy and responsible use.
- Way forward: rights-based digital governance — empowerment as rule, surveillance as narrow exception.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's diaspora-linked social media shaped the 2020–21 farm movement; same space used for gangster propaganda and fake news.

*Conclusion:* Conclude: the paradox is resolved not by less technology but by more accountability — of states, platforms and users.

**QA2.** Artificial Intelligence: A Tool of Human Progress or Challenge to Human Values? *(50 marks, 800 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* AI appears in GS-I, GS-IV and Essay in this exam — the single most visible 2026 theme.

*Intro:* Start with an anecdote: an AI model detecting crop disease from a photo in a Punjab village vs. a deepfake video spreading before an election.

*Points:*
- Progress: healthcare diagnostics, precision agriculture, education in mother tongue, weather forecasting, governance efficiency.
- Economic: productivity growth; India's IndiaAI Mission (2024) for compute, models and datasets.
- Value challenges: bias and discrimination, privacy, job displacement, deepfakes and misinformation, autonomy and manipulation, lethal autonomous weapons.
- Philosophical: what makes us human — empathy, judgment, responsibility — cannot be outsourced.
- Governance: human-in-the-loop, explainability, accountability; India AI Governance Guidelines (2025), global efforts (EU AI Act).
- Skilling and social safety nets to share gains.
- Gandhian test: does this technology help the last person?
- Synthesis: AI is a mirror and multiplier of human values — the choice is ours.

*Punjab:*
- AI-based pest alerts and soil advisories for Punjab farmers; risk of deepfakes in Punjab politics.

*Conclusion:* Conclude: AI is a tool; whether it is progress or threat depends on the values we encode and the institutions we build.

**QA3.** Digital innovation in administration must be tempered by ethical vigilance to prevent societal divides. *(50 marks, 800 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Digital governance and exclusion is a stable theme; likely to recur in some form.

*Intro:* Open with an elderly widow denied pension because her fingerprints failed on an e-PoS machine.

*Points:*
- Gains of digital administration: DBT savings, speed, transparency, less corruption (JAM trinity, DigiLocker, UPI).
- Divides: access (devices, internet), skills, gender and rural–urban gaps, language barriers, disability.
- Exclusion errors: biometric failures, data mismatch, online-only processes.
- Ethical risks: privacy, surveillance, algorithmic bias, lack of grievance redress.
- Ethical vigilance means: assisted/offline options, consent, data protection (DPDP 2023), human override, social audits.
- Inclusive design: vernacular interfaces, Common Service Centres, Sewa Kendras, BharatNet.
- Officers' role: empathy and discretion within rules.
- Measure success by inclusion, not just digitisation.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab Sewa Kendras and online land records — need help-desks for digitally weak citizens.

*Conclusion:* Conclude: digital India must remain human India — technology should close gaps, not create new ones.

**QA4.** Can technology alone solve India's environmental crises, or is behavioural change more crucial? *(50 marks, 800 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Environment–behaviour essays (Mission LiFE) are a recurring type.

*Intro:* Start with Punjab's stubble: subsidised Happy Seeders exist, yet fires continue — technology without behaviour change fails.

*Points:*
- Crises: air pollution, water depletion, waste, climate change, biodiversity loss.
- Technology's role: renewables (solar), EVs, waste-to-energy, micro-irrigation, CRISPR crops, carbon capture.
- Limits of technology: rebound effect, cost, access, e-waste, needs adoption.
- Behaviour: consumption patterns, segregation of waste, water use, travel choices; Mission LiFE (2021) idea of 'pro-planet people'.
- Institutions and incentives link both — prices, regulations, nudges (e.g. Swachh Bharat behaviour campaign).
- Indian traditions of restraint (Gandhi, Guru Nanak's 'Pavan Guru, Pani Pita, Mata Dharat Mahat').
- Synergy: technology makes sustainable choices easy; behaviour makes technology work.
- Policy mix: green tech + nudges + community action.

*Punjab:*
- Paddy straw management, water-saving DSR adoption, Seechewal model of community river cleaning.

*Conclusion:* Conclude: technology is the engine, behaviour the steering — India needs both to reach a sustainable future.

**QB1.** In an age of constant connectivity, silence has become the rarest form of freedom. *(50 marks, 800 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Abstract/philosophical essays now form a full part (Part B) — this style will continue.

*Intro:* Open with an image: notifications at midnight; the ancient yogi's maun vrat as a form of freedom.

*Points:*
- Meaning: silence as inner space for thought, freedom from noise and manipulation.
- Hyper-connectivity: attention economy, information overload, FOMO, anxiety, loss of deep work.
- Silence as freedom: to think independently, resist propaganda, creativity and reflection.
- Spiritual traditions: meditation, Gurbani's 'sunn samadhi', Gandhi's weekly day of silence.
- Silence vs silencing: voluntary silence is freedom; forced silence (censorship) is oppression.
- Society: digital detox, right to disconnect debates, mindful technology use for students and officials.
- Leadership: reflective decision-making needs quiet time.
- Balance: connectivity as tool, silence as anchor.

*Punjab:*
- Gurbani's emphasis on naam simran and inner stillness; Punjab youth and screen addiction.

*Conclusion:* Conclude: in a noisy age, choosing silence is choosing to think — and thinking is the root of freedom.

**QB2.** The greatest danger to progress is not resistance to change, but unthinking acceptance of it. *(50 marks, 800 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Philosophical Part B style; theme of critical thinking about change is evergreen.

*Intro:* Begin with the Green Revolution: welcomed without questioning — and its unforeseen costs in Punjab's soil and water.

*Points:*
- Progress requires change, but change is not automatically progress.
- Resistance has value: it tests ideas (environmental movements, civil liberties).
- Unthinking acceptance: blind adoption of technology, fashion, ideologies, policies without evaluation.
- Examples: plastic, chemical overuse, social media addiction, financial bubbles.
- Scientific temper (Art. 51A(h)): question, test, adapt.
- Role of institutions: impact assessments, pilot testing, public consultation.
- Individual level: critical thinking, informed choices.
- Ideal: reflective acceptance — embrace change after scrutiny.

*Punjab:*
- Green Revolution in Punjab — success and its ecological debt.

*Conclusion:* Conclude: progress belongs to societies that change thoughtfully, not to those that refuse or blindly follow change.

**QB3.** When speed becomes a virtue, reflection turns into a liability. *(50 marks, 800 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Similar abstract themes recur, but this exact framing is unlikely to repeat.

*Intro:* Open with 'instant noodles, instant news, instant judgments' — and the cost of a hasty decision.

*Points:*
- Cult of speed: instant communication, quick commerce, 24×7 news, fast policy.
- Benefits of speed: efficiency, disaster response, competitiveness.
- Costs: errors, shallow thinking, burnout, misinformation spreading faster than facts.
- Reflection: deliberation in democracy, judicial reasoning, scientific peer review.
- Governance examples: hurried laws struck down or withdrawn; well-deliberated reforms last.
- Ethics: considered judgment, empathy need time.
- Balance: 'think slow, act fast' — reflection first, speed in execution.
- Education: train for depth, not just speed.

*Punjab:*
- Hasty withdrawal/revisions of policies vs Punjab's long consultations on water sharing.

*Conclusion:* Conclude: speed should serve wisdom, not replace it.

**QB4.** Civilisations advance not merely by what they create, but by what they choose to restrain. *(50 marks, 800 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Restraint/ethics-of-power themes link to AI, climate and nuclear issues — likely in new wording.

*Intro:* Open with Ashoka after Kalinga — the strongest king choosing restraint.

*Points:*
- Creation: science, technology, art, institutions mark progress.
- Restraint: moral limits on power and desire — rule of law, rights, constitutionalism.
- Examples: nuclear non-use norms, bans on chemical weapons, germline editing restraint, AI safety.
- Environmental restraint: sustainable consumption, Mission LiFE.
- Personal: self-control, Gandhian non-violence, Sikh ideal of 'sanjam'.
- Failures of non-restraint: colonialism, wars, ecological collapse.
- Institutions of restraint: constitution, independent judiciary, free press.
- Future: wisdom to say 'no' to harmful innovations.

*Punjab:*
- Sikh Gurus' ideals of restraint and service; Punjab's need to restrain groundwater overuse.

*Conclusion:* Conclude: the true measure of a civilisation is the power it holds and wisely declines to use.

**QC1.** True empowerment of the marginalized lies not in policies alone, but in empathetic and decisive administrative action. *(50 marks, 800 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Governance-for-the-poor essays are a staple; fits the SDM role directly.

*Intro:* Start with an SDM who visits a Dalit hamlet and fixes a long-pending land issue in a day — while the scheme existed for years.

*Points:*
- Policy framework: reservations, welfare schemes, rights-based laws (MGNREGA, NFSA, RTE, FRA).
- Implementation gap: leakages, exclusion, apathy, red tape.
- Empathy: understanding lived realities, dignity in service, foundational values of civil service.
- Decisiveness: timely action, use of discretion, courage against vested interests.
- Examples: transformative officers (district-level innovations), Aspirational Districts programme.
- Tools: grievance camps, social audits, door-step delivery, convergence.
- Accountability: Right to Service laws, citizen charters.
- Empowerment = rights + capabilities + voice (Amartya Sen).

*Punjab:*
- Punjab SC population (about 32%) and landless labourers; Sewa Kendras; panchayat common land for Dalits.

*Conclusion:* Conclude: policies give the map, but administrators walk the road — empathy and courage turn entitlements into empowerment.

**QC2.** India's growth story is service-led rather than manufacturing-led. Is this sustainable for long-term employment generation? *(50 marks, 800 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Growth–jobs debate is central in GS-III too; very likely again.

*Intro:* Open with the paradox: IT exports boom while millions of youth seek factory or government jobs.

*Points:*
- Facts: services contribute over half of GVA; manufacturing share in GDP stagnant around 15–17%.
- Strengths of service-led growth: IT/ITES, finance, tourism, high productivity, exports; GCCs.
- Limits: services employ skilled workers; low absorption of low-skilled labour moving out of agriculture.
- East Asian path: labour-intensive manufacturing created mass jobs.
- Indian efforts: Make in India, PLI schemes, Labour Codes, logistics (PM GatiShakti).
- Emerging jobs: gig/platform economy — numbers but insecurity.
- Need: both — labour-intensive manufacturing (textiles, food processing, electronics assembly) + skill upgrading for services.
- Human capital, women's participation, MSME credit.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's youth migration abroad; industrial clusters (Ludhiana) vs IT in Mohali.

*Conclusion:* Conclude: service-led growth is necessary but not sufficient; jobs for the many need manufacturing and skills.

**QC3.** "Punjab's fiscal health is strained by subsidies, debt, and limited revenue generation." Critically assess whether current fiscal policies are sustainable, and suggest reforms that balance welfare needs with long-term economic stability. *(50 marks, 800 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Punjab fiscal stress appears in Essay and GS-IV case; certain to remain topical till 2029.

*Intro:* Open with the numbers: outstanding liabilities about 45% of GSDP (2026-27 BE) — among the highest in India.

*Points:*
- Causes: power subsidy, rising salary and pension bill, interest payments; legacy of militancy-era loans.
- Weak revenue: stagnant agriculture taxes, GST loss after compensation ended, low own-tax effort, industrial migration.
- New welfare schemes add to committed spending.
- Sustainability test: revenue deficit, interest-to-revenue ratio, debt trajectory; low capex.
- Reforms – revenue: GST compliance, excise and mining auctions, property tax, land monetisation.
- Reforms – expenditure: targeted subsidies (DBT), pension reform, rationalised schemes, outcome budgeting.
- Growth: crop diversification, food processing, MSMEs, tourism, ease of doing business.
- Centre–state: fair finance commission devolution, special support for border state; transparent fiscal reporting.

*Punjab:*
- PRS: fiscal deficit target 4.1% of GSDP in 2026-27; actual 4.7% in 2024-25.

*Conclusion:* Conclude: welfare is sustainable only on a growing economy — Punjab needs fiscal courage today to protect its people tomorrow.

**QC4.** Without inclusive and quality development of human capital, the demographic dividend risks becoming a demographic burden. *(50 marks, 800 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Demographic dividend/skills essays are frequent in UPSC and PPSC.

*Intro:* Open with India's median age (under 30) and the clock ticking — the dividend window lasts only a few decades.

*Points:*
- Demographic dividend: rising working-age share can boost growth.
- Requirements: health, nutrition, education, skills, jobs.
- Gaps: learning poverty (ASER), stunting (NFHS-5: 35.5%), low female labour participation, skill mismatch.
- Risk: unemployed youth, migration, drug abuse, social unrest — burden instead of dividend.
- Policies: NEP 2020, Skill India, PM Internship Scheme, Poshan Abhiyaan, Ayushman Bharat.
- Inclusion: women, SC/ST, rural youth, persons with disabilities.
- Quality: teacher training, foundational literacy, apprenticeships, digital skills.
- States diverge: southern states ageing, northern states young — tailored strategies.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab: youth out-migration via study visas, drug problem, low female workforce participation.

*Conclusion:* Conclude: people become an asset only when invested in — India must spend on human capital as on infrastructure.
