**Q1.** "The Industrial Revolution was a mixed blessing." Comment. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* World history from the 18th century is a syllabus item; Industrial Revolution is a staple.

*Intro:* The Industrial Revolution (Britain, c. 1760–1840, then Europe, USA, Japan) shifted production from hand tools to machines and factories.

*Points:*
- Blessings: huge rise in productivity and output; cheaper goods; steam power, railways and steamships connected the world.
- Urbanisation, new middle and working classes, later rise in living standards, education and science.
- Political effects: growth of democracy, trade unions, socialism and welfare laws (Factory Acts from 1833).
- Curses: child and women labour, long hours, slums, disease, pollution (e.g., 19th-century Manchester).
- Alienation and inequality — the basis of Marx's critique.
- Colonialism: search for raw materials and markets led to empire; India's handicrafts declined (de-industrialisation) and India became a raw-cotton supplier.
- Long-term environmental cost: fossil-fuel use began the carbon emissions behind today's climate change.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's later industrial growth (Ludhiana hosiery, Batala foundries) shows the same mix of jobs and pollution (Buddha Nullah).

*Conclusion:* It was a mixed blessing: it created modern prosperity but at high human, colonial and ecological cost — lessons for sustainable industrialisation today.

**Q2(b).** Write short note on: Zenith of Mughal painting under Jahangir. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Mughal art is a regular culture question.

*Intro:* Mughal painting reached its peak under Jahangir (1605–27), himself a keen connoisseur.

*Points:*
- Shift from large group manuscripts (Akbar) to single-artist works, portraits and albums (muraqqa).
- Naturalism: Ustad Mansur's birds and animals (e.g., Siberian crane, zebra); flowers painted with scientific accuracy.
- Portraiture: Abul Hasan (titled Nadir-uz-Zaman) and Bishandas; allegorical portraits of the emperor with haloes.
- European influence: perspective, shading, Christian motifs from Jesuit gifts.
- Decorated borders (hashiya) and fine brushwork; Jahangir claimed he could identify each painter's hand.
- After him, under Shah Jahan, painting became formal and declined under Aurangzeb.

*Punjab:*
- Later Pahari and Sikh-court painting (Lahore under Ranjit Singh) drew on the Mughal tradition.

*Conclusion:* Jahangir's reign marks the zenith of Mughal painting for its realism and refinement.

**Q3.** Describe the contribution of revolutionary activities outside India with special reference to Ghadar Party and Udham Singh. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Ghadar movement and Udham Singh are Punjab-centric favourites of PPSC.

*Intro:* Revolutionaries abroad kept the freedom struggle alive internationally and inspired youth at home.

*Points:*
- Early centres: India House, London (Shyamji Krishna Varma, 1905); Madan Lal Dhingra's assassination of Curzon Wyllie (1909); Paris and Berlin groups.
- Ghadar Party: founded in 1913 on the US Pacific coast (Sohan Singh Bhakna president, Lala Har Dayal key ideologue); weekly 'Ghadar' from San Francisco (from November 1913).
- Members were largely Punjabi Sikh farmers and workers in the US and Canada; secular, anti-colonial, revolutionary ideology.
- Komagata Maru episode (1914) — passengers refused entry in Canada and fired upon at Budge Budge on return — radicalised many.
- Planned uprising of February 1915 among Indian troops failed due to infiltration; Lahore Conspiracy trials; Kartar Singh Sarabha executed (1915).
- Berlin Committee and the Provisional Government of India at Kabul (1915).
- Udham Singh: Jallianwala Bagh survivor; shot Michael O'Dwyer, Lt Governor of Punjab in 1919, at Caxton Hall, London (13 March 1940); hanged in July 1940; called himself 'Ram Mohammad Singh Azad' to show unity.
- Contribution: internationalised India's cause, inspired Bhagat Singh's generation, showed secular revolutionary nationalism; limits — small numbers, poor organisation, informers.

*Punjab:*
- Desh Bhagat Yadgar Hall (Jalandhar) honours Ghadarites; Sunam (Sangrur) is Udham Singh's birthplace; Sarabha village (Ludhiana).

*Conclusion:* Revolutionaries abroad, especially Punjabi Ghadarites and Udham Singh, gave the movement courage and global visibility.

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Post-independence consolidation is a syllabus item; Nehru's legacy is debated.

*Intro:* As India's first Prime Minister (1947–64) Nehru shaped its political, economic and foreign-policy foundations.

*Points:*
- Democracy: parliamentary system, universal franchise, regular elections (1951–52), respect for institutions.

*Punjab:*
- Bhakra Nangal Dam and Chandigarh (planned by Le Corbusier, Nehru's vision of a modern city) are direct Punjab legacies.

*Conclusion:* Nehru laid the institutional foundation of modern India; later leaders corrected some economic choices but built on his framework.

**Q5.** Explain the rise and fall of Ranjit Singh's Empire. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Ranjit Singh and annexation of Punjab are asked every year.

*Intro:* Ranjit Singh built a powerful Sikh state (1799–1839) that collapsed within a decade of his death.

*Points:*
- Rise: Sukerchakia misl leader; capture of Lahore (1799); Maharaja (1801); Amritsar (1802).
- Treaty of Amritsar (1809) fixed the Sutlej as the boundary with the British; expansion to Multan (1818), Kashmir (1819), Peshawar (1834).
- Strengths: modern army (Fauj-i-Ain, European officers), efficient revenue, secular administration, diplomacy.
- Fall: no strong succession — Kharak Singh, Nau Nihal Singh and Sher Singh died in quick succession (1839–43); Dalip Singh a minor.
- Court intrigue: Dogra brothers (Dhian Singh, Gulab Singh), Sandhanwalias; army panchayats became powerful and unruly.
- British expansion: First Anglo-Sikh War (1845–46) — Treaty of Lahore (March 1846), Jalandhar Doab and Kashmir lost; Treaty of Bhairowal (December 1846).
- Second Anglo-Sikh War (1848–49) after Multan revolt; battles of Chillianwala and Gujrat; annexation by Dalhousie in March 1849.

*Punjab:*
- Battlefields in Punjab: Mudki, Ferozeshah, Sabraon (Ferozepur district) — Anglo-Sikh War memorial at Ferozeshah.

*Conclusion:* The empire rested on one man's genius; weak institutions of succession and British ambition led to its fall.

*Diagram:* Timeline 1799 → 1809 → 1839 → 1845–46 → 1848–49 → annexation 1849.

**Q6.** What is groundwater crisis in Punjab? How its depletion creates water crisis in Punjab; relate with the cropping pattern. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Groundwater depletion is the most repeated Punjab geography/economy theme.

*Intro:* Punjab draws far more groundwater than is recharged; CGWB assessments put its stage of extraction above 150%, the highest among major states.

*Points:*
- Most blocks are 'over-exploited'; water tables in central Punjab fall by about 0.5–1 m a year in many areas.
- Cropping pattern: wheat–paddy rotation dominates; paddy, a water-intensive kharif crop, spread after the Green Revolution although Punjab's rainfall is moderate.
- Drivers: free electricity for tubewells (since 1997), assured MSP procurement of paddy and wheat, submersible pumps, decline of canal irrigation share.
- Effects: deeper and costlier tubewells, small farmers' debt, drying of shallow wells, rising arsenic/uranium/nitrate contamination in parts of Malwa.
- Steps: Punjab Preservation of Subsoil Water Act 2009 (delays paddy transplanting until mid-June); direct seeded rice incentives; 'Paani Bachao Paise Kamao' pilot; ban on PUSA-44 seed sale (2024).
- Diversification to maize, cotton, pulses, basmati, horticulture; micro-irrigation; canal lining and recharge structures.
- Need: assured markets for alternative crops and metered/priced power in the long run.

*Punjab:*
- Sangrur, Moga, Barnala, Patiala and Jalandhar are among the worst-hit districts.

*Conclusion:* Unless the paddy-centred cropping pattern changes, Punjab risks desertification of its aquifers.

*Diagram:* Cycle: free power + MSP paddy → over-pumping → falling water table → costlier pumping → debt.

**Q7.** Drainage pattern in Peninsular India is a result of its geological structure and topography. Elaborate. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Indian physical geography; drainage is a standard question.

*Intro:* Peninsular rivers are old, mostly rain-fed and shaped by the plateau's tilt, faults and the Western Ghats.

*Points:*
- The Western Ghats act as the main water divide; the plateau tilts east, so most rivers (Mahanadi, Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri) flow east to the Bay of Bengal.
- East-flowing rivers build large deltas on the gentle eastern coastal plain.
- Narmada and Tapi flow west through rift valleys (faults) between Vindhya–Satpura ranges and form estuaries, not deltas.
- Short, swift west-flowing rivers of the Western Ghats form waterfalls and estuaries on the narrow western coast.
- Northern rivers of the plateau (Chambal, Betwa, Son) flow north-east to the Ganga because of the northward slope of the Malwa/Bundelkhand area.
- Patterns: radial (Amarkantak), trellis and rectangular in jointed rocks, dendritic in uniform areas.
- Old, graded rivers with broad shallow valleys; seasonal flow due to lack of glaciers.

*Punjab:*
- Contrast with Punjab's Himalayan perennial rivers (Sutlej, Beas, Ravi) that carry snowmelt and alluvium.

*Conclusion:* Structure and slope, not just rainfall, explain the peninsular drainage pattern.

*Diagram:* Sketch map of India showing east- and west-flowing peninsular rivers and the Western Ghats divide.

**Q8.** How can the western triangle comprising of Ruhr and Flanders be comparable to Chotanagpur industrial belt in India? Bring out the salient features. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Industrial location comparison; specific comparison is rare.

*Intro:* Both regions grew as heavy-industry belts on coal and iron, but their paths have diverged.

*Points:*
- Ruhr–Flanders (Germany–Belgium–NE France): rich coal, iron ore from Lorraine, Rhine waterway, dense rail, skilled labour — steel, chemicals, engineering.
- Chota Nagpur (Jharkhand, West Bengal, Odisha): coal (Jharia, Raniganj, Bokaro), iron ore (Singhbhum, Odisha), manganese, mica; Damodar Valley Corporation (1948) for power and water.
- Industries: Jamshedpur (Tata steel, 1907), Bokaro, Durgapur, Rourkela; heavy engineering at Ranchi.
- Similarities: raw-material-based location, steel and power, dense rail links, pollution.
- Differences: Ruhr diversified into services and green industries after coal decline (regeneration of the Emscher area); Chota Nagpur still mining-dependent.
- Chota Nagpur faces tribal displacement, poor urban amenities, left-wing extremism and lower technology levels.
- Ruhr enjoys cheap water transport on the Rhine; Chota Nagpur depends on rail and is far from ports.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's Mandi Gobindgarh steel-rerolling cluster depends on scrap and raw material brought from such belts — shows importance of location factors.

*Conclusion:* Chota Nagpur can learn from Ruhr's regeneration: diversification, environmental restoration and skills.

**Q9(a).** Explain briefly: Soils and Vegetation are true index of climate. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Basic physical geography concept.

*Intro:* Over long periods, temperature and rainfall decide how soils form and which plants grow, so soils and vegetation reflect climate.

*Points:*
- Zonal soils: laterites in hot, wet tropics (leaching); podzols under cold, humid coniferous forests; chernozems in temperate grasslands; arid soils in deserts.
- Vegetation zones follow climate: equatorial rainforests, savannas, deserts, Mediterranean scrub, taiga, tundra.
- Köppen based his climate classes partly on vegetation boundaries.
- In India: evergreen forests in high-rainfall Western Ghats, thorn forests in Rajasthan, alpine vegetation in high Himalaya.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab: alluvial soils with tropical dry deciduous/thorn vegetation in the south-west (semi-arid), Shivalik scrub in the north-east.

*Conclusion:* Hence soil and vegetation maps closely match climate maps.

<a id="q9-b"></a>

**Q9(b).** Explain briefly: Mechanism of plate tectonic. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Core geomorphology; asked often in short form.

*Intro:* Plate tectonics (1960s) holds that the lithosphere is broken into rigid plates moving over the asthenosphere.

*Points:*
- Driving forces: mantle convection currents, ridge push at mid-ocean ridges, slab pull at subduction zones.
- Divergent boundaries: plates move apart, new crust forms (Mid-Atlantic Ridge, East African Rift).
- Convergent boundaries: subduction (ocean–continent: Andes; ocean–ocean: island arcs) or collision (Indian plate with Eurasian → Himalaya).
- Transform boundaries: plates slide past (San Andreas Fault).
- Results: earthquakes, volcanoes, mountain building, ocean trenches.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab lies in seismic zone IV in the north-east (Shivalik) due to Himalayan plate collision.

*Conclusion:* Plate tectonics unifies continental drift and sea-floor spreading.

*Diagram:* Diagram of three plate boundaries.

**Q10(a).** Write brief notes on: Fohn, Depression, Tsunami, Richter Scale. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Definitions; occasional short-note item.

*Intro:* Four terms from climatology and geophysics:

*Points:*
- Föhn: warm, dry wind descending the leeward slopes of the Alps; heats by compression, melts snow (similar: Chinook in the Rockies).
- Depression: an area of low pressure with converging winds; in India, the stage of a tropical disturbance before it becomes a cyclonic storm; brings cloud and rain.
- Tsunami: series of long sea waves caused by undersea earthquakes, landslides or volcanoes; e.g., Indian Ocean tsunami of 26 December 2004.
- Richter scale: logarithmic scale of earthquake magnitude devised by Charles Richter (1935); each whole number = about 32 times more energy; now largely replaced by the moment magnitude scale.

*Punjab:*


*Conclusion:* Each term describes a distinct natural phenomenon or measure.

- Rare in the South Atlantic and South-East Pacific due to cold currents and strong wind shear.

*Punjab:*

**Q11.** Describe the changing patterns of rural-urban demography in India. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Population and urbanisation are a syllabus item; Census 2027 will renew interest.

*Intro:* India is still mostly rural, but urban growth has accelerated.

*Points:*
- Census 2011: urban population 31.2% (about 377 million); for the first time since 1921 urban areas added more people than rural areas in a decade (2001–11).
- Census towns (rural-administered but urban in character) rose sharply between 2001 and 2011 — 'hidden urbanisation'.
- Growth of large cities and urban agglomerations; peri-urban sprawl along highways.
- Migration: rural-to-urban for jobs; seasonal and circular migration; Covid (2020) showed reverse migration.
- Demographic differences: lower fertility in cities; older age structure in some rural areas due to youth out-migration; urban sex ratio lower than rural.
- Rural non-farm employment rising; rural–urban continuum blurring old boundaries.
- Census 2027 (first digital census, with caste enumeration) will update these patterns.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab: 37.5% urban (Census 2011); rural Doaba has many elderly as youth emigrate abroad; migrant workers from UP/Bihar fill farm and factory jobs.

*Conclusion:* India's demography is steadily urbanising; planning must treat rural and urban as one continuum.

**Q13.** Define ethical dilemma. Discuss some common ethical dilemmas that arise for those holding public offices. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Ethics questions appear every year (now also in the GS IV problem-solving section).

*Intro:* An ethical dilemma is a situation where a person must choose between two or more morally valid options, and choosing one means compromising another.

*Points:*
- Rules vs compassion: removing encroachments of poor families.
- Loyalty to superiors/political executive vs public interest (illegal orders).

- Resolution aids: Constitution and law, codes of conduct, Nolan principles (selflessness, integrity, objectivity, accountability, openness, honesty, leadership), conscience, consulting seniors, documenting decisions.

*Punjab:*
- Example: an SDM in Punjab facing pressure to go soft on illegal sand mining or stubble-burning cases.

*Conclusion:* Clear values and institutional support help officers resolve dilemmas in favour of the public good.

**Q15.** Critically examine the causes and consequences of increasing atrocities against women despite the existence of stringent laws. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Women's safety is perennial; asked across GS-I and essays.

*Intro:* India has strong laws, yet crimes against women remain high — NCRB recorded about 4.45 lakh such cases in 2022.

*Points:*
- Laws: Criminal Law (Amendment) Act 2013 (after the Justice Verma Committee), POCSO 2012, Domestic Violence Act 2005, POSH Act 2013; now Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita (2023) with a chapter on offences against women and children.
- Causes: patriarchal attitudes and gender stereotypes; low conviction rates and long trials; police insensitivity and under-reporting.
