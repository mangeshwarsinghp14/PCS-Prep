**Q1.** "Transparency and Accountability are the key to the success of Good Governance." Build your case for or against it. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Good governance, transparency and accountability are asked nearly every year.

*Intro:* Good governance (UNDP/World Bank) rests on participation, rule of law, transparency, responsiveness, equity, effectiveness and accountability.

*Points:*
- Case for: transparency exposes corruption and arbitrariness; accountability ensures officials answer for results and misconduct.
- Tools: Right to Information Act 2005, citizen charters, social audits, CAG audits, e-governance, Lokpal (first appointed 2019).
- Evidence: RTI exposed scams; DBT and e-procurement cut leakages.
- Limits: transparency without capacity creates 'paralysis by audit'; officials fear honest decisions.
- Other pillars also needed: competence, ethics, participation, timely justice.
- Balanced view: they are necessary but not sufficient; must be combined with capacity and integrity.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab: Right to Service Act 2011, Sewa Kendras, online land records and the anti-corruption helpline improve transparency.

*Conclusion:* Transparency and accountability are the backbone of good governance, but they work best with capable, ethical institutions.

**Q2.** "Corruption in public life is a great challenge to the Indian democracy." Explain it. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Corruption/probity is perennial in PPSC.

*Intro:* Corruption — abuse of public office for private gain — undermines trust, equality and development.

*Points:*
- India ranked 96th of 180 countries in Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index 2024 (score 38).
- Effects on democracy: money power in elections, policy capture, unequal access to services, loss of faith in institutions.
- Economic costs: leakages in welfare, inflated project costs, deterred investment.
- Causes: discretionary powers, complex procedures, low pay in some levels, weak enforcement, political funding opacity.
- Measures: Prevention of Corruption Act 1988 (amended 2018 — bribe-giving made an offence), Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act 2013, CVC, RTI, e-governance, DBT, GeM.
- Needed: electoral funding reform, faster trials, whistle-blower protection, ethics training, reduced discretion.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab Vigilance Bureau's anti-corruption helpline and action against corrupt officials; drug-money and sand-mining nexus show local challenges.

*Conclusion:* Corruption threatens the very legitimacy of democracy; systemic reform plus ethical leadership are needed.

**Q4.** "Federal principle is one of the basic features of Indian Constitution but it is equally true that federalism under the Indian Constitution leans in favour of a strong Centre that militates against the concept of federalism." Discuss. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Federalism and Centre–State relations are asked almost every year.

*Intro:* India is a 'Union of States' (Article 1) — federal in form with strong unitary features; S.R. Bommai (1994) declared federalism part of the basic structure.

*Points:*
- Federal features: written constitution, division of powers (Seventh Schedule), independent judiciary, bicameralism, Finance Commission.
- Centre-leaning features: residuary powers with Union (Article 248), Parliament can create/alter states (Article 3), legislate on State List in national interest (Articles 249, 250, 252, 253).
- Emergency provisions (Articles 352, 356, 360); Governor appointed by Centre; All-India Services; single citizenship; integrated judiciary.
- Fiscal dominance: Union controls major taxes; states depend on transfers.
- Why: Partition, integration of princely states, need for unity — 'quasi-federal' (K.C. Wheare); 'cooperative federalism' (Granville Austin).
- Recent shifts: GST Council (2017) as shared sovereignty; Finance Commission devolution raised to 42% (14th) and 41% (15th).
- Concerns: misuse of Article 356 (curbed by Bommai), Governor controversies, centralised schemes.

*Punjab:*
- Anandpur Sahib Resolution (1973) demanded greater state autonomy; Punjab's river-water and Chandigarh disputes highlight federal tensions.

*Conclusion:* Indian federalism is flexible: a strong Centre for unity with states as partners — the balance must be preserved through cooperative practice.

*Punjab:*

*Conclusion:* Judicial activism is a corrective, not a substitute, for good administration.

**Q6.** Discuss the benefits and challenges to go for simultaneous elections to Lok Sabha and State Assemblies. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* 'One Nation, One Election' bill (2024) is pending before a JPC — very live.

*Intro:* Simultaneous elections were the norm from 1951–52 to 1967; the cycle broke with early dissolutions.

*Points:*
- Recent steps: Law Commission (170th report, 1999) and NITI Aayog paper (2017) supported it; High-Level Committee under Ram Nath Kovind reported in March 2024; Constitution (129th Amendment) Bill introduced in December 2024 and sent to a Joint Parliamentary Committee.
- Benefits: lower election costs, less policy paralysis from repeated Model Code of Conduct, better governance focus, higher turnout, fewer deployments of security forces.
- Challenges: constitutional amendments (Articles 83, 85, 172, 174, 356), what if a government falls mid-term, curtailing or extending assembly terms.
- Federal concerns: national issues may overshadow state issues; advantage to national parties.
- Logistics: huge EVM/VVPAT and manpower needs.
- Way forward: broad consensus, phased synchronisation, constructive no-confidence motion idea.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's Assembly cycle (2022–27) differs from Lok Sabha (2024–29); synchronisation would mean curtailing or extending its term.

*Conclusion:* Simultaneous elections can improve governance, but only with consensus and federal safeguards.

**Q10.** Discuss the impact of industrial effluents on aquatic life with reference to the rivers of Punjab. Also suggest some valid mitigation measures. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* River pollution (Buddha Nullah, Sutlej) is a recurring Punjab question and NGT matter.

*Intro:* Untreated or partly treated industrial effluents and sewage have badly polluted stretches of the Sutlej and Beas systems.

*Points:*
- Hotspot: Buddha Nullah in Ludhiana carries effluents from dyeing, electroplating and dairy units plus sewage into the Sutlej.
- Others: Kali Bein, Chitti Bein (Jalandhar), East Bein; effluents reach Harike wetland and then canals feeding Rajasthan.
- Impacts: high BOD and COD, very low dissolved oxygen, fish kills, heavy metals and dyes accumulating in fish and the food chain.
- Threat to the endangered Indus river dolphin in the Beas and to migratory birds and turtles at Harike.
- Human health: contaminated drinking water and irrigation water downstream (Malwa, Rajasthan).
- Mitigation: common effluent treatment plants (CETPs) for dyeing clusters, zero liquid discharge where feasible, online continuous monitoring, strict PPCB enforcement under the Water Act 1974, polluter-pays penalties.
- Sewage treatment plants, rejuvenation projects (e.g., Buddha Nullah rejuvenation project from 2020), NGT-appointed monitoring committees.
- Community action like the Kali Bein clean-up; public reporting of water-quality data.

*Punjab:*
- Ludhiana's Buddha Nullah and Jalandhar's Chitti Bein are the classic examples; the NGT has repeatedly fined and directed Punjab on Sutlej pollution.

*Conclusion:* Clean rivers need strict enforcement, treatment infrastructure and industry–community partnership.

**Q11.** Public Sector Undertakings have been the mainstay of India’s industrialization thrust. The same model was accepted by the States of the Union. However, over a period of time, some of the public sector undertakings due to faulty policy of governance landed themselves in a grave situation. Slowly and gradually, the ill effects of poor governance resulted in continuing losses for the PSUs, resulting in their being declared as a sick unit. As a newly posted Managing Director of such a sick PSU, you are saddled with the task of reviving it. In your effort to revive, you need to highlight the main drawbacks which were the bedrock of such a situation, and, the new initiatives you would deploy to revive the sick unit. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* 'Reviving a loss-making PSU' is a named problem-solving syllabus item.

*Intro:* First diagnose, then design a time-bound turnaround with stakeholder support.

*Points:*
- Drawbacks: political interference in appointments and pricing, overstaffing, outdated technology, poor marketing, weak financial discipline, high debt, delayed decisions, low accountability.
- Step 1 — Diagnosis: independent financial and operational audit; identify loss centres and viable products.
- Step 2 — Financial restructuring: negotiate with lenders, settle dues, seek one-time government support linked to milestones.
- Step 3 — Operations: modernise plant, cut waste, outsource non-core work, monetise idle land/assets.
- Step 4 — People: right-size through voluntary retirement, retrain staff, performance-linked incentives, union consultation.
- Step 5 — Market: new product mix, quality certification, digital marketing, tie-ups with private partners.
- Step 6 — Governance: professional board with independent directors, MoU-based performance targets, transparent procurement.
- Exit option: if unviable, strategic disinvestment or closure with fair worker settlement.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab examples: losses of state transport undertakings (PRTC/PUNBUS) and some state corporations; Milkfed's Verka shows a well-run cooperative model.

*Conclusion:* A turnaround needs professional autonomy, accountability and a clear, time-bound plan.

*Diagram:* Turnaround flow: diagnose → restructure finance → modernise → re-skill → market → govern.

**Q12.** Urban Slums pose a serious challenge before the urban planners. According to an estimate by 2030, 50% of India’s population would reside in urban areas. The impact of globalization and urbanization has stimulated the urban migration, resulting in a situation where the center-state collaboration could be the key to solve the riddle of re-setting and housing the newly migrated urban population. As an administrator, responsible for the cohesive urban development of the state, what policy initiatives would you propose to meet the emerging challenge. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Urban slums and housing is a named problem-solving item.

*Intro:* India's slum population was about 6.5 crore (Census 2011); migration keeps adding to informal settlements.

*Points:*
- Survey and map slums with GIS; secure tenure/property rights to encourage self-investment.
- In-situ redevelopment with private partners (land as resource), rather than distant relocation.
- Affordable housing: PMAY-Urban (2015) and PMAY-U 2.0 (2024); Affordable Rental Housing Complexes (2020) for migrants.

- Centre–state–ULB cooperation: convergence of schemes, municipal finance reform, community participation.
- Prevention: rental housing and regulated land supply so new slums do not form.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab gave proprietary rights to slum dwellers under its 'Basera' scheme (2020 law); Ludhiana and Amritsar have large slum clusters.

*Conclusion:* Inclusive, rights-based planning can turn slums into liveable neighbourhoods.

The riddle is, a rising income inequality cannot sustain the long-term growth of the economy. <details><summary>Model answer points</summary>
