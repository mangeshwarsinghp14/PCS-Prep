**Q1.** Has the implementation of the FRBM Act constrained the role of fiscal policy in the growth and development of India? Discuss. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Fiscal rules and debt are a constant theme; Centre and Punjab both face consolidation pressure in 2026–29.

*Intro:* The Fiscal Responsibility and Budget Management Act 2003 set rules to cut fiscal and revenue deficits; the 2018 amendment made debt the anchor (general government debt 60% of GDP; Centre 40%) with a fiscal deficit target of 3%.

*Points:*
- Constraint view: deficit targets led to cuts in capital expenditure and social spending in some years; pro-cyclical tightening.
- Escape clause (Sec. 4(2)) used in crises — targets repeatedly deferred (2008–09, COVID-19), showing rigidity is limited.
- Creative accounting: off-budget borrowings (e.g. FCI's NSSF loans) until brought on budget in 2021.
- Enabling view: fiscal discipline lowered interest costs, preserved credit ratings and created space for record public capex after 2020.
- Consolidation path: fiscal deficit 4.4% of GDP (2025-26 BE) and 4.3% (2026-27 BE); debt-to-GDP to fall to about 55.6% in 2026-27 with a goal of 50±1% by 2030-31.
- Quality matters: shift from revenue to capital spending supports growth while meeting targets.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab FRBM Act 2003 targets are routinely missed — fiscal deficit 4.7% of GSDP in 2024-25 (actual) against 3.8% budgeted; outstanding liabilities about 45% of GSDP (2026-27 BE).

*Conclusion:* FRBM has disciplined rather than disabled fiscal policy; a flexible, debt-anchored rule with protected capex is the balanced path.

**Q2.** Why did rapid economic growth not help reduce unemployment significantly in India? *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Jobless growth/employment is the most repeated economy theme in UPSC/PPSC.

*Intro:* India has been among the fastest-growing large economies, yet employment growth — especially quality, formal jobs — has lagged ('jobless growth').

*Points:*
- Growth led by capital- and skill-intensive services (IT, finance) rather than labour-intensive manufacturing.
- Manufacturing share in GDP stuck around 15–17% for decades; labour-intensive sectors (textiles, leather, food processing) under-performed.
- Agriculture still employs the largest share of workers with disguised unemployment.
- Skill mismatch: graduates lack employable skills; low vocational training.
- Informality: about 90% of workforce in informal sector; rigid labour laws (now consolidated into four Labour Codes, in force from Nov 2025) discouraged scale.
- Automation and capital-labour substitution.
- Low female labour force participation hides under-employment.
- Note: PLFS shows unemployment rate fell to about 3.2% (2023-24, usual status, 15+) but much new work is self-employment and unpaid family labour.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab youth unemployment and migration abroad (study visas) reflect lack of quality jobs despite decent incomes.

*Conclusion:* Growth must become employment-intensive through labour-intensive manufacturing, MSMEs, skills and women's work.

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Punjab-specific labour/gender question; Mawan Dhian Satkar cash scheme (2026) keeps women's economic role in debate.

*Intro:* Punjab's female literacy was 70.7% (Census 2011), above the national average, yet its female labour force participation rate (15+, PLFS) was only 27.6% in 2022-23 against 37.0% for India.

*Points:*
- Agrarian structure: mechanised wheat–paddy farming needs little female labour, unlike hill or rice-transplanting states.

*Punjab:*
- Policies: Mukh Mantri Mawan Dhian Satkar Yojana cash transfer (2026-27), SHGs under PSRLM, women's reservation in jobs (33% in Punjab government jobs).

*Conclusion:* Punjab's challenge is converting education into employment through local jobs, safe transport, childcare and skills.

**Q4.** Critically evaluate the performance and challenges of Public Distribution System (PDS) in ensuring food and nutrition security in India. *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* PDS/food security is a perennial GS-III theme with Punjab as a major procurement state.

*Intro:* PDS distributes subsidised foodgrains through about 5 lakh fair price shops; since the National Food Security Act 2013 it is a legal entitlement for up to 75% rural and 50% urban population (about 81 crore people).

*Points:*

- Reforms: diversify basket (millets, pulses), DBT pilots, Census-based updating, better storage, convergence with ICDS and PM POSHAN.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab supplies a large share of central pool wheat and rice; state schemes like the Atta-Dal scheme and Meri Rasoi ration kits (2026-27) add nutrition support.

*Conclusion:* PDS has evolved from food to rights-based security; the next step is nutrition security with efficiency.

**Q5.** An analysis of monthly wages paid to the workers of two firms A and B belonging to the same industry gives the following results: Firm A — No. of workers 500, Average daily wage Rs 186.00, Variance of distribution of wages 81; Firm B — No. of workers 600, Average daily wage Rs 175.00, Variance of distribution of wages 100. (a) Which firm A or B has a larger wage bill? (b) In which firm A or B, is there greater variability in individual wages? *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Statistics (Paper VI) is a compulsory syllabus section; numerical items on averages, dispersion, correlation and graphs will recur every year.

*Intro:* Wage bill = number of workers × average wage; variability is compared using standard deviation and, more properly when means differ, the coefficient of variation (CV = SD/mean × 100).

*Points:*
- (a) Wage bill A = 500 × 186 = Rs 93,000; wage bill B = 600 × 175 = Rs 1,05,000.
- Firm B has the larger wage bill (by Rs 12,000 per wage period).
- (b) SD A = √81 = 9; SD B = √100 = 10.
- CV A = 9/186 × 100 ≈ 4.84%; CV B = 10/175 × 100 ≈ 5.71%.
- Firm B has greater variability in individual wages, both in absolute terms (higher SD) and relative terms (higher CV).
- Firm A's wages are more uniform/consistent.

*Punjab:*


*Conclusion:* Firm B pays the larger total wage bill and also has more unequal (variable) wages.

**Q6(a).** Two welfare schemes show the following distribution of welfare benefits: Scheme A — Mean Rs 10,000, SD Rs 2,000; Scheme B — Mean Rs 25,000, SD Rs 4,000. Using the coefficient of variation, analyse which scheme demonstrates greater relative dispersion. *(5 marks, 50 words)*

*Intro:* Coefficient of variation (CV) = (SD ÷ Mean) × 100; it compares dispersion of series with different means.

*Points:*
- CV of Scheme A = 2,000/10,000 × 100 = 20%.
- CV of Scheme B = 4,000/25,000 × 100 = 16%.
- Scheme A has greater relative dispersion (20% > 16%), even though Scheme B has the larger absolute SD.
- Hence benefits under Scheme B are more uniformly distributed relative to its mean.

*Conclusion:* Scheme A shows greater relative dispersion; Scheme B is more consistent.

**Q9.** How does Operation Sindoor represent a watershed moment in India's national security doctrine? *(10 marks, 100 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Very recent and central to security discourse; Punjab border was directly affected.

*Intro:* Operation Sindoor was India's precision strike campaign on 7 May 2025 against nine terror sites in Pakistan and PoK, in response to the Pahalgam terror attack of 22 April 2025 that killed 26 civilians.

*Points:*
- Targets included headquarters of Jaish-e-Mohammed (Bahawalpur) and Lashkar-e-Taiba (Muridke) — deep inside Pakistan, not only PoK.
- After Pakistani retaliation, India struck Pakistani air bases; a cessation of hostilities was agreed on 10 May 2025.
- Doctrinal shift 1: any major terror attack treated as an act of war, with response on India's terms and timing.
- Doctrinal shift 2: no tolerance for 'nuclear blackmail' — conventional punishment below the nuclear threshold.
- Doctrinal shift 3: no distinction between terrorists and their state sponsors.
- Non-military tools: Indus Waters Treaty held in abeyance (April 2025), trade and visa curbs, global diplomatic outreach by all-party delegations.
- Showcase of indigenous systems (Akash, BrahMos, integrated air defence) and drones; jointness of forces.
- Continuum: Surgical strikes (2016) → Balakot (2019) → Sindoor (2025) — rising escalation threshold.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab border districts saw drone and missile interception, blackouts and civilian preparedness drills during May 2025.

*Conclusion:* Sindoor marks a move from strategic restraint to proactive deterrence; sustaining it needs strong air defence, intelligence and diplomacy.

**Q11.** Critically examine the role of the minimum support price in improving farm income security and its distorting impacts on cropping patterns, agricultural markets, and fiscal sustainability. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* MSP debate (legal guarantee demand, diversification) is central to Punjab and asked repeatedly.

*Intro:* MSP is announced by the Centre on CACP recommendations for 22 mandated crops (plus FRP for sugarcane); since 2018-19 it is fixed at least 1.5 times the A2+FL cost of production.

*Points:*
- Income security: assured price reduces price risk and distress sales; supported Green Revolution and food self-sufficiency.
- Ensures stocks for PDS and buffer; stabilises rural demand.
- Limited reach: effective procurement mainly for wheat and paddy and in a few states (Punjab, Haryana, MP, Chhattisgarh); most farmers elsewhere sell below MSP.
- Cropping distortion: assured procurement of wheat and rice locks Punjab–Haryana into water-guzzling paddy; pulses, oilseeds and millets neglected — India imports edible oils and pulses.
- Ecological cost: groundwater depletion, stubble burning, soil degradation.
- Market distortion: crowds out private trade, excess stocks beyond buffer norms, export competitiveness issues, WTO Agreement on Agriculture scrutiny (peace clause).
- Fiscal burden: high food subsidy, carrying cost of FCI stocks; open-ended procurement is costly.
- Way forward: price deficiency payments (Bhavantar model), crop-neutral incentives, procurement of pulses/oilseeds (PM-AASHA), income support (PM-KISAN), crop diversification with assured buying of maize and cotton.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's near-total procurement of wheat and paddy; demand for legal MSP was a core issue of the 2020–21 farm protests.

*Conclusion:* MSP should evolve from a crop-specific procurement tool into a diversified income-security system that rewards sustainable crops.

*Diagram:* Flow: MSP + assured procurement → wheat–paddy monoculture → groundwater/stubble problems → need for diversification incentives.

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Investment models are a named syllabus node; PPP is repeated across three papers of this exam.

**Q15.** How did land reforms attempt to address social and economic equity in rural India? *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Land reforms are a named node; asked periodically by PPSC and UPSC.

*Intro:* After independence, land reforms aimed to end feudal exploitation, give land to the tiller and reduce rural inequality — a Directive Principle (Art. 39(b),(c)).

*Points:*
- Abolition of intermediaries (Zamindari, Jagirdari) in the 1950s — tenants came into direct contact with the state.
- Tenancy reforms: regulation of rent, security of tenure, ownership rights to tenants (West Bengal's Operation Barga, 1978).
- Land ceiling laws: surplus land to be distributed to landless, especially SC/ST.
- Consolidation of holdings: reduced fragmentation and enabled tubewells and mechanisation.
- Bhoodan–Gramdan movement (Vinoba Bhave, 1951) for voluntary land gifting.
- Ninth Schedule (1st Amendment 1951) protected land laws from litigation.
- Equity outcomes: weakened landed aristocracy, empowered middle peasantry; but ceiling laws had loopholes (benami transfers), little land distributed, oral tenancy persisted.
- Modern phase: digitisation of land records (DILRMP), SVAMITVA property cards (2020), Model Agricultural Land Leasing Act (2016).

*Punjab:*
- Punjab led consolidation of holdings (Punjab Holdings (Consolidation and Prevention of Fragmentation) Act 1948); Punjab Land Reforms Act 1972 fixed ceiling; abolition of biswedari strengthened owner-cultivators — a base for the Green Revolution, though Dalits remained largely landless.

*Conclusion:* Land reforms succeeded in abolishing intermediaries but only partly in redistribution; secure land records and leasing reform are the next step.

**Q18.** Digital radicalization has transformed the nature of extremism in India. Elaborate how online extremism and digital propaganda affect India's national security. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Role of media/social networking in security is a named node; online radicalisation is a rising theme.

*Intro:* Digital radicalisation is the process by which individuals adopt extremist ideas mainly through online content, encrypted messaging and social media communities.

*Points:*
- Recruitment without contact: ISIS-inspired modules recruited youth online (cases from Kerala, Karnataka, Maharashtra).
- Lone-wolf and self-radicalised attackers are harder to detect than organised cells.
- Encrypted apps, dark web and gaming platforms used for planning, funding (crypto) and propaganda.
- Cross-border influence operations: fake accounts spread separatist narratives and disinformation during crises (e.g. after Pahalgam/Op Sindoor).

- Effects: threat to internal security, social harmony, democratic processes and trust in institutions.
- Responses: IT Act Sec. 69A blocking, IT Rules 2021 (due diligence, traceability debates), UAPA, NIA cyber units, platform cooperation, counter-narrative and de-radicalisation programmes, digital literacy.
- Balance: free speech (Art. 19) and privacy vs security; avoid over-broad shutdowns.

*Punjab:*
- Online pro-Khalistan propaganda from abroad targets Punjab youth; gangsters use social media for recruitment and extortion.

*Conclusion:* Extremism has moved from jungles to screens; counter-radicalisation must combine intelligence, platform regulation and community engagement.

**Q19.** Money laundering sustains organized crime and terrorism. Critically examine the mechanisms of money laundering and their impact on internal security. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Money laundering is a named syllabus node; ED/PMLA and FATF are always topical.

*Intro:* Money laundering is disguising the illegal origin of money through three stages — placement, layering and integration — so that it appears legitimate.

*Points:*
- Mechanisms: hawala networks; trade-based laundering (over/under-invoicing); shell companies and benami property.
- Cash-intensive businesses, real estate, gold and jewellery.
- Cryptocurrencies, online gaming and betting apps, mule bank accounts.
- Round-tripping through tax havens; NGOs and charities misused for terror funding.
- Impact on security: finances terror groups (narco-terror in Punjab and J&K), arms and fake currency (FICN); funds Left-Wing Extremism via extortion.
- Corrupts officials, police and politics; distorts economy and tax base.
- Legal framework: PMLA 2002 (ED powers upheld in Vijay Madanlal Choudhary, 2022), FIU-IND, Black Money Act 2015, Benami Act (amended 2016), UAPA for terror financing; crypto entities brought under PMLA (2023).
- International: FATF mutual evaluation (June 2024) placed India in 'regular follow-up' — a positive rating; concerns remain on low conviction rate and misuse allegations.

*Punjab:*
- Drug money from cross-border smuggling in Punjab is laundered through hawala and property; Punjab Police attach properties under NDPS Act Sec. 68.

*Conclusion:* Following the money is the surest way to disable crime and terror networks; strong financial intelligence must go with fair, quick prosecution.

**Q20.** Analyse the major security challenges faced by India in its border areas and the strategies adopted for their management. Substantiate with examples from Punjab. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Border management with Punjab focus is a perennial PPSC question.

*Intro:* India has about 15,000 km of land borders with seven countries; each border has distinct threats — terrain, hostile neighbours and porous stretches.

*Points:*
- Pakistan border: infiltration, cross-border terrorism, drones carrying drugs and weapons, ceasefire violations.
- China (LAC): territorial disputes, infrastructure race, grey-zone tactics (Galwan 2020).
- Bangladesh: illegal migration, cattle and fake-currency smuggling, porous riverine border.
- Nepal/Bhutan open borders: misuse by criminals and insurgents; Myanmar: insurgents, drugs and refugees (Free Movement Regime review).
- Strategies: border fencing and floodlighting; Comprehensive Integrated Border Management System (smart fencing, sensors, cameras).
- One Border One Force policy; BSF, ITBP, SSB, Assam Rifles; anti-drone systems.
- Border infrastructure: roads, ICPs (Attari), Vibrant Villages Programme (I for China border, II approved 2025 for other land borders including Punjab).
- Community engagement: Border Area Development Programme, civic action, village defence committees.
- Punjab: 553 km border with Pakistan; riverine gaps (Ravi, Sutlej) and farmland beyond the fence; drones from Pakistan; BSF jurisdiction extended to 50 km (2021); coordination with Punjab Police and anti-drone tech.

*Punjab:*
- Attari–Wagah ICP; border villages of Gurdaspur, Amritsar, Tarn Taran, Ferozepur, Fazilka; farmers' access to land beyond fence.

*Conclusion:* Border management must combine technology, forces and development — secure borders need prosperous border villages.
