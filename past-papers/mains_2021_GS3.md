**Q1.** Discuss the characteristics of the Indian labour market. What are the reforms that are needed to improve the state of employment in India and what do the four codes on wages, on industrial relations, on occupational health, safety and working conditions and on social security propose in achieving the labour reforms? *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Labour codes came into force on 21 November 2025 and states (incl. Punjab) are framing rules — very live.

*Intro:* India's labour market is large, young and mostly informal; 29 central labour laws were merged into four codes, which came into force on 21 November 2025.

*Points:*
- Characteristics: about 90% informal employment; high self-employment; low female labour force participation (41.7% in PLFS 2023-24, 15+ years, usual status) though rising; disguised unemployment in farming; skill mismatch; youth unemployment higher than overall.
- Code on Wages, 2019: universal minimum wage, national floor wage, timely payment, equal pay regardless of gender.
- Industrial Relations Code, 2020: prior permission for lay-off/closure only for units with 300+ workers (earlier 100), fixed-term employment, recognition of negotiating unions, notice for strikes, re-skilling fund.
- Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 2020: single registration and licence, free annual health check-ups, women allowed in night shifts with consent and safeguards, inter-state migrant worker provisions.
- Code on Social Security, 2020: covers gig and platform workers for the first time, aggregator contribution, ESIC/EPFO expansion, e-Shram database for unorganised workers.
- Reforms still needed: labour-intensive manufacturing, MSME credit, skilling with apprenticeships, women-friendly workplaces (childcare, safety, transport), urban employment programmes.
- Concerns: dilution of job security, weaker union rights, state-level variation in rules, enforcement capacity ('inspector-cum-facilitator').

*Punjab:*
- Punjab has notified rules under the codes (2026); Ludhiana's hosiery, bicycle and auto-part MSMEs employ many migrant workers who benefit from portable social security.
- Punjab farm-labour (largely Dalit and migrant) remains outside most formal protections.

*Conclusion:* The codes simplify and modernise labour law; their success depends on job creation and fair enforcement.

*Diagram:* Four boxes — one per code — with 2–3 key features each.

*Points:*

*Punjab:*

*Conclusion:* NIP gave direction; implementation needs state capacity, private trust and quick dispute resolution.

**Q3.** Explain the structure and state of the power sector in India. Present a critical analysis of the government proposal to privatise power distribution companies in the country via the Electricity Amendment Bill 2020 and the Draft Standard Bidding Document for privatisation of Discoms. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Power-sector reform and discom finances are perennial; Punjab's free-power subsidy makes it Punjab-central.

*Intro:* India's power sector has three segments — generation, transmission and distribution — with distribution the weakest link.

*Points:*
- Generation: mix of central, state and private; non-fossil sources reached about 50% of installed capacity in mid-2025, five years ahead of the 2030 NDC target, though coal still dominates actual generation.
- Transmission: One Nation One Grid (national grid synchronised in 2013).
- Distribution: mostly state-owned discoms with high losses, unpaid subsidies and tariff gaps; repeated bailouts (UDAY 2015, RDSS 2021).
- Electricity (Amendment) Bill 2020 draft: Electricity Contract Enforcement Authority, cost-reflective tariffs, subsidy via DBT, distribution sub-licensees/franchisees, renewable purchase obligations with penalties.
- Standard Bidding Document (2020): framework to privatise discoms of Union Territories — e.g., Dadra & Nagar Haveli and Daman & Diu, and Chandigarh (taken over by a CESC group company in February 2025).
- Arguments for: efficiency, lower losses, investment, better service (Delhi and Odisha privatisation results).
- Against: states' federal concerns (electricity is concurrent), fear of tariff hikes, threat to free/cheap power for farmers, employee opposition.
- Balanced path: competition through multiple suppliers, smart metering, targeted DBT subsidy, regulatory independence.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab: PSPCL supplies free power to farm tubewells (since 1997) and up to 300 units/month free to households (from July 2022), creating a large subsidy bill.
- PSPCL bought the 540 MW GVK Goindwal Sahib thermal plant in 2024; Punjab farmers and employees opposed the 2020 bill strongly.

*Conclusion:* Privatisation alone is not a magic bullet; tariff rationalisation, subsidy targeting and governance reform must go together.

*Diagram:* Flow diagram: generation → transmission → distribution → consumer, with loss points marked.

**Q4.** Discuss the concepts of network trade and network products. Critically analyse India's performance in the manufacture and trade of network products. Do you think that the Covid-19 crisis has presented India with a unique opportunity to replace China as the manufacturing hub of the world? *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Global value chains and 'China+1' remain live; the Covid framing is dated.

*Intro:* Network trade is trade within global production networks where different stages of a product are made in different countries; network products are goods made this way.

*Points:*
- Network products: electronics and telecom equipment, computers, electrical machinery, automobiles and parts, aircraft parts.
- East Asia (China, ASEAN) dominates because of scale, infrastructure, trade openness and logistics.
- India's performance was weak: small share in global network-product exports, high tariffs on inputs, logistics costs, rigid labour laws, poor scale.
- Since 2020: PLI for mobile phones and electronics made smartphones one of India's top exports; Apple suppliers expanded in India.
- Covid and US–China tensions led firms to adopt 'China+1'; Vietnam, Mexico and India gained.
- Constraints to replacing China: component ecosystem still Chinese, lower productivity, power and land issues, need for skilled labour.
- Needed: lower input tariffs, FTAs, plug-and-play industrial parks, semiconductor mission (2021), logistics policy (2022).

*Punjab:*
- Punjab can join auto-part, bicycle and electronics networks (Ludhiana, Mohali) if logistics and power reliability improve.

*Conclusion:* India can become an alternative hub, not a full replacement for China, by deep integration into value chains.

**Q5.** Critically analyse the suspension of the Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code by the Government of India in wake of the Covid-19 pandemic. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* One-off Covid measure; IBC questions recur but not this angle.

*Intro:* In 2020 the government suspended fresh insolvency filings under the IBC, 2016 to protect firms hit by the lockdown.

*Points:*
- Default threshold for filing raised from ₹1 lakh to ₹1 crore (March 2020).
- Section 10A (Ordinance June 2020, later Act): no new insolvency case for defaults arising from 25 March 2020, extended up to 25 March 2021 — one year in all.
- Rationale: viable firms should not be pushed into liquidation due to a temporary shock; protects jobs and MSMEs.
- Concerns: creditors lost a recovery tool; ever-greening and hidden stress; delayed resolution erodes value.
- Defaults during the period could never be used for insolvency — a permanent bar.
- Follow-up: pre-packaged insolvency for MSMEs (April 2021); RBI resolution frameworks 1.0 and 2.0.
- Wider IBC issues: long delays beyond time limits, low recovery rates, NCLT vacancies.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's MSMEs in Ludhiana and Mandi Gobindgarh benefited from relief; local banks had to restructure loans.

*Conclusion:* Suspension was a justified emergency step, but it should be temporary and paired with faster resolution.

**Q6.** Comment on the stance of the Indian Government on the regulation of Cryptocurrencies in India. What are the advantages and disadvantages of Cryptocurrencies in the Indian context? Discuss. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Crypto regulation is unsettled; asked in economy and security contexts.

*Intro:* India has moved from hostility to 'regulate and tax' without legalising crypto as currency.

*Points:*
- RBI circular (April 2018) stopped banks from serving crypto firms; struck down by the Supreme Court in IAMAI v. RBI (March 2020).
- Budget 2022: 30% tax on income from virtual digital assets and 1% TDS on transfers.
- March 2023: crypto service providers brought under the Prevention of Money Laundering Act; must register with FIU-IND.
- RBI's pilots of the digital rupee (CBDC) from November–December 2022 as the official alternative.
- India pushed a global framework during its G20 presidency (2023).
- Advantages: fast cheap remittances, blockchain innovation, financial inclusion, new jobs in Web3.
- Disadvantages: extreme volatility, investor losses and frauds, money laundering and terror financing, threat to monetary sovereignty, capital flight, energy use.
- A comprehensive law is still pending; a discussion paper has been awaited for years.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab Police and ED have found crypto used in drug and gangster payments across the border.

*Conclusion:* India's cautious, tax-and-trace approach is sensible; clear legislation and global coordination are the next steps.

**Q7.** In the post economic reforms period, the slowdown of agriculture sector of Punjab has impacted and restricted the growth of other sectors of the economy which resulted into marginalization of the state's economy. Examine this statement critically. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Punjab economy and agrarian stagnation are asked in almost every PPSC GS-III paper.

*Intro:* Punjab led India after the Green Revolution, but since the 1990s its growth has lagged the national average.

*Points:*
- Agriculture plateaued: wheat–paddy yields near ceiling, little diversification, MSP-dependent marketing.
- Agriculture's share is still large — about 23% of GSVA (Punjab Economic Survey 2025-26); agriculture contracted by 2.5% in 2025-26 after the 2025 floods.
- Groundwater crisis: extraction above 150% of annual recharge (CGWB assessments), driven by free power and paddy.
- Linkages: farm income stagnation lowered demand for local industry and services; agro-processing remained weak.
- Industry also faced own problems: border location, insurgency years (1980s–early 90s), tax incentives to neighbouring hill states (2003), high power costs.
- Fiscal stress: heavy debt and subsidies limit public investment.
- Critique: not only agriculture — weak industrial policy, brain drain and emigration also matter; services have grown (48% of GSVA, 2025-26).
- Way out: crop diversification (Johl Committee 1986, 2002), agro-processing clusters, FPOs, water-pricing reform, MSME revival, skill-based jobs.

*Punjab:*
- Real GSDP growth 6.1% and per capita income ₹2,30,523 (Punjab Economic Survey 2025-26); sector shares agri 23%, industry 29%, services 48%.

*Conclusion:* Agrarian stagnation is a key, but not the only, cause; a diversified, value-added economy is the way forward.

*Diagram:* Cycle diagram: wheat–paddy lock-in → groundwater depletion → stagnant incomes → weak demand → low investment.

**Q8.** Explain that with Covid's looming impact on the economy, there is an urgent need to increase skill imparting initiatives to hold onto India's Demographic Dividend. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Skilling and the demographic dividend are repeat themes.

*Intro:* India's working-age share will stay high until about 2040, but the dividend pays only if workers are skilled and employed.

*Points:*

*Punjab:*
- Punjab Skill Development Mission and ITIs; many Punjabi youth choose IELTS and migration over local skilling — skilling for overseas jobs can be formalised.

**Q11.** Do you think that the reforms in Police as a law enforcement agency is one of the bottlenecks in the internal security policy of India. Examine critically. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Police reform is a perennial question; new criminal laws (2024) add relevance.

*Intro:* Police is a state subject and the first responder to internal security threats, yet reforms remain incomplete.

*Points:*
- Prakash Singh judgment (2006): seven directives — State Security Commission, fixed tenure for DGP and SPs, separation of investigation and law-and-order, Police Establishment Board, Police Complaints Authority — largely unimplemented in spirit.
- Problems: political interference, vacancies, poor training, outdated equipment, weak forensics, low conviction rates, overburdened constables.
- Intelligence and coordination gaps between state police and central agencies.
- Counter-view: internal security also depends on central forces, intelligence, border management and development; police is one, not the only, bottleneck.
- Recent changes: Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita and Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam in force from 1 July 2024 — forensic visits mandatory for serious crimes, e-FIR, video recording.
- Modernisation: CCTNS, ICJS, NATGRID; SMART policing.
- Way forward: implement Model Police Act (2006), 2nd ARC 5th report, community policing, cyber and forensic capacity.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab Police Act 2007; Punjab's Sadak Suraksha Force (2024) and anti-gangster task force; border-state policing against drones and narco-terror.

*Conclusion:* Police reform is a central bottleneck; without it, security policy fails at the cutting edge.

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

- International cooperation: Budapest Convention (India not a member), UN cybercrime convention adopted 2024.
- Needed: specialised cyber units, blockchain analytics, global information sharing, cyber-skilled police.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab Police and NCB have traced drug orders and payments via dark-web marketplaces; Punjab State Cyber Crime cell handles such cases.

*Conclusion:* Countering dark-web threats needs technology, skilled people and international cooperation.

**Q13.** Often the causes of violent extremism are categorised into 'push' and 'pull' factors and overlooks the links between 'individual' and 'community' factors. Critically examine the individual and community factors which are useful to understand extremism in India? *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Extremism and development linkage is a syllabus item; this framing is less common.

*Intro:* Extremism grows where personal vulnerabilities meet community grievances; seeing both levels helps prevention.

*Points:*
- Individual factors: identity crisis, sense of injustice, unemployment and frustration, psychological traits, online radicalisation, charismatic recruiters, adventure or revenge.
- Community factors: collective grievances (land, displacement, discrimination), weak state presence, ethnic or religious polarisation, local conflicts, diaspora networks.
- Link: personal grievance gets framed as community injustice by extremist narratives; community approval gives legitimacy.
- Indian examples: Left Wing Extremism among displaced tribal communities; youth radicalisation online in J&K; insurgencies in the North-East.
- Push factors (poverty, exclusion) and pull factors (money, status, ideology) operate through both levels.
- Responses: development with dignity (Aspirational Districts), rights-based governance (PESA, Forest Rights Act), de-radicalisation and counselling, community policing, counter-narratives.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's militancy (1980s–early 90s) showed both personal and community grievances; today, gangster–terror links and foreign-based radical handlers target unemployed youth and drug addicts.

*Conclusion:* Linking individual and community factors gives a fuller picture and better prevention strategies.

*Diagram:* Venn diagram: individual factors ∩ community factors = radicalisation zone.

**Q16.** Based on the given data, answer the following questions:  
 | Village X | Village Y  
Average income (in Rs.) | 1750 | 1860  
Standard Deviation of income (in Rs.) | 100 | 81  
Number of Employees | 600 | 500  
(i) In which Village is the variation in income greater?

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Mean/SD/CV numericals recur in PPSC GS-III statistics section.

*Intro:* Compare relative variation with the coefficient of variation (CV), then use the combined SD formula.

*Points:*

- Combined SD = √11,436.8 ≈ ₹106.9.
