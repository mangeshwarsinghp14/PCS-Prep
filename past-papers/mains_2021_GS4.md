**Q1.** Analyse the role of the private sector in proposed Draft Spacecom Policy 2020 for reforming the Indian space sector. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Space-sector opening (IN-SPACe, Space Policy 2023, private launches) is a hot S&T theme.

*Intro:* The Draft Spacecom Policy 2020 (October 2020) aimed to open satellite communication to private players as part of the 2020 space reforms.

*Points:*
- Context: ISRO-centred model; reforms of June 2020 created IN-SPACe (authoriser/promoter) and gave NSIL (2019) a commercial role.
- Draft Spacecom policy: private firms could build, own and operate satcom satellites and ground stations, use Indian or foreign satellites with authorisation, and get spectrum and orbital slots through government.
- Role envisaged for private sector: capital, innovation, manufacturing, services (broadband, DTH, IoT), exports.
- Safeguards: national security, licensing and monitoring by the Department of Space.
- Follow-up: Indian Space Policy 2023 replaced draft policies with one framework; FDI rules liberalised in 2024 (up to 100% in components, higher automatic limits for satellites).
- Results: Skyroot's Vikram-S, first private Indian rocket (November 2022); Agnikul's launch (2024); hundreds of space start-ups; private satcom licences including LEO broadband.
- Challenges: regulatory clarity, spectrum allocation method, funding for deep-tech, dependence on ISRO facilities, liability law (a Space Activities Bill is pending).

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's engineering institutes (PEC Chandigarh, Thapar, IIT Ropar) can feed the space start-up ecosystem; satellite broadband can serve border villages.

*Conclusion:* Private participation, guided by clear regulation, can make India a major player in the global space economy.

*Diagram:* Flowchart: Department of Space → IN-SPACe (authorise) → NSIL (commercial) → private firms.

**Q2.** Discuss the features and significance of the Hypersonic Technology Demonstration Vehicle (HSTDV). *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Indigenous defence technology is recurrent; hypersonic tests continue.

*Intro:* The HSTDV is DRDO's unmanned scramjet demonstrator, successfully flight-tested on 7 September 2020 from Dr APJ Abdul Kalam Island, Odisha.

*Points:*
- Uses an air-breathing scramjet engine that burns fuel in supersonic airflow; no onboard oxidiser.
- Launched on an Agni-based solid booster to about 30 km altitude, then cruised at about Mach 6 under scramjet power for about 20 seconds.
- Made India one of a few countries (after the US, Russia and China) to demonstrate scramjet flight.
- Significance: base for long-range hypersonic cruise missiles that are hard to intercept; low-cost reusable satellite launch; materials and thermal-management know-how.
- Spin-offs: high-temperature materials, guidance and control at hypersonic speed.
- Follow-up: DRDO flight-tested a long-range hypersonic missile in November 2024 and has carried out long-duration scramjet ground tests.
- Concerns: hypersonic arms race, strategic stability with China and Pakistan.

*Punjab:*
- Indigenous defence R&D creates opportunities for Punjab's precision-engineering MSMEs (Mohali, Ludhiana) as suppliers.

*Conclusion:* HSTDV is a milestone in India's strategic and space technology self-reliance.

**Q3.** What are Trans Fats? Describe the recent measures taken up by WHO and FSSAI for their elimination towards healthier food systems. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Lifestyle diseases and food safety recur; trans-fat elimination is ongoing.

*Intro:* Trans fats are unsaturated fats with trans double bonds; industrial trans fats come from partial hydrogenation of oils (vanaspati).

*Points:*
- Health effects: raise LDL and lower HDL cholesterol, increasing heart disease; WHO links industrial trans fats to up to about 5 lakh deaths a year.
- Sources: vanaspati, bakery shortenings, fried snacks, street food reheated in oil.
- WHO: REPLACE action package (2018) aimed at global elimination by 2023; best-practice policies — a limit of 2 g per 100 g of total fat, or a ban on partially hydrogenated oils; validation programme for countries.
- FSSAI: limited trans fat to 3% in oils and fats from January 2021 and 2% from January 2022, including in foods containing them.
- Campaigns: 'Eat Right India' (2018), 'Heart Attack Rewind', 'Trans-fat free' logo.
- Challenges: unorganised sector and street food, reuse of frying oil, testing capacity.
- Way forward: total polar compound limits for used oil, awareness, labelling, inspections.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's food safety wing runs 'Mission Tandarust Punjab' drives and inspections of sweet shops and dhabas, especially during festivals.

*Conclusion:* Eliminating trans fats is a cheap, high-impact public-health step; enforcement in the informal sector is key.

**Q5.** What are Microplastics? Critically analyse Microplastics as an emerging contaminant in nature. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Plastic pollution and the global plastics treaty talks keep this live.

*Intro:* Microplastics are plastic particles smaller than 5 mm, either made small (primary) or formed by breakdown of larger plastics (secondary).

*Points:*
- Sources: microbeads in cosmetics, synthetic textile fibres, tyre wear, fragmented bags and bottles.
- Found everywhere: oceans, rivers, soil, air, Himalayan snow, drinking water, salt; also detected in human blood and placenta.
- Impacts: ingested by plankton, fish and birds; carry toxic chemicals and microbes; may cause inflammation and hormonal effects in humans (research still evolving).
- Hard to remove — persistent, invisible, no treatment at most wastewater plants.
- India: Plastic Waste Management Rules 2016 (amended 2021, 2022), ban on identified single-use plastics from 1 July 2022, Extended Producer Responsibility.
- Global: UN talks for a plastics treaty failed to agree in 2024 (Busan) and 2025 (Geneva).
- Gaps: weak enforcement, informal waste sector, lack of monitoring standards.
- Way forward: reduce production, design for recycling, filters in washing machines, research and monitoring.

*Punjab:*
- Buddha Nullah and Sutlej carry plastic waste from Ludhiana; Punjab banned plastic carry bags (2016) but enforcement is weak.

*Conclusion:* Microplastics are a slow, global threat; prevention at source is more effective than clean-up.

**Q12.** What is Genetic Rescue and how it aids in conservation? Discuss with some suitable examples. *(15 marks, 200 words)*

<details><summary>Model answer points</summary>

*Why it matters:* Specialised conservation-genetics topic; rarely repeated.

*Intro:* Genetic rescue is introducing new individuals (genes) into a small, inbred population to increase its genetic diversity and fitness.

*Points:*
- Small isolated populations suffer inbreeding depression — low fertility, disease, deformities — and lose adaptive potential.
- Gene flow from another population restores heterozygosity and survival.
- Example: Florida panther — eight female Texas pumas released in 1995; kink tails and heart defects declined and numbers rose.
- Isle Royale wolves (USA): new wolves translocated in 2018–19 after the population collapsed through inbreeding.
- Greater prairie chicken (Illinois): birds from other states restored egg hatching rates.
- Risks: outbreeding depression, disease spread, loss of local adaptations — needs genetic study first.
- Indian relevance: tigers in isolated reserves; corridors and translocations act as natural genetic rescue; Project Cheetah brings new founders.

*Punjab:*
- Punjab's small Indus dolphin and hog deer populations in isolated habitats could need genetic monitoring.

*Conclusion:* Genetic rescue, used carefully, can save small populations from extinction vortex.
