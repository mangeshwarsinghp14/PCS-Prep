## Short notes

- Genetic engineering = direct manipulation of an organism's DNA; core tools are restriction enzymes (molecular scissors), DNA ligase, vectors (plasmids, viruses), PCR and gene cloning.
- Recombinant DNA (rDNA) technology produced human insulin in bacteria (first rDNA drug, 1982), hepatitis B vaccine, growth hormone and clotting factors.
- CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing (Doudna and Charpentier, Nobel Chemistry 2020) allows precise, cheap edits; base editing and prime editing are newer refinements.
- Applications in agriculture: Bt crops, herbicide tolerance, genome-edited stress-tolerant varieties, biofortification (Golden Rice with beta-carotene).
- Applications in medicine: gene therapy for inherited disorders (sickle cell disease, thalassaemia, spinal muscular atrophy), CAR-T cell therapy for blood cancers, mRNA vaccines, genetic testing.
- India's first indigenous CAR-T therapy (NexCAR19, IIT Bombay–Tata Memorial–ImmunoACT) was approved by CDSCO in 2023 at a fraction of global cost.
- Genome India Project (DBT) sequenced 10,000 genomes from diverse Indian populations (data released 2025) to support precision medicine.
- National Sickle Cell Anaemia Elimination Mission (2023) targets elimination by 2047 — screening, counselling and future gene therapy relevance.
- Regulation of GMOs: Environment Protection Act 1986 and Rules 1989; GEAC (MoEFCC) approves environmental release; RCGM (DBT) reviews research.
- Stem cells: unspecialised cells that self-renew and differentiate; types — totipotent (zygote), pluripotent (embryonic), multipotent (adult/bone marrow), induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs; Yamanaka, Nobel 2012).
- Established stem-cell therapy: haematopoietic stem cell (bone marrow) transplantation for leukaemia, thalassaemia, aplastic anaemia; umbilical cord blood banking.
- Research uses: regenerative medicine (retina, heart, spinal injury), disease modelling, drug testing, organoids.
- ICMR National Guidelines for Stem Cell Research (2017) permit only approved therapies outside trials; unapproved 'stem-cell clinics' and stem-cell tourism are banned commercially.
- New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules 2019 govern stem-cell and gene-therapy products as new drugs.
- Ethical issues: embryo destruction, germline editing and 'designer babies' (He Jiankui case, China 2018), consent, equity of access, genetic discrimination and data privacy.
- Way forward: clear law on genetic data, affordable indigenous therapies, strong ethics committees, public engagement and biosafety capacity.

## Deep dive

### Tools and milestones — timeline

- 1953 — Watson and Crick's DNA double helix (built on Rosalind Franklin's X-ray data); 1958 — Meselson–Stahl show semi-conservative replication.
- 1968 — Nobel to Khorana, Nirenberg and Holley for the genetic code.
- 1970s — restriction enzymes (Nobel 1978); Paul Berg's first recombinant DNA (1972); Cohen and Boyer clone genes in plasmids (1973); Asilomar conference sets voluntary safety rules (1975).
- 1977 — Sanger sequencing; 1983 — Kary Mullis invents PCR (Nobel 1993).
- 1980 — Diamond v Chakrabarty (US Supreme Court) allows a patent on Indian-born Ananda Chakrabarty's oil-eating bacterium — the first patent on a GM organism.
- 1990–2003 — Human Genome Project (about 3 billion base pairs, about 20,000 protein-coding genes); 1996 — Dolly the sheep cloned by nuclear transfer.
- 1998 — human embryonic stem cells isolated; 2006 — induced pluripotent stem cells (Yamanaka, Nobel 2012).
- 2012 — CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing (Doudna and Charpentier, Nobel 2020); 2018 — He Jiankui's gene-edited babies in China, widely condemned.
- 2023 — Casgevy becomes the first approved CRISPR therapy (UK, then US) for sickle cell disease and beta-thalassaemia.
- 2024 — Nobel in Medicine for microRNA (Ambros, Ruvkun) and in Chemistry for protein design and AI-based structure prediction (Baker; Hassabis, Jumper); 2025 — Nobel in Medicine for peripheral immune tolerance and regulatory T cells (Brunkow, Ramsdell, Sakaguchi).

### Techniques explained (concept → use)

- Vectors: plasmids, bacteriophages, the Ti plasmid of Agrobacterium (plant transformation), and viral vectors such as AAV and lentivirus for gene therapy.
- Gene transfer methods: microinjection, gene gun (biolistics), electroporation and lipid-based delivery.
- Selection uses antibiotic-resistance markers and blue–white screening (insertional inactivation).
- PCR and RT-PCR amplify DNA and RNA (COVID-19 testing); DNA fingerprinting (Alec Jeffreys, 1984) was pioneered in India by Lalji Singh at CCMB; CDFD Hyderabad does forensic DNA work.
- RNA interference (Nobel 2006) silences genes — used for nematode-resistant tobacco and new drugs.
- Base editing and prime editing change single letters without cutting both DNA strands; gene drives can spread a trait through mosquito populations (malaria research).
- Next-generation and nanopore sequencing power genomic surveillance — INSACOG tracked SARS-CoV-2 variants from 2020.
- Synthetic biology designs new cells and pathways; transgenic animals serve as disease models and as 'bioreactors' making medicines in milk.

### India — programmes, firsts and regulation

- DBT (1986), BIRAC (2012) for start-ups, and BRIC (2023), an umbrella council for DBT's autonomous institutes.
- Genome India (launched 2020; data released January 2025): about 10,000 genomes from dozens of population groups, roughly 180 million variants, data held at the Indian Biological Data Centre, Faridabad; about 20 institutions led by IISc's Centre for Brain Research.
- BIRSA-101 (November 2025): CSIR-IGIB's CRISPR (enFnCas9) gene therapy for sickle cell disease, named after Birsa Munda and transferred to Serum Institute for scale-up.
- Haemophilia A: a DBT-supported first-in-human lentiviral gene therapy trial at CMC Vellore reported encouraging results in 2024.
- National Sickle Cell Anaemia Elimination Mission (2023–2047) screens about 7 crore people aged 0–40 in affected, largely tribal, districts.
- GMO regulation under the Environment Protection Act 1986 and the 1989 Rules: IBSC (institution), RCGM (DBT, research), GEAC (MoEFCC, environmental release), and state and district monitoring committees.
- Gene and cell therapies are 'new drugs' under the New Drugs and Clinical Trials Rules 2019; national gene therapy guidelines (2019) prohibit germline gene therapy.
- Related laws: ART (Regulation) Act 2021 and Surrogacy (Regulation) Act 2021 govern embryos and clinics; the PCPNDT Act 1994 bans sex selection; the DNA Technology Regulation Bill 2019 was withdrawn in 2023.

### Stem cells — deeper concepts and applications

- Sources: embryonic (inner cell mass), umbilical cord blood (blood-forming stem cells), bone marrow, fat and dental pulp (mesenchymal), and iPSCs made by reprogramming adult cells with four factors.
- Haematopoietic stem-cell transplants (autologous or allogeneic, needing HLA matching) treat leukaemia, thalassaemia and aplastic anaemia; volunteer registries such as DATRI find unrelated donors.
- India's LV Prasad Eye Institute has long used cultivated limbal stem cells to restore sight after corneal burns.
- ICMR's National Guidelines for Stem Cell Research (2017) treat only blood-stem-cell transplants for blood disorders as standard care; everything else must be a regulated clinical trial, overseen by a national apex committee.
- Organoids and organ-on-chip models reduce animal testing; 2023 amendments to India's clinical trial rules allow such non-animal methods.
- Ongoing frontiers: 3D bioprinting of skin and cartilage; gene-edited pig organs transplanted into humans (2022 onwards).
- Commercial cord-blood banking is licensed by CDSCO, but ICMR discourages routine private banking because the chance of later use is low.
- Risks: tumour formation, immune rejection, high cost and unproven 'stem-cell clinics' selling therapies for autism or ageing.

### Ethics, equity and governance — answer kit

- Somatic editing (affects only the patient) is accepted under regulation; germline editing (heritable) is banned in India and most countries.
- Concerns: designer babies and eugenics, consent from vulnerable or tribal communities, benefit sharing, genetic discrimination in jobs and insurance, and export of Indian genomic data.
- Access: single gene therapies abroad cost over USD 2 million; indigenous platforms (NexCAR19, BIRSA-101) and the National Policy for Rare Diseases 2021 (up to Rs 50 lakh support) aim at affordability.
- Biosafety and biosecurity: dual-use research, gain-of-function studies, and the Biological Weapons Convention.
- FSSAI has proposed rules for GM food approval and labelling; consumer choice and trade rules collide here.
- Lenses for GS-IV links: precautionary principle vs innovation; justice and fairness (Rawls); autonomy and informed consent.

### Punjab angle

- Har Gobind Khorana (born Raipur, undivided Punjab, 1922) built the first synthetic gene; the Khorana Program runs Indo-US research internships for students.
- PGIMER Chandigarh offers bone-marrow transplants, genetic diagnosis and thalassaemia care for Punjab's patients.
- Thalassaemia carriers are common enough in Punjab to make premarital and antenatal screening a sensible public-health step.
- Mohali–Chandigarh: BRIC-NABI (crop and food biotechnology, genome-editing research), CSIR-IMTECH (microbial genomics), IISER Mohali (biology), PAU's School of Agricultural Biotechnology (marker-assisted breeding).
- Bt cotton in Punjab's Malwa belt (Bathinda, Mansa, Fazilka) has suffered from pink bollworm and whitefly, and illegal herbicide-tolerant seed is a regulatory worry.
- Genetic counselling and newborn screening can be added to Punjab's district hospitals and Aam Aadmi Clinics referral chain.

**1.** What is CRISPR-Cas9? Discuss its potential applications in health and agriculture and the ethical concerns it raises. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Define: bacterial immune system adapted for targeted DNA editing.
- Health: sickle cell and thalassaemia therapies, cancer immunotherapy, diagnostics.
- Agriculture: stress-tolerant, high-yield genome-edited crops; India's 2022 exemption for SDN-1/2.
- Ethics: germline editing, off-target effects, equity, consent, biodiversity risk.
- Regulation in India: EPA 1986 rules, RCGM, GEAC, ICMR guidelines.
- Conclusion: harness with strong safeguards.

**2.** Differentiate between embryonic, adult and induced pluripotent stem cells. Why is stem-cell therapy tightly regulated in India? (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Embryonic: pluripotent, ethical issues.
- Adult: multipotent, e.g. bone marrow.
- iPSCs: reprogrammed adult cells, avoid embryo use.
- Regulation: ICMR 2017 guidelines; NDCT Rules 2019; only HSC transplant standard.
- Reason: unproven claims, patient exploitation, safety risks.
- Conclusion: research freedom with patient protection.

</details>

**3.** Genetic engineering can make advanced therapies affordable for India. Discuss with examples and suggest policy measures. (200 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Examples: rDNA insulin, hepatitis B vaccine, NexCAR19 CAR-T.
- Genome India Project and precision medicine.
- Barriers: high cost, limited manufacturing, regulatory delays, skilled workforce.
- Policy: BioE3, PLI for biologics, fast-track regulation, insurance coverage under PM-JAY.
- Conclusion: indigenous biotech for health equity.

</details>
