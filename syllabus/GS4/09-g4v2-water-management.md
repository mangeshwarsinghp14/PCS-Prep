## Short notes

- Scale: India has about 18% of world population but only about 4% of its freshwater resources; per capita availability has fallen below the 1,700 cubic metre 'water-stressed' mark.
- Use pattern: agriculture uses the bulk (roughly 80%) of withdrawals; India is the world's largest user of groundwater, which meets most rural drinking and irrigation needs.
- Groundwater crisis: over-exploitation in north-west India (Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan), with falling water tables and rising pumping costs; CGWB releases periodic Dynamic Ground Water Resource assessments.
- Quality issues: arsenic, fluoride, nitrate, uranium and salinity contamination; untreated sewage and industrial effluents pollute rivers.
- Institutions: Ministry of Jal Shakti (2019), Central Water Commission (surface water), Central Ground Water Board/Authority; water is a State subject (Entry 17, List II) subject to Entry 56, List I (inter-state rivers).
- Policy and law: National Water Policy 2012; Dam Safety Act 2021; Inter-State River Water Disputes Act 1956; Model Groundwater Bill proposals; Mihir Shah Committee (2016) suggested a unified National Water Commission.
- Flagship programmes: Jal Jeevan Mission (2019, functional household tap connections; extended to 2028), Atal Bhujal Yojana (community groundwater management in seven states), Jal Shakti Abhiyan 'Catch the Rain', Mission Amrit Sarovar (2022, 75 ponds per district), PMKSY.
- Urban water: AMRUT 2.0 (2021) for universal tap coverage and water bodies; reuse of treated wastewater; non-revenue water losses are high in Indian cities.
- Conservation methods: rainwater harvesting (rooftop and recharge wells), watershed development, check dams, revival of village ponds, managed aquifer recharge.
- Agricultural efficiency: drip and sprinkler irrigation, laser land levelling, direct seeded rice, alternate wetting and drying, crop diversification away from paddy, tensiometers.
- Pricing and incentives: free/flat-rate farm power encourages over-pumping; direct benefit transfer for power savings is a reform option.
- Community models: Ralegan Siddhi and Hiware Bazar (Maharashtra), Rajasthan's johads (Tarun Bharat Sangh), Pani Panchayats.
- Inter-state disputes: Cauvery, Krishna, Mahadayi, Ravi–Beas and the SYL canal; transboundary: India placed the Indus Waters Treaty (1960) in abeyance in April 2025.
- Measurement: NITI Aayog's Composite Water Management Index (2018) ranks states on water governance.
- Way forward: treat water as an economic and ecological good, aquifer mapping, groundwater regulation with community participation, water budgeting at village level, demand management in agriculture.

## Punjab angle

- Punjab's groundwater extraction exceeds 150% of annual recharge (CGWB assessments), with well over 100 of its 153 assessment blocks over-exploited.
- Laws: Punjab Preservation of Subsoil Water Act 2009 (paddy transplanting dates); Punjab Water Resources (Management and Regulation) Act 2020 set up the Punjab Water Regulation and Development Authority to regulate extraction and charges.
- Paani Bachao Paisa Kamao (pilot from 2018) paid farmers for saving electricity on agricultural feeders; DSR incentive of ₹1,500 per acre.
- SYL canal: Punjab's Termination of Agreements Act 2004 was held invalid by the Supreme Court in a Presidential reference (2016); the dispute with Haryana continues.
- Punjab declared 100% rural household tap-water coverage under Jal Jeevan Mission (2022); uranium and arsenic in parts of Malwa and Majha need treatment plants.

## How PPSC asks it

Punjab's groundwater crisis is a favourite PPSC theme; questions ask causes, consequences and solutions. Use CGWB data trends, laws (2009 and 2020 Acts) and specific methods (DSR, micro-irrigation, recharge).

## Deep dive

### Present scenario — data bank (with year)

- CGWB 2025 assessment: recharge 448.52 BCM, extractable 407.75 BCM, extraction 247.22 BCM; national stage 60.63%; of 6,762 units, 10.8% over-exploited, 2.97% critical, 11.21% semi-critical, 73.14% safe, 1.88% saline.
- Over-exploited units cluster in the north-west (Punjab, Haryana, Delhi, western UP), the west (Rajasthan, Gujarat) and parts of the south (Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, Telangana, Andhra Pradesh).
- Punjab: 156.87% stage and 115 of 153 units over-exploited in 2024; about 152% and 110 over-exploited units in 2025.
- Per-capita water availability: about 1,816 m³ (2001) and 1,545 m³ (2011), projected to fall to about 1,486 m³ (2021) and 1,367 m³ (2031) — India is 'water-stressed'.
- NITI Aayog's Composite Water Management Index (2018) warned of about 600 million people under high to extreme water stress.
- Rainfall is not the main problem: the 2025 south-west monsoon was 108% of normal (IMD), yet floods and groundwater depletion coexisted — the issue is storage, recharge and demand.
- Agriculture uses about 80% or more of withdrawals; irrigation accounts for the bulk of groundwater extraction.

### Institutions, law and reform — critical appraisal

- Constitution: water is a State subject (Entry 17, List II) subject to Entry 56, List I; Article 262 covers inter-state river disputes; courts have read the right to water into Article 21.
- Groundwater rights follow land ownership under the Indian Easements Act 1882, encouraging competitive pumping; Model Groundwater Bills (2011, 2016) propose treating groundwater as a common pool held in public trust.
- Central Ground Water Authority (1997) issues NOCs under 2020 guidelines with water-conservation fees for industrial and commercial users.
- Mihir Shah Committee (2016) recommended merging the CWC and CGWB into a National Water Commission; its draft National Water Policy (2021) has not been adopted, so the 2012 policy still applies.
- Dam Safety Act 2021 and the River Basin Management Bill (2018, lapsed) show the gap between river-basin thinking and state-wise administration.
- Water regulators: Maharashtra's MWRRA (2005) was the first; Punjab set up the Punjab Water Regulation and Development Authority under its 2020 Act.
- Fifteenth Finance Commission tied 60% of rural local-body grants to drinking water, rainwater harvesting and sanitation — a lever for panchayat-level water security.

### Methods of conservation — matrix (method — where — example)

- Rooftop rainwater harvesting — cities — made mandatory for buildings in Tamil Nadu (2003); recharge shafts in parks and along roads.
- Watershed development (ridge to valley) — rainfed areas — Ralegan Siddhi and Hiware Bazar (Maharashtra), Arvari river revival by Tarun Bharat Sangh (Rajasthan; Rajendra Singh won the Stockholm Water Prize in 2015).
- Traditional systems — johad and taanka (Rajasthan), kuhl (Himachal), zabo (Nagaland), eri (Tamil Nadu tanks), phad (Maharashtra), bamboo drip (Meghalaya), stepwells.
- Demand management in farms — drip and sprinklers (Per Drop More Crop), laser land levelling, direct seeded rice, alternate wetting and drying, shift to millets, pulses and maize.
- Urban reuse and loss control — Nagpur sells treated sewage to a thermal plant; cutting non-revenue water (often 30–40% in Indian cities) through metering and leak detection.
- Community programmes — Atal Bhujal Yojana (water security plans in 7 states), Jal Sanchay Jan Bhagidari (2024, recharge structures), Mission Amrit Sarovar (2022, 75 ponds per district), Jal Shakti Abhiyan 'Catch the Rain'.
- Managed aquifer recharge and NAQUIM aquifer maps let plans target the right geology.

### Punjab water management — causes, policies, options

- Causes of depletion: paddy (over 30 lakh ha) in the hottest months, free farm power since 1997, assured MSP procurement for wheat and paddy, and over 14 lakh tube-wells.
- Incentives: ₹1,500 per acre for direct seeded rice; Paani Bachao Paisa Kamao (2018) paid farmers for saving power on metered feeders; promotion of short-duration varieties such as PR-126.
- Canal revival: lining and reopening of minor canals to reach tail-ends — the 2025 data showed groundwater extraction easing as canal irrigation expanded.
- Village ponds: the Thapar and Seechewal models treat village wastewater for irrigation and reduce stagnant-pond health hazards.
- Inter-state: SYL canal and BBMB release disputes; the Indus treaty's abeyance (2025) and Shahpur Kandi dam raise questions of storage for more Ravi water.
- Floods and droughts in one state: the 2025 floods showed the need for floodplain zoning, desilting, stronger dhussi bundhs and better reservoir-operation rules alongside recharge.

### Way forward — frameworks for answers

- Integrated water resources management at basin and aquifer scale, with village water budgets.
- Price and power reform with direct benefit transfer so farmers are paid for saving water rather than charged punitively.
- Crop diversification backed by assured procurement or price deficiency payments for maize, pulses and oilseeds.
- Data: India-WRIS, real-time groundwater level telemetry and public dashboards.
- Circular water economy: mandatory reuse of treated wastewater in industry and thermal power.
- Nature-based solutions: wetlands and floodplains as sponges, catchment forests, revival of traditional tanks and ponds.
- Behaviour change: Jal Jeevan Mission's community water committees and school water literacy.

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** Punjab is heading towards desertification due to groundwater over-exploitation. Analyse the causes and suggest a roadmap for sustainable water management. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: CGWB data — extraction above 150%, majority of blocks over-exploited.
- Causes: paddy monoculture, MSP-procurement bias, free farm power, declining canal share, urban demand.
- Consequences: deeper borewells, costs, quality decline, food security risk.
- Roadmap: diversification with assured markets, DSR and PR-126, micro-irrigation, canal revival to tail ends, recharge structures.
- Governance: PWRDA regulation, metering, DBT for power, village water budgets.
- Conclusion: water security as Punjab's survival issue.

</details>

**2.** Examine the role of Jal Jeevan Mission and Atal Bhujal Yojana in addressing India's water challenges. (200 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- JJM: household tap connections, source sustainability, community ownership (pani samitis).
- Atal Bhujal: demand-side groundwater management in seven states, water security plans.
- Achievements and gaps: coverage vs functionality, source drying, quality testing.
- Way forward: convergence with MGNREGA, recharge, user charges.
- Conclusion: supply and demand management together.

</details>

**3.** Discuss traditional and modern methods of water conservation in India with examples. (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Traditional: johads, baolis, kuhls, tanks, village ponds (Punjab's chhappars).
- Modern: rooftop harvesting, recharge wells, drip and sprinkler, treated wastewater reuse.
- Community examples: Ralegan Siddhi, Hiware Bazar, Rajasthan johads.
- Policy support: Jal Shakti Abhiyan Catch the Rain, Mission Amrit Sarovar, PMKSY micro-irrigation subsidies, building bye-laws mandating rooftop harvesting.
- Conclusion: blend traditional wisdom with technology.

</details>
