## Short notes

- Decision framework for municipal problems: diagnose (service gaps, data, finances) → legal duties → quick wins plus systemic fixes → finance plan → citizen participation → monitoring with service-level benchmarks.
- Constitutional base: 74th Amendment (1992) and Twelfth Schedule (water supply, sanitation, solid waste, slum improvement); State Finance Commissions; in Punjab — Punjab Municipal Act 1911 and Punjab Municipal Corporation Act 1976.
- Solid Waste Management Rules 2016: segregation at source into biodegradable, non-biodegradable and domestic hazardous waste; door-to-door collection; user fees and spot fines through bye-laws; ban on burning and littering; integration of informal waste pickers.
- SWM Rules 2016 duties: bulk waste generators must segregate and process wet waste on-site where feasible; only rejects to go to sanitary landfills; legacy dumps to be bio-mined and remediated.
- Related rules: Plastic Waste Management Rules 2016 (single-use plastic items banned from July 2022; Extended Producer Responsibility), Construction and Demolition Waste Rules 2016, Bio-Medical Waste Rules 2016, E-Waste Rules 2022.
- Waste hierarchy: reduce → reuse → recycle → recover (compost, biogas, RDF) → dispose; decentralised models — Indore (repeatedly ranked cleanest city) and Ambikapur's women-run segregation centres.
- Financing municipal services: own revenue (property tax, user charges, advertisement, fees), transfers (Finance Commission tied grants for sanitation and water, SFC devolution), borrowing (municipal bonds), PPP, land value capture.
- Missions: Swachh Bharat Mission-Urban 2.0 (2021, garbage-free cities), SBM-Gramin Phase II (ODF Plus), AMRUT 2.0 (2021, water and sewerage), Jal Jeevan Mission (rural taps), Mission Amrit Sarovar (ponds).
- Sanitation: ODF → ODF+ → ODF++ (faecal sludge management); Prohibition of Employment as Manual Scavengers and their Rehabilitation Act 2013; NAMASTE scheme for safety of sanitation workers.
- Pollution control: Water Act 1974 and Air Act 1981 — consent to establish and operate from State Pollution Control Board; Environment Protection Act 1986; NGT for enforcement; CETPs for industrial clusters.
- Magistrate's power: BNSS Section 152 (formerly CrPC 133) to remove public nuisance; Municipal Council, Ratlam v Vardhichand (1980) held civic bodies cannot plead lack of funds to avoid sanitation duties.
- Forests and water conservation: Indian Forest Act 1927; Forest (Conservation) Act 1980 as amended 2023; Forest Rights Act 2006; Compensatory Afforestation Fund Act 2016; village pond renovation and rainwater harvesting.
- Urban slums and housing: PMAY-Urban 2.0 (2024) — in-situ redevelopment, affordable rental housing, interest subsidy; Olga Tellis (1985) linked shelter to the right to livelihood; Ajay Maken v Union of India (Delhi HC, 2019) required survey and rehabilitation before eviction.
- Rural water quality: field test kits with trained women, laboratory network, community water purification plants where arsenic, fluoride or uranium exceed limits.
- Common failures: no segregation, unpaid contractors, weak user-fee collection, dumps catching fire, encroachment on water bodies, untreated sewage in drains.
- Ethical lens: environmental justice — the poor live next to dumps and drains; intergenerational equity; dignity of sanitation workers.

## Punjab angle

- The NGT imposed environmental compensation of ₹2,080 crore on Punjab (2022) for failures in solid and liquid waste management.
- Large legacy dumps exist at Tajpur Road (Ludhiana), Wariana (Jalandhar) and Bhagtanwala (Amritsar); bio-mining is under way at some sites.
- Buddha Nullah in Ludhiana receives dyeing-industry and sewage effluents; rejuvenation needs CETPs and sewage treatment plus enforcement.
- Village ponds: the Thapar model and Seechewal model of low-cost pond wastewater treatment are used in Punjab; encroachment on shamlat land is handled under the Punjab Village Common Lands (Regulation) Act 1961.
- Punjab Slum Dwellers (Proprietary Rights) Act 2020 (Basera scheme) gives ownership rights to eligible slum dwellers on state land; Punjab reported 100% rural tap connections under JJM in 2022.

## How PPSC asks it

Cases revolve around garbage crises, polluted drains/ponds and slum evictions. Reward: immediate relief, correct rules (SWM 2016, BNSS 152, PMAY-U 2.0), financing ideas and citizen participation, with humane handling of the poor.

## Deep dive

### Legal and institutional map for urban and rural services in Punjab

- Urban local bodies: Punjab Municipal Act 1911 (municipal councils and nagar panchayats) and Punjab Municipal Corporation Act 1976 (13 municipal corporations, including Amritsar, Ludhiana, Jalandhar, Patiala, Bathinda, Mohali and Phagwara).
- Agencies: Department of Local Government; Punjab Municipal Infrastructure Development Company (PMIDC) as nodal agency for urban missions; Punjab Water Supply and Sewerage Board; Department of Water Supply and Sanitation (rural); Punjab Pollution Control Board.
- Magistrate and regulator powers: BNSS S.152 (removal of public nuisance), Environment Protection Act 1986 Section 5 (directions, including closure), Water Act 1974 Section 33A and Air Act 1981 Section 31A (closure or stopping power supply), NGT Act Section 15 (relief and compensation).
- Finance Commission conditions: the 15th FC tied part of its local-body grants to sanitation and water, and made states notify minimum property-tax floor rates before ULBs could get grants — a lever for municipal finance reform.
- Constitutional duty: the 74th Amendment and the Twelfth Schedule assign water supply, sanitation, solid waste, slum improvement and urban forestry to ULBs; the 2nd ARC 6th Report (Local Governance, 2007) recommended devolving funds, functions and functionaries, and setting up metropolitan planning committees.
- Rural: gram panchayats under the Punjab Panchayati Raj Act 1994; village water and sanitation committees under JJM; the Punjab Village Common Lands (Regulation) Act 1961 governs shamlat land, including village ponds.

### Solid waste — technical and financial design

- Indian municipal waste is roughly half wet organic matter with low calorific value. Mixed-waste incineration usually fails; segregation at source makes composting, biogas and recycling viable.
- Processing options: home and community composting, windrow and vermi-composting, biomethanation and compressed biogas (SATAT 2018; GOBARdhan), material recovery facilities for dry waste, refuse-derived fuel for co-processing in cement kilns, and sanitary landfills for inert rejects only.
- Legacy dumps: bio-mining (trommel screening) and bio-remediation; SBM-Urban 2.0 aims to remediate all legacy dumpsites. Punjab's large dumps are at Tajpur Road (Ludhiana), Wariana (Jalandhar) and Bhagtanwala (Amritsar).
- Bulk waste generators (100 kg a day or more, under the 2016 Rules) must segregate and process wet waste on site; this is an enforcement priority with clear targets such as hotels, marriage palaces and institutions.
- Revenue: user charges notified in bye-laws, spot fines, GIS-based property-tax surveys, sale of compost and recyclables, carbon or compressed-biogas revenue; tie payments to contractors to tonnage that is actually processed, not just tonnage collected.
- People: waste pickers' ID cards and their integration into MRFs; NAMASTE (extended to waste pickers in 2024) for safety and welfare; women's SHGs running segregation centres (Ambikapur model).
- Swachh Survekshan (since 2016) ranks cities. Indore's long run at the top came from strict segregation, 100% door-to-door collection and decentralised processing. A 'Super Swachh League' for repeat toppers was added in Swachh Survekshan 2024-25 (results July 2025).

### Water, sewage, rivers, forests and air — Punjab focus

- Groundwater: CGWB assessments put Punjab's extraction well above annual recharge, the highest stage of extraction in India. The Punjab Water Resources (Management and Regulation) Act 2020 set up a regulator that charges industrial and commercial users.
- Water quality: arsenic and uranium in parts of Malwa and Majha groundwater (CGWB, state studies); community RO plants are a stop-gap; canal-based surface water supply schemes (World Bank and AIIB co-financed for Amritsar and Ludhiana) are the long-term fix.
- Buddha Nullah (Ludhiana): dyeing-industry and sewage pollution feeding the Sutlej. Remedies under way: CETPs for dyeing clusters, new STPs, ending direct discharges, and online effluent monitoring; enforcement by PPCB under NGT oversight.
- Kali Bein (Kapurthala/Sultanpur Lodhi): community kar-seva restoration led by Sant Balbir Singh Seechewal from 2000 — a model of people's participation; his low-cost 'Seechewal model' treats village pond water for irrigation.
- Stubble burning: the Commission for Air Quality Management Act 2021 lets authorities levy environmental compensation per incident, scaled by farm size (the amounts were doubled in November 2024). In-situ machines (Happy Seeder, Super Seeder), ex-situ use (biomass power, CBG, pellets for thermal plants) and paddy diversification are the policy mix.
- Forests: Punjab has one of the lowest forest covers in India (only a few per cent of its area). Hill areas are protected by the Punjab Land Preservation Act 1900 (PLPA), and T.N. Godavarman (1996) gave 'forest' its dictionary meaning. The Forest (Conservation) Amendment Act 2023 renamed the 1980 law the Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam.
- Wetlands: Punjab's Ramsar sites include Harike, Kanjli, Ropar, Nangal, the Beas Conservation Reserve and Keshopur-Miani. Harike, at the Beas–Sutlej confluence, is the most important.

### Urban slums, housing and street vendors

- Rights framework: Olga Tellis (1985) linked livelihood to the right to life; Sudama Singh (Delhi HC, 2010) and Ajay Maken (Delhi HC, 2019) require a survey and rehabilitation before eviction and treat the right to housing as part of dignity.
- Punjab Slum Dwellers (Proprietary Rights) Act 2020 ('Basera'): eligible slum dwellers on state land get ownership rights, with in-situ upgrading where feasible.
- PMAY-Urban 2.0 (2024): 1 crore urban houses through four verticals — beneficiary-led construction, affordable housing in partnership, affordable rental housing, and an interest subsidy scheme. Affordable Rental Housing Complexes (2020) target migrant workers.
- Street Vendors (Protection of Livelihood and Regulation of Street Vending) Act 2014: a Town Vending Committee (40% vendors, a third of them women), a survey and certificate before any eviction, natural markets recognised. PM SVANidhi (2020) provides small working-capital loans.
- Night shelters and services for the homeless (DAY-NULM shelter scheme); urban health and wellness centres; anganwadis in slums.
- Good practice: in-situ upgrading over relocation; land titling; community toilets maintained by SHGs; using MGNREGA-like urban wage programmes during crises.

### Worked cases set in Punjab (approach in brief)

- Case 1 — Ludhiana, Tajpur Road dump fire; residents protest the smoke. Immediately: fire tenders, cover and dampen the dump, health camps and an advisory for children and asthma patients. Medium term: stop fresh dumping, award a bio-mining contract, set up MRFs and ward-level composting. Accountability: NGT compliance report, BNSS S.152 order against negligent agencies, public updates.
- Case 2 — Ludhiana dyeing units discharge into Buddha Nullah at night. Joint PPCB–police inspections with videography; closure orders under Water Act S.33A against repeat violators; online effluent monitoring; check CETP capacity and connection deadlines; compliance help for small units — strict but fair, so compliant units are not harassed.
- Case 3 — Bathinda: jhuggis on railway land are to be cleared for a project. Survey households; serve notice; rehabilitate under Basera/PMAY or ARHC; keep children in school; avoid monsoon and exam season; women's safety; coordinate with Railways; no demolition without an alternative, as courts require.
- Case 4 — a Patiala-district village: the shamlat pond is encroached and stinking. Start eviction proceedings under the 1961 Act; restore the pond with the Thapar or Seechewal treatment model; desilt with MGNREGA labour; involve the gram sabha in maintenance; plant trees on the banks.
- Case 5 — ethics: a contractor offers a kickback to tweak garbage-tender conditions. Refuse, record and report to the Vigilance Bureau, keep the e-procurement terms transparent, and pay against tonnage processed and checked by an independent verifier.
- Case 6 — Sangrur: stubble fires keep breaking out in a village despite fines. Map the farms; deploy Super Seeders through the cooperative society and custom hiring centres (free or subsidised for small farmers); line up a buyer for bales (biomass plant or CBG unit); use the gurdwara and panchayat for peer pressure; levy fines only on repeat offenders after giving machines; reward zero-burning panchayats.

### Principles, case law and traps

- Environmental principles: polluter pays and precautionary principle (Vellore Citizens' Welfare Forum, 1996); public trust doctrine (M.C. Mehta v Kamal Nath, 1997); sustainable development; intergenerational equity; subsidiarity (services at the lowest competent level).
- Ratlam (1980): a municipality cannot plead lack of money to avoid its sanitation duty, and a magistrate can compel it under the nuisance provision.
- Ethical anchors: dignity of sanitation workers (end manual scavenging under the 2013 Act), equity in service coverage for slums, honesty in Swachh rankings (no 'cosmetic' clean-ups before inspections).
- Trap: the 2016 SWM Rules apply beyond municipal limits — census towns, notified areas, railways, airports, SEZs, places of pilgrimage.
- Trap: ODF = no open defecation; ODF+ = toilets maintained and solid/liquid waste managed; ODF++ = faecal sludge and septage also safely managed.
- Trap: AMRUT 2.0 aims at universal tap water in all statutory towns and sewerage in the 500 AMRUT cities; JJM is the rural mission (tap connection for every rural household).

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** You are SDM and also Administrator of a Municipal Council in Sangrur district. The town's dumping site near a residential colony has caught fire for the third time this month; residents have blocked the main road; the waste contractor has not been paid for five months; no segregation happens; and the council's own revenue covers barely half of its salary bill. The NGT has sought a compliance report. What short-term and long-term measures will you take?

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Immediate: fire brigade and water tankers, health camp for residents, temporary halt of fresh dumping, meet protesters with a written timeline.
- Short-term: clear contractor dues partly via available grants against performance, start ward-wise segregation pilots, issue SWM bye-law notice on user fee and fines.
- Systemic: decentralised composting pits and Material Recovery Facility, tie-up with recyclers, waste pickers formalised, bulk generators to process on-site.
- Legacy waste: bio-mining with SBM-U 2.0 support; capping.
- Finance: property tax survey and digitisation, user charges, 15th FC tied grants, cost recovery from compost/recyclables.
- IEC: schools, RWAs, religious places; ward champions.
- Compliance report to NGT with dated action plan.
- Conclusion: segregation plus finance plus participation.

</details>

**2.** As SDM in Moga district, you receive repeated complaints that a village pond has become a cesspool of sewage, overflowing into houses and causing skin and stomach infections. Part of the pond area has been encroached upon by influential families, and the panchayat is divided. How will you resolve the problem?

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Immediate: health camp, chlorination of drinking water, temporary pumping of overflow.
- Legal: notice for nuisance removal under BNSS Section 152; encroachment proceedings under the Punjab Village Common Lands (Regulation) Act 1961, applied without bias.
- Technical fix: Thapar or Seechewal pond-treatment model; convergence with MGNREGA, SBM-G greywater management and 15th FC grants.
- Treated water for irrigation to give farmers a stake.
- Consensus: gram sabha meeting, involve women and SHGs, transparent measurement.
- Maintenance: user committee and O&M fund.
- Conclusion: lawful, participatory and sustainable solution.

</details>

**3.** The High Court has ordered removal of encroachments along a canal bank in Ludhiana for a road-widening project. About 300 families, mostly migrant labourers, live there; the monsoon is approaching and children's school exams are due next month. Local politicians oppose the drive. As the officer responsible, how will you comply with the order while protecting the families' dignity?

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Respect for the court order is non-negotiable; humane execution is the officer's duty.
- Survey and biometric listing of families; eligibility under PMAY-U 2.0 and state schemes; transit accommodation or rental housing.
- Timing: seek the court's approval for a phased schedule after exams and avoiding peak monsoon if justified.
- Advance notice, dialogue with residents, NGOs and labour department; school transfer support; ration portability (ONORC).
- Execution: no night drives, women officers present, belongings protected, videography.
- Long-term: affordable rental housing for migrant workers; prevent fresh encroachments.
- Conclusion: rule of law with compassion (Olga Tellis, Ajay Maken).

</details>
