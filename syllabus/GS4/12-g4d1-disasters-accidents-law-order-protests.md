## Short notes

- Decision framework for any crisis (use as answer skeleton): 1) Secure life and safety first; 2) Establish facts and verify information; 3) Identify stakeholders and their interests; 4) Locate legal mandate and powers; 5) List options with pros and cons; 6) Choose the least restrictive effective option (proportionality); 7) Communicate transparently; 8) Follow up, document and fix accountability.
- Values to show: impartiality, rule of law, empathy for victims, courage under pressure, transparency, non-partisanship, and dialogue before force.
- Disaster Management Act 2005: DDMA (Section 25) chaired by Deputy Commissioner; Section 30 lists DDMA functions; Section 34 gives powers during a threatening disaster (evacuation, traffic control, removal of debris, search and rescue, shelter, food, medical aid).
- DM Act Section 65 allows requisition of resources, premises and vehicles for rescue; Sections 51 and 54 penalise obstruction and false alarms/warnings.
- Incident Response System (NDMA guidelines): the Deputy Commissioner is the Responsible Officer; an Incident Commander (often SDM at site) runs operations, planning, logistics; unified command with police, health, fire, NDRF/SDRF and Army.
- Relief norms: SDRF/NDRF norms fix minimum relief (e.g. ex gratia of ₹4 lakh per deceased person); states may add top-ups from their own budget.
- BNSS 2023 Section 163 (formerly CrPC 144): District Magistrate, SDM or other empowered Executive Magistrate may issue an order in urgent cases of nuisance or apprehended danger; ordinarily valid up to two months, extendable by State Government up to six months.
- Anuradha Bhasin v Union of India (2020): prohibitory orders and internet suspensions must be reasoned, proportionate, published and periodically reviewed, and cannot be used to suppress legitimate expression.
- BNSS Sections 148–149 (formerly CrPC 129–130): Executive Magistrate or officer in charge may order an unlawful assembly to disperse and use civil force; armed forces may be requisitioned by the senior-most Executive Magistrate present when necessary.
- Use of force must be graded and minimum: warning and announcement, water cannon, tear gas, lathi charge, then other measures only as last resort; record by videography; magisterial inquiry thereafter where deaths occur.
- Right to protest: Articles 19(1)(a) and (b) subject to reasonable restrictions for public order; Ramlila Maidan case (2012) on state excesses; Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan (2018) on balancing protest with residents' rights; Amit Sahni (2020, Shaheen Bagh) — public roads cannot be occupied indefinitely.
- Damage to property: Prevention of Damage to Public Property Act 1984; Supreme Court guidelines in In re Destruction of Public and Private Properties (2009) and Kodungallur Film Society (2018) fix liability on organisers.
- Internet suspension is governed by the Telecommunications Act 2023 and its suspension rules; orders come from the Union or State Home Secretary, not the SDM — the SDM can recommend with reasons.
- Riot control and prevention: intelligence from beat staff, peace committees with religious and community leaders, flag marches, rumour control on social media, preventive action against known trouble-makers under BNSS security provisions.
- Road and industrial accidents: golden-hour care; Good Samaritan protection under Motor Vehicles Act Section 134A; cashless treatment for road accident victims (national scheme, 2025); hit-and-run compensation scheme (2022); factory and fire safety inspections.
- Communication: single official spokesperson, regular media briefings, helpline numbers, fact-checks against rumours, respectful handling of victims' families.
- Post-incident: magisterial or departmental inquiry, compensation, rehabilitation, review of SOPs, accountability of negligent officials and owners.

## Punjab angle

- 2023 and 2025 floods: SDMs ran evacuation, relief camps and fodder supply with Army, NDRF and SDRF; breaches in dhussi bundhs along the Sutlej, Beas and Ghaggar were key flashpoints.
- Farmers' protest at Shambhu and Khanauri borders (February 2024 to March 2025) blocked national highways for over a year before Punjab Police cleared the sites — a case in balancing protest rights and public inconvenience.
- Sacrilege incidents (Bargari, 2015) and the Behbal Kalan police firing show how mishandled protests escalate; sensitive handling of religious sentiment is essential in Punjab.
- Punjab's Sadak Suraksha Force (2024) patrols highways for accident response; the state's Farishtey scheme offers free treatment for road accident victims and rewards Good Samaritans.
- Industrial/accident disasters: Giaspura gas leak, Ludhiana (2023); Batala factory blast (2019); Joda Phatak train accident, Amritsar (2018).

## How PPSC asks it

PPSC case studies test what an SDM would actually do, in sequence, within the law. Reward: immediate life-saving steps, correct legal powers (DM Act, BNSS 163/148), balanced options, communication, and long-term prevention — not moral lectures.

## Deep dive

### SDM's legal toolkit — BNSS 2023 sections (old CrPC in brackets)

- S.126 (107) — Executive Magistrate may require a bond for keeping the peace from a person likely to cause a breach of peace; S.128 (109) and S.129 (110) — security for good behaviour from suspected persons and habitual offenders.
- S.148 (129) — Executive Magistrate or police officer in charge may order an unlawful assembly to disperse and use civil force; S.149 (130) — use of armed forces on the requisition of the senior-most Executive Magistrate present; S.151 (132) — prosecution of officers for acts done in good faith needs sanction.
- S.152 (133) — conditional order to remove a public nuisance (obstruction of a public way, dangerous trades, unsafe structures, unfenced wells). Municipal Council, Ratlam v Vardhichand (1980) turned this into a tool for civic duties.
- S.163 (144) — urgent order in cases of nuisance or apprehended danger; up to two months, and the State Government can extend it up to six months. It must be reasoned and proportionate (Anuradha Bhasin, 2020; Ramlila Maidan, 2012).
- S.164 (145) — dispute over land or water likely to cause a breach of peace: the magistrate decides who is in actual possession; S.165 (146) — attachment of the disputed property; S.166 (147) — disputes over a right to use land or water.
- S.172 (new in BNSS) — persons are bound to obey lawful police directions, and police may detain those who refuse; S.170 (151) — arrest to prevent cognisable offences.
- S.196 (176) — inquiry into the cause of death: a Judicial Magistrate must inquire into deaths, disappearances or rape in police custody; Executive Magistrates inquire in other cases, such as suicide or suspicious death of a woman within seven years of marriage.
- BNS offences that come up in public-order cases: S.189 (unlawful assembly), S.191 (rioting), S.196 (promoting enmity, old IPC 153A), S.298–299 (injuring a place of worship; deliberately outraging religious feelings, old 295/295A).
- Other laws: Prevention of Damage to Public Property Act 1984 (the Supreme Court in Kodungallur Film Society, 2018, held protest organisers liable for damage); Telecommunications Act 2023 Section 20 and the 2024 Temporary Suspension Rules, for internet shutdowns with a review committee.

### Disaster Management Act 2005 as amended in 2025 — what changed

- The Disaster Management (Amendment) Bill passed the Rajya Sabha on 25 March 2025 and the Lok Sabha (final passage) on 27 March 2025 (PRS).
- Statutory status for the National Crisis Management Committee (chaired by the Cabinet Secretary) as the nodal body for major disasters, and for the High-Level Committee (which approves central assistance and mitigation-fund spending).
- States may create Urban Disaster Management Authorities for state capitals and cities with municipal corporations: the Municipal Commissioner chairs and the District Collector is vice-chair.
- States may constitute a State Disaster Response Force by law; NDMA and SDMAs must keep national and state disaster databases (risk, funds, plans).
- NDMA can assess state preparedness and audit preparedness and response after a disaster; NDMA and SDMAs now prepare the plans themselves and must periodically assess emerging risks, including climate risks.
- Old powers an SDM still uses: Sec 30 (DDMA functions), Sec 33 (requisition of officers), Sec 34 (powers of the competent authority during a threatening disaster), Sec 65 (requisition of premises and vehicles), Sec 51–60 (penalties for obstruction, false claims, false warnings, misappropriation, failure of officers).

### Running a response: Incident Response System, money and relief norms

- Incident Response System (NDMA guidelines, July 2010): the Deputy Commissioner is the Responsible Officer; Incident Response Teams at district and sub-division level (the SDM leads at sub-division). Command staff: Information and Media Officer, Safety Officer, Liaison Officer. General staff: Operations, Planning, Logistics, Finance/Administration sections.
- IRS principles: unified command (civil, police, Army, NDRF/SDRF), a manageable span of control (about 3–7 people reporting to one supervisor, ideally 5), one incident action plan per operational period, and standard terminology.
- Preparedness checklist before the monsoon: update the District Disaster Management Plan, inspect embankments and drains, stock sandbags, boats, life jackets and fodder, mock drills, list vulnerable people (elderly, disabled, pregnant women), pre-identify relief camps with toilets.
- Finance: SDRF (Centre 75%, state 25%; 90:10 for NE and Himalayan states) and NDRF top-ups; the 15th Finance Commission created mitigation funds (SDMF/NDMF) and split the national disaster pool roughly 80:20 between response and mitigation.
- Relief norms (MHA norms for 2022–26): ₹4 lakh ex gratia per death; agricultural input subsidy by crop type (rain-fed, irrigated, perennial) for crop loss of 33% or more; house damage and livestock norms. States top up from their own budget.
- Sendai Framework 2015–2030: four priorities (understand risk, strengthen risk governance, invest in resilience, improve preparedness and 'build back better') and seven global targets; India's PM gave a 10-point agenda in 2016.
- After a disaster: post-disaster needs assessment, speedy girdawari (crop-loss survey), restoring livelihoods (seed, fodder, desilting fields), psychosocial care (NDMA mental-health guidelines), and an inquiry into avoidable causes.

### Punjab hazard profile and lessons from the 2023 and 2025 floods

- Hazards: riverine floods (Sutlej, Beas, Ravi, Ghaggar) and flash floods in the Kandi 'choes'; heatwaves; cold-wave fog that causes highway pile-ups; industrial chemical accidents (Ludhiana); stubble-smoke episodes; lightning; seismic zones III–IV in the north.
- 2023: July rains and August reservoir releases caused breaches along the Ghaggar (Sangrur, Patiala) and the Sutlej–Beas; dhussi bundh failures and illegal mining near embankments came under scrutiny.
- 2025: the August–September floods were among the worst in decades, and the state declared all districts flood-affected. Gurdaspur, Amritsar, Ferozepur, Fazilka and Kapurthala were hit hardest. Debate followed over dam-release timing and damage to headworks on the Ravi.
- Dam governance: the Bhakra Beas Management Board (Punjab Reorganisation Act 1966, Section 79) controls releases. Disputes with Haryana over water releases (2025) and over BBMB membership rules show how interstate politics overlaps with disaster management. The Dam Safety Act 2021 created the NCDS and NDSA, with state dam-safety organisations.
- Relief innovations announced by the state in 2025: ₹20,000 per acre compensation for crop loss and the 'Jisda Khet, Usdi Ret' scheme letting farmers sell sand and silt deposited on their fields. Present these as policy choices, and note the risk to the environment.
- Community assets: gurdwaras as relief hubs (langar, shelter), Khalsa Aid and other NGOs, village youth clubs. An SDM should formally bring them into the IRS through the Liaison Officer rather than leaving them to work in parallel.

### Law and order and protests — decision ladder

- Before the event: intelligence inputs, dialogue with organisers, written permission with conditions (route, time, sound limits, number of vehicles), a designated protest site (Mazdoor Kisan Shakti Sangathan, 2018), and joint briefing of magistrates and police.
- Public ways cannot be occupied indefinitely: Amit Sahni (Shaheen Bagh, 2020). The farmers' blockade at Shambhu and Khanauri (2024–25) led to Punjab and Haryana High Court orders and a Supreme Court-appointed committee (headed by Justice Nawab Singh, 2024) — a Punjab example of balancing rights.
- On the day: magistrates on duty with written orders, warnings by loudspeaker in Punjabi, videography, graded force (water cannon → tear gas → lathi charge → other measures only as a last resort), an ambulance corridor, women police for women protesters.
- Communal and sacrilege flashpoints: secure the accused (to prevent lynching) and the site, involve religious leaders (granthi, SGPC members, imams), set up peace (aman) committees, debunk rumours on social media quickly, and give a time-bound investigation update. The Bargari and Behbal Kalan episodes (2015) show the cost of delay and of firing.
- Internet shutdowns: only under the Telecom Act 2023 rules, with a reasoned order, limited area and duration, review by a committee, and publication (Anuradha Bhasin).
- After the event: magisterial inquiry where there is death or serious injury; report custodial deaths to the NHRC within 24 hours; compensation; recover damages under the 1984 Act; lessons-learned review.

### Worked cases set in Punjab districts (approach in brief)

- Case 1 — Ferozepur, 2 a.m.: a Sutlej dhussi bundh is breaching near a village. Activate the sub-division IRT; sound sirens and gurdwara loudspeakers; evacuate to pre-identified schools (women, children, elderly first; cattle to high ground); request the Army or NDRF through the DC; plug the breach with the Drainage department; keep a log; next morning start the girdawari and order an inquiry into maintenance lapses and illegal mining.
- Case 2 — Moga: rumour of sacrilege at a village gurdwara; a crowd surrounds the suspect's house. Life first: shift the suspect and family to safety under police cover; seal and preserve the scene; register an FIR (BNS 298/299); bring in the granthi, sarpanch and SGPC representative; issue a limited S.163 order only if needed; announce the investigation timeline; avoid force unless violence starts.
- Case 3 — Rajpura (NH-44): farmers block the highway over MSP. Meet the leaders; forward their memorandum to the state and Centre; offer a nearby designated site; keep one lane open for ambulances and essential supplies; prepare graded options with legal cover (S.152/S.163) but treat force as a last resort; keep communication transparent.
- Case 4 — Ludhiana (Giaspura-type gas leak): cordon off the area and evacuate upwind; call NDRF chemical teams and PPCB to identify the gas (hydrogen sulphide from sewers is suspected); triage at the Civil Hospital; flush and inspect sewers; fix liability (Public Liability Insurance Act 1991, Environment Protection Act); order an audit of industrial discharges into sewers.
- Case 5 — ethical pressure: a local MLA asks the SDM to send relief first to villages of party supporters. Respond with a needs-based priority list made with patwaris and panchayats, public display of beneficiary lists, polite firmness, a written record of the instruction, and escalation to the DC if the pressure continues.
- Values to name in each case: sanctity of life, impartiality, rule of law, compassion, transparency, courage, accountability.

### Ethical principles and answer framework for GS-IV cases

- Structure: facts and dilemma → stakeholders and their interests → legal powers → options with pros and cons → chosen course with justification → long-term systemic fixes.
- Ethical lenses: duty-based (Kant — never treat people merely as means), consequences (the greatest good, without overriding individual rights), virtue (what would an honest, brave officer do), Gandhi's talisman (the poorest person's welfare), Rawls' difference principle (prioritise the least advantaged).
- Nolan Committee's seven principles of public life (UK, 1995): selflessness, integrity, objectivity, accountability, openness, honesty, leadership — quote them for conduct dilemmas.
- Emotional intelligence in crises: stay calm, empathise with victims, communicate clearly, manage the team's stress and fatigue.
- Pitfalls examiners mark down: choosing extreme options (do nothing / use full force), passing the buck to seniors without acting, ignoring legal powers, moralising without practical steps.
- Cite reports: 2nd ARC 3rd Report (Crisis Management) and 5th Report (Public Order); NDMA's Incident Response System and Minimum Standards of Relief guidelines.

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** You are SDM, Sultanpur Lodhi (Kapurthala). Around midnight during heavy monsoon rain, the Beas breaches a dhussi bundh and water is entering four villages. Many families refuse to leave without their cattle; a local leader is pressing you to cut another embankment to divert water away from his village towards a neighbouring one; NDRF teams will take four hours to arrive. Electricity is down and rumours on WhatsApp claim a dam has burst. What immediate and follow-up actions will you take? Justify your decisions.

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Immediate: activate sub-divisional control room and IRS; alert DC (DDMA chair); mobilise SDRF, police, local boats, divers and gurdwara committees; prioritise elderly, children, pregnant women, disabled.
- Evacuation under DM Act Section 34; arrange cattle evacuation to higher ground and fodder so families agree to move — empathy solves resistance.
- Refuse unauthorised embankment cutting: illegal, shifts risk to others; any breach decision only on technical advice of Water Resources Dept and DC.
- Rumour control: verify with BBMB/WRD, issue official messages via SACHET, loudspeakers, gurdwara announcements; warn under DM Act Section 54.
- Relief camps: food, drinking water, chlorination, medical teams, sanitation, women's privacy, animal care.
- Follow-up: damage assessment (girdawari), SDRF relief, embankment audit, action against illegal mining near bundh.
- Conclusion: life first, fairness to all villages, rule of law and transparency.

</details>

**2.** As SDM, Rajpura (Patiala), you learn that a farmers' union has blocked NH-44 and the adjoining railway line, demanding release of pending crop compensation. Ambulances and goods traffic are stuck; traders threaten counter-protests; senior officers want the road cleared within two hours, and the protest leaders insist on meeting a minister. How will you handle the situation?

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Assess: crowd size, mood, intelligence on outside elements, alternate routes; coordinate with SSP/DSP and Railway Protection Force.
- Immediate humanitarian step: negotiate a green corridor for ambulances; divert traffic.
- Dialogue: meet leaders, verify the compensation claim, get written status from Revenue/Agriculture; offer a time-bound meeting with DC/department.
- Legal tools if talks fail: BNSS Section 163 order around tracks; warnings; graded minimum force under BNSS 148 with videography, never against women/elderly first.
- Prevent escalation with traders: separate groups, peace committee.
- Communicate publicly on diversions and grievance timelines.
- Follow-up: expedite pending payments, fix responsibility for delay; cite Amit Sahni (2020) on public roads.
- Conclusion: protest is a right, blockade is not; resolve the grievance, not just the crowd.

</details>

**3.** You are SDM in a sub-division of Faridkot district. Torn pages of a holy book are found near a village religious place in the morning. By noon a large crowd has gathered, some youths are blocking roads, and videos alleging the involvement of a particular community are spreading on social media. Political leaders are arriving. Describe your course of action to maintain peace and ensure justice.

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Immediate: rush with police, secure the site and evidence respectfully with religious leaders; ensure FIR and forensic examination.
- Calm the crowd: speak with religious and village leaders, assure a fair, time-bound investigation; avoid any provocative force.
- Rumour control: fact-check videos with police cyber cell; recommend to Home Department any targeted, proportionate internet measures only if needed (Anuradha Bhasin).
- Prevent retaliation: protection to the targeted community; flag march; BNSS Section 163 order if gatherings threaten violence.
- Engage political leaders to make joint appeals for peace.
- Follow-up: peace committee meetings, CCTV at religious places, quick charge-sheet, lessons from Bargari–Behbal Kalan.
- Conclusion: impartiality and speed of justice preserve communal harmony.

</details>
