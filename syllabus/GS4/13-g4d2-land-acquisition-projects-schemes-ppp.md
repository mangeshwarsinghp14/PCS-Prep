## Short notes

- Decision framework for projects and schemes: needs assessment → stakeholder consultation → objectives (SMART) → design using Logical Framework (inputs, activities, outputs, outcomes, impact) → feasibility (technical, financial, social, environmental) → DPR and appraisal → implementation plan (PERT/CPM, Gantt) → monitoring (MIS, dashboards) → evaluation and social audit.
- Right to property is a constitutional right under Article 300A; Vidya Devi v State of Himachal Pradesh (2020) — taking land without compensation and due process is unconstitutional; Kolkata Municipal Corporation v Bimal Kumar Shah (2024) listed procedural sub-rights within Article 300A.
- RFCTLARR Act 2013: replaces the Land Acquisition Act 1894; mandates Social Impact Assessment (SIA) with public hearing, expert group appraisal and consent of affected families for private and PPP projects (80% and 70% respectively).
- RFCTLARR safeguards: Section 10 — irrigated multi-cropped land to be acquired only as a demonstrable last resort; Section 40 urgency clause limited to defence, national security and natural calamity emergencies.
- Compensation under RFCTLARR: market value multiplied by a factor (up to 2 in rural areas as notified by the state), plus value of structures and trees, plus 100% solatium; R&R entitlements under the Second Schedule (housing, subsistence allowance, annuity or employment options, transport).
- Other RFCTLARR provisions: Section 24 (lapse and pending cases, interpreted in Indore Development Authority v Manoharlal, 2020); Section 101 (return of land unused for five years); LARR Authority for disputes; compensation norms extended to central Acts such as the National Highways Act 1956.
- Better practice in acquisition: transparent rates, prior consultation, special help for small and marginal farmers and landless labourers, timely payment into bank accounts, grievance camps, alternatives like land pooling with fair terms.
- Flagship scheme implementation issues: delayed fund flow, state-share delays, inclusion and exclusion errors, weak monitoring, poor convergence across departments, low awareness, capture by intermediaries.
- Tools: Direct Benefit Transfer and PFMS; Single Nodal Agency mechanism for centrally sponsored scheme funds; geo-tagging; PRAGATI reviews (since 2015); PM Gati Shakti National Master Plan (2021) for infrastructure coordination.
- Aspirational Districts Programme (2018) and Aspirational Blocks Programme (2023): convergence, collaboration and competition based on real-time indicators — a model for district-level target achievement.
- Accountability: social audit (mandatory under MGNREGA Section 17), citizen charters, public display of beneficiary lists, Right to Information.
- PPP models: BOT, BOOT, DBFOT, Hybrid Annuity Model (HAM, used in highways since 2016), EPC (not PPP, public-funded), O&M contracts, management contracts, leases.
- PPP support: Viability Gap Funding scheme, India Infrastructure Project Development Fund, National Monetisation Pipeline (2021); Kelkar Committee (2015) on revisiting PPPs recommended better risk sharing and an independent regulator.
- PPP in service delivery: Pradhan Mantri National Dialysis Programme (2016), emergency ambulance services, diagnostics, waste processing, e-governance kiosks.
- PPP pitfalls: aggressive bidding, poor risk allocation, renegotiation, user-fee resistance, weak contract monitoring, profit-driven neglect of poor users.
- Safeguards: output-based contracts with service-level KPIs, penalties, escrow accounts, independent audits, citizen feedback, exit clauses and protection for the vulnerable.

## Punjab angle

- Punjab's Land Pooling Policy 2025 for urban estates was withdrawn in August 2025 after farmer protests — a lesson in consultation and trust.
- Highway projects such as the Delhi–Amritsar–Katra Expressway and Bharatmala corridors faced delays in land possession in Punjab over compensation disputes.
- Moga and Ferozepur are Aspirational Districts; border blocks face special delivery challenges.
- Most Punjab farmers are small and medium holders, so loss of even a few acres threatens livelihoods; land is also tied to identity and social status.
- Punjab closed several toll plazas on state roads (2022–23), showing the politics of user charges in PPP road projects.

## How PPSC asks it

Cases test an officer's ability to reconcile development deadlines with rights of land losers and beneficiaries. Reward: correct RFCTLARR provisions, stakeholder-wise solutions, concrete implementation tools (DBT, dashboards, social audit) and PPP contract safeguards.

## Deep dive

### RFCTLARR Act 2013 — procedure step by step (Collector/SDM view)

- Sec 4 — Social Impact Assessment (SIA) study notified after consulting the panchayat or municipality; normally completed within six months. Sec 5 — public hearing in the affected area.
- Sec 7 — an independent multidisciplinary Expert Group appraises the SIA within two months and may advise dropping the project if costs outweigh benefits. Sec 8 — the government examines the proposal and records its reasons.
- Sec 11 — preliminary notification, published in local language, panchayat office and websites; from that date no transactions in the land are allowed without permission. It must follow within 12 months of the SIA appraisal, or a fresh SIA is needed.
- Sec 15 — objections within 60 days, heard by the Collector; Sec 16 — survey and draft Rehabilitation and Resettlement (R&R) scheme by the Administrator; Sec 19 — final declaration, which must come within 12 months of the preliminary notification.
- Sec 23 — Collector's award within 12 months of the declaration, otherwise the whole proceeding lapses (Sec 25). Sec 26 — market value is the highest of: the guideline/circle rate, the average of the top 50% of recent sale deeds in the vicinity, or the consented amount (for private or PPP projects).
- Sec 26–30 — market value × a multiplier (1 to 2 in rural areas, depending on distance from urban areas, as notified by the state) + value of buildings and trees + 100% solatium; 12% a year extra on market value from the preliminary notification to the award.
- Sec 38 — possession only after full compensation is paid (within three months) and R&R entitlements are given (within six months); Sec 40 — urgency is limited to defence, national security and natural calamities.
- Sec 64 — reference to the Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Authority; Sec 74 — appeal to the High Court. Sec 96 — compensation is free of income tax and stamp duty. Sec 101 — land unused for five years returns to the owner or the land bank.
- Sec 105 and the Fourth Schedule — 13 central laws (such as the National Highways Act 1956 and the Railways Act 1989) are outside the Act's procedure, but a 2015 order extended its compensation and R&R norms to them.

### Highways, consent and Punjab-specific law

- National Highways Act 1956, Sections 3A–3J: notification by the Competent Authority for Land Acquisition (CALA, usually an SDM or Additional DC); disputes over compensation go to an arbitrator (often the Divisional Commissioner).
- Union of India v Tarsem Singh (2019) — a Punjab case: the Supreme Court held that solatium and interest must also be paid for highway acquisitions, removing an unequal treatment. Cite it in any Punjab highway case.
- Consent rule: 80% of affected families for private projects, 70% for PPP projects; none for the government's own projects. Section 10: irrigated multi-crop land only as a demonstrable last resort, with equivalent wasteland to be developed for farming.
- Several states amended the Act for their own projects under Article 254(2) (with presidential assent) to exempt categories from the SIA and consent rules; the central 2014–15 ordinances that tried to do this nationally lapsed.
- Punjab context: small holdings, high land values and land as social identity make acquisition contentious. The Land Pooling Policy 2025 was withdrawn after farmer protests; highway corridors (the Delhi–Amritsar–Katra Expressway, Bharatmala routes) saw possession delays over rate disputes.
- Alternatives to compulsory acquisition: negotiated purchase at fair rates, land pooling with developed-plot return, long-term lease with annuity, Transferable Development Rights (TDR), and using government land banks first.

### Rehabilitation and SIA — doing it humanely

- Second Schedule entitlements for affected families: a house for those who lose one; a choice of a job, a one-time ₹5 lakh, or an annuity of ₹2,000 a month for 20 years; a subsistence grant of ₹3,000 a month for a year; transport help of ₹50,000; a one-time resettlement allowance of ₹50,000; extra benefits for SC/ST families.
- Third Schedule: 25 kinds of infrastructure at resettlement sites — roads, drainage, drinking water, school, health centre, panchayat ghar, places of worship, cremation grounds and others.
- Sec 45 — a project-level R&R Committee for projects of 100 acres or more, with women, SC/ST, panchayat and NGO members; Sec 50 — state monitoring committee.
- Do not forget the 'invisible' affected: landless labourers, tenants and theka (lease) cultivators, artisans, women-headed households, people who depend on common land.
- Michael Cernea's model of displacement risks (World Bank): landlessness, joblessness, homelessness, marginalisation, food insecurity, ill-health, loss of common property, and social disintegration. Use it to structure an R&R answer.
- Good process: share information in Punjabi, joint surveys with drones and patwaris in front of owners, camp-mode payment into bank accounts, help desks, and grievance timelines.

### Designing projects and public–private partnerships

- PPP models: BOT-toll and BOT-annuity; Hybrid Annuity Model (2016: 40% government during construction, 60% private recovered as annuity); EPC (government-funded construction); Toll-Operate-Transfer; InvITs for monetisation; DBFOT. A Swiss challenge lets others match an unsolicited proposal.
- Viability Gap Funding: usually up to 40% of project cost (20% Centre + 20% state or sponsor), with higher support for social infrastructure under the revised scheme.
- Kelkar Committee (2015) on revisiting PPPs: share risks fairly, discourage Swiss challenge, set up independent sector regulators and a dispute tribunal, and protect honest officers. The Prevention of Corruption Act was amended in 2018 to separate bona fide decisions from corruption.
- Pipeline: National Infrastructure Pipeline (2019), National Monetisation Pipeline (2021), PM Gati Shakti National Master Plan (2021), PPP Appraisal Committee (PPPAC) clearance for central projects.
- Punjab: the Punjab Infrastructure Development Board (Punjab Infrastructure (Development and Regulation) Act 2002); closure of several toll plazas (2022–23) shows the political risk of user charges; health and diagnostics PPPs need patient-protection clauses.
- Good contract design: clear KPIs and output specifications, penalties and step-in rights, an independent engineer, termination payments, a public grievance mechanism, and disclosure of contracts under RTI.

### Implementing flagship schemes — field mechanics

- District convergence: DISHA (District Development Coordination and Monitoring Committee, 2016), chaired by the senior-most MP, reviews central schemes; the DC drives the line departments; the SDM runs sub-divisional reviews.
- Money flow: PFMS and the Single Nodal Agency system, moving to 'just-in-time' releases through SNA-SPARSH (2023); this reduces idle balances but needs states to release their share on time.
- Social audit: MGNREGA Section 17 and the Audit of Schemes Rules 2011, with independent state Social Audit Units; Meghalaya passed the first state law on social audit of public services (2017).
- Technology: geo-tagged asset photos (Bhuvan) for MGNREGA and PMAY-G, Aadhaar-based payments (mandatory for MGNREGA from 2024 — watch for exclusions), dashboards, and PRAGATI reviews for big projects.
- Aspirational Districts (Moga and Ferozepur in Punjab): 49 indicators across health and nutrition, education, agriculture, financial inclusion and infrastructure; monthly delta ranking.
- Common failure points: late release of the state share, contractor capture, poor quality of assets, gaps in beneficiary identification, weak maintenance after the asset is built. Remedies: third-party quality checks, community operation-and-maintenance committees, display boards at worksites.

### Worked cases set in Punjab (approach in brief)

- Case 1 — Sangrur/Patiala highway: farmers say the compensation rates are too low and the alignment cuts their fields in two. As CALA: verify sale deeds; explain entitlements under the NH Act and Tarsem Singh (solatium plus interest); add underpasses and service roads; run joint drone-plus-patwari surveys; hold payment camps; send rate disputes to the arbitrator; no forcible possession before payment.
- Case 2 — Ludhiana periphery: an industrial park needs 1,000 acres of irrigated multi-crop land. Apply Section 10 (last resort): offer degraded or government land first; propose land pooling with a developed-plot share; carry out the SIA honestly; secure 80% or 70% consent if the project is private or PPP; plan specifically for landless labourers.
- Case 3 — JJM/PMAY-G in a Fazilka block: complaints of bribes for inclusion and substandard pipes. Display beneficiary lists publicly; order a social audit; take geo-tagged photos and third-party quality tests; recover money and register FIRs; blacklist the contractor; open a helpline.
- Case 4 — a PPP diagnostic centre in a civil hospital refuses free tests to poor patients. Enforce the contract's KPIs and penalties, use step-in rights if needed, put up a patient help desk, publish monthly performance, and review the contract.
- Case 5 — ethical dilemma: a relative of a minister wants the road alignment shifted to raise the value of his land. Refuse, record the request, decide the alignment on technical criteria, and keep a transparent file trail.
- Values to name in these cases: fairness to the weakest affected family, transparency of rates and lists, empathy for loss of land as identity, rule of law over expediency, integrity under political pressure.

### Principles, cases and traps

- Ethical anchors: free, prior and informed consent; distributive justice (who gains and who loses); intergenerational equity; Amartya Sen's capability approach (displacement is a loss of capabilities, not only of property); Gandhi's talisman.
- Key judgments: Vidya Devi (2020) and Kolkata Municipal Corporation v Bimal Kumar Shah (2024) on Article 300A due process; Indore Development Authority (2020) on lapse under Section 24; Tarsem Singh (2019) on parity for highway acquisitions.
- Trap: consent is not needed for government's own projects; SIA can be exempted for Fourth Schedule laws and under state amendments.
- Trap: 'urgency' (Section 40) covers only defence, national security and natural calamities (plus Parliament-approved emergencies) — not 'industrial urgency'.
- Trap: under HAM the government pays 40% during construction; under BOT-annuity the private party funds 100% and gets annuities.
- Write the R&R figures (₹5 lakh, ₹2,000 a month for 20 years, ₹3,000 a month for a year) correctly — they show command of the Act.

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** You are SDM in a sub-division of Sangrur district where land is being acquired for a national highway. Farmers have stopped survey work, alleging that compensation is far lower than in a neighbouring district. A few small farmers will lose almost all their land, a village gurdwara's boundary wall falls on the alignment, and the project agency is pressing to hand over possession within 30 days to avoid cost escalation. How will you proceed?

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Acknowledge both interests: national infrastructure vs livelihoods and faith.
- Facts: verify the market-value basis, circle rates, multiplier, and the neighbouring award; explain differences transparently in village meetings.
- Legal route: compensation per RFCTLARR norms with 100% solatium; R&R for those losing most land; arbitration route for rate disputes under the NH Act.
- Special care: small farmers — explore alignment tweaks, employment or annuity options, skill training; gurdwara — consult the committee, seek minor realignment or respectful relocation with consent.
- Speed: grievance camps, on-spot documentation, direct bank payment, fixed timeline; escalate to DC and project agency for realistic deadlines.
- Avoid force; use dialogue with unions and panchayat; communicate in Punjabi.
- Conclusion: fairness and transparency make faster acquisition possible.

</details>

**2.** As SDM in Ferozepur, an Aspirational District, you are asked to improve indicators on institutional deliveries and full immunisation in border villages within one year. Health staff are short, some villages lie beyond the Sutlej and get cut off in monsoon, and data shows large gaps between reported and actual figures. Prepare an action plan.

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Baseline: field verification of data; village-wise gap mapping.
- Targets: SMART quarterly milestones; weekly review dashboard.
- Convergence: ASHA, Anganwadi, ANM, PRIs, BSF and Army medical support in border belt.
- Access: pre-monsoon birth waiting homes, ambulance tie-ups, boat/temporary arrangements, outreach camps.
- Demand side: JSY incentives via DBT, community champions, women's SHGs, religious leaders.
- Accountability: honest reporting culture, recognise performers, audit data.
- Conclusion: Aspirational Districts model — convergence, data, competition.

</details>

**3.** A municipal council in Bathinda district wants to run a dialysis and diagnostic centre in the civil hospital through a PPP. Earlier, a similar PPP in another town failed when the private operator levied hidden charges and kept machines idle. As the officer in charge, how will you design and monitor the new PPP to protect patients and public money?

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Assess need and options: in-house vs PPP; learn from the failed contract.
- Design: output-based contract — uptime, waiting time, quality standards, fixed rates displayed, free service for eligible poor (PM-JAY linkage).
- Risk allocation, performance guarantee, penalties, step-in rights, exit clause.
- Transparent bidding, no unrealistically low bids.
- Monitoring: online dashboard, patient feedback, surprise inspections, third-party audit.
- Grievance redress and display of helpline.
- Conclusion: PPP works when the state is a strong regulator.

</details>
