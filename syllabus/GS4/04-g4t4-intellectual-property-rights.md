## Short notes

- IPR = legal rights over creations of the mind: patents, copyrights, trademarks, industrial designs, geographical indications (GI), plant varieties, trade secrets.
- International framework: Paris Convention (1883, industrial property), Berne Convention (1886, copyright), WIPO (1967), WTO TRIPS Agreement (1995) setting minimum standards.
- Indian laws: Patents Act 1970 (amended 2005 to allow product patents in pharma, food and chemicals), Copyright Act 1957, Trade Marks Act 1999, Designs Act 2000, GI of Goods Act 1999, Protection of Plant Varieties and Farmers' Rights (PPV&FR) Act 2001, Biological Diversity Act 2002.
- Patent term is 20 years; criteria are novelty, inventive step and industrial application.
- Section 3(d) of Patents Act bars patents on new forms of known substances without enhanced efficacy — upheld in Novartis v Union of India (2013) over Glivec; key tool against 'evergreening'.
- Compulsory licensing (Section 84): first granted in 2012 to Natco for Bayer's cancer drug Nexavar (sorafenib) on grounds of high price and limited availability.
- Doha Declaration (2001) affirmed that TRIPS should not prevent members from protecting public health; India and South Africa sought a TRIPS waiver for COVID-19 products in 2020.
- Biopiracy cases: US patents on turmeric (wound healing) and neem were revoked/challenged; RiceTec's basmati patent was narrowed — leading to the Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (TKDL, 2001).
- PPV&FR Act protects breeders and farmers; farmers may save, use, exchange and sell farm-saved seed (not branded); PepsiCo's 2019 suits against Gujarat potato farmers sparked a debate on farmers' rights.
- GI tags protect origin-linked products; Darjeeling Tea was India's first GI; basmati GI covers specified areas of Indo-Gangetic plains, including Punjab, and is contested by Pakistan in the EU.
- National IPR Policy 2016 ('Creative India; Innovative India'); Cell for IPR Promotion and Management (CIPAM) under DPIIT.
- Tribunals Reforms Act 2021 abolished the Intellectual Property Appellate Board; appeals now go to High Courts (Delhi and Madras HCs have IP divisions).
- Issues: patent backlog and examiner shortage, low patent filing by Indian universities, piracy (films, software), counterfeit goods, US Special 301 'Priority Watch List' pressure on India.
- Emerging issues: AI-generated works and authorship, patentability of software, data exclusivity demands in FTAs, open-source vs proprietary models.
- Balance: IPR must reward innovation without blocking access to medicines, seeds and knowledge — India's 'TRIPS-flexibilities' stance.
- Way forward: faster examination, IP literacy in MSMEs and universities, commercialisation offices, stronger enforcement against counterfeits, defensive protection of traditional knowledge.

## Punjab angle

- Phulkari embroidery is a registered GI (shared by Punjab, Haryana and Rajasthan); basmati GI region includes Punjab.
- Pakistan's claim on basmati in the EU directly affects Punjab's export interests; protecting GI standards is vital for exporters.
- PAU Ludhiana licenses its crop varieties and farm machinery (e.g. Happy Seeder) — an example of public-sector IP commercialisation.
- Punjab's generic pharma and sports goods (Jalandhar) and hosiery (Ludhiana) industries face counterfeiting and design-copying issues.

## How PPSC asks it

Usual framing: balance between innovation and access (medicines, seeds), traditional knowledge protection and GI. Cite Novartis, Natco compulsory licence, TKDL and a Punjab GI to score.

## Deep dive

### Legal architecture — provisions examiners quote

- Patents Act 1970, Section 3 lists what is not an invention: 3(d) new forms of known substances without enhanced efficacy, 3(i) methods of treatment, 3(j) plants and animals (except micro-organisms), 3(k) computer programs per se and business methods, 3(p) traditional knowledge.
- Section 25 allows both pre-grant and post-grant opposition — widely used by patient groups against weak drug patents.
- Compulsory licences: Section 84 (three years after grant, if the invention is unaffordable, unavailable or not worked in India), Section 92 (national emergency), Section 92A (exports to countries without manufacturing capacity).
- Patents (Amendment) Rules 2024 cut the deadline for requesting examination from 48 to 31 months and made 'working' statements (Form 27) due once every three financial years.
- Copyright Act 1957 (amended 2012 to give lyricists and composers royalty rights and to allow accessible formats for the visually impaired — India was the first country to ratify the Marrakesh Treaty, 2014).
- Trade Marks Act 1999 (10-year renewable protection; 'well-known marks'); Designs Act 2000 (10 + 5 years); GI Act 1999 (10-year renewable registration, 'authorised users').
- PPV&FR Act 2001 is India's sui generis plant-variety law under TRIPS Article 27.3(b): protection for 15 years for field crops and 18 years for trees and vines, plus farmers' rights and benefit sharing.
- Biological Diversity Act 2002 (amended 2023) needs National Biodiversity Authority approval before seeking IPR on inventions based on Indian bio-resources.
- India has no separate trade-secrets statute; secrets are protected through contracts and common law, and the Law Commission (289th Report, 2024) recommended a dedicated law.

### Cases and controversies (year — case — significance)

- 1997 — US patent on turmeric for wound healing revoked after CSIR showed prior art in Indian texts.
- 2000 — European Patent Office revoked a neem-based fungicide patent; RiceTec's basmati patent was narrowed in the US around 2001.
- 2012 — Natco received India's first compulsory licence for Bayer's cancer drug Nexavar (sorafenib) at a 6% royalty; upheld by IPAB (2013) and Bombay High Court (2014).
- 2013 — Novartis v Union of India: Supreme Court refused a patent for a new form of imatinib (Glivec) under Section 3(d), checking 'evergreening'.
- 2016–17 — Delhi University photocopy case: courts accepted course packs as educational fair use; publishers withdrew the suit.
- 2019 — Supreme Court restored Monsanto's patent on its Bt cotton gene technology, overturning a Delhi High Court division bench.
- 2019–21 — PepsiCo sued Gujarat potato farmers over its protected FL-2027 variety, then withdrew; the PPV&FR Authority revoked the registration in 2021, fuelling the farmers'-rights debate.
- 2024 — Delhi High Court awarded Ericsson about Rs 244 crore against Lava for infringing standard-essential patents (FRAND licensing).
- From 2024 — ANI v OpenAI in the Delhi High Court tests whether training AI on news content infringes copyright.

### IPR, public health and agriculture — the balance

- TRIPS flexibilities India uses: compulsory licensing, parallel imports, the Bolar exception (Section 107A) for early generic development, and no data exclusivity.
- Doha Declaration (2001) affirmed that TRIPS must not stop countries protecting public health; the 2003 decision became TRIPS Article 31bis (in force 2017) for exports under compulsory licence.
- At MC12 (2022) the WTO agreed a limited TRIPS waiver for COVID-19 vaccines — narrower than the 2020 India–South Africa proposal.
- Evergreening, patent thickets and standard-essential patents (SEPs) with FRAND terms are the modern battlegrounds.
- Seeds: Bt cotton trait fees are capped under the Cotton Seed Price (Control) Order 2015; farmers may save, use, exchange and sell (unbranded) farm-saved seed under the PPV&FR Act.
- India resists TRIPS-plus demands such as data exclusivity in trade deals; the India–EFTA Trade and Economic Partnership Agreement (in force 1 October 2025) is often cited in this debate.
- The US Special 301 Report keeps India on its Priority Watch List, citing patent standards and enforcement.
- WIPO adopted a Treaty on IP, Genetic Resources and Associated Traditional Knowledge in May 2024, requiring patent applicants to disclose the origin of genetic resources — a long-standing Indian demand.

### Institutions and data

- The Controller General of Patents, Designs and Trade Marks (Mumbai) runs patent offices in Kolkata, Delhi, Chennai and Mumbai, the Trade Marks Registry and the GI Registry (Chennai).
- 2024-25: patent applications crossed 1,10,000 (a record), over 61% by Indian residents; total IPR filings rose about 20% to roughly 7.5 lakh, with over 5.5 lakh trademark applications.
- India ranks 38th in the WIPO Global Innovation Index 2025; the top five are Switzerland, Sweden, the US, South Korea and Singapore.
- CIPAM (2016) under DPIIT runs IP awareness; the National IP Awareness Mission (2021) has reached lakhs of students; start-ups get an 80% rebate on patent fees.
- Traditional Knowledge Digital Library (2001, CSIR) documents Ayurveda, Unani, Siddha and yoga knowledge in patent-office languages and has helped block many foreign patent claims.
- Persisting issues: examiner shortage and pendency, low commercialisation of university patents, counterfeits and online piracy.

### Punjab angle

- Basmati's GI covers specified districts in seven states including Punjab; Pakistan registered its own basmati GI and contests India's application in the European Union.
- In September 2025 the Punjab Food Processing Department began exploring a GI tag for Amritsari kulcha.
- Punjab State Council for Science and Technology runs a Patent Information Centre that helps local innovators, artisans and MSMEs file patents and GIs.
- PAU Ludhiana licenses its varieties and machines (such as the Happy Seeder) to companies — a model of public-sector IP commercialisation.
- Jalandhar's sports goods and Ludhiana's hosiery and bicycle industries face counterfeits and design copying.
- Seed law in action: Punjab's April 2025 ban on Pusa-44 and hybrid paddy seeds was partly struck down by the Punjab and Haryana High Court (18 August 2025) — notified hybrids cannot be banned by the state under the Seeds Act 1966, but the ban on non-notified seeds stands.

### Answer frames

- 'IPR vs access to medicines': Section 3(d) + Novartis + Natco licence + Doha Declaration + India as supplier of affordable generics.
- 'IPR and innovation': patents data, GII rank, start-up rebates, need for university technology transfer offices.
- 'Traditional knowledge and biopiracy': turmeric, neem, basmati, TKDL, Biological Diversity Act, WIPO 2024 treaty.
- 'IPR and farmers': PPV&FR farmers' rights, PepsiCo potato case, Bt cotton trait fees, seed sovereignty.
- 'AI and IPR': can AI be an author or inventor, is training on copyrighted data fair use (ANI v OpenAI), need for clear rules.
- Conclude with balance: reward inventors, protect public interest — the TRIPS-flexibility approach.

**2.** What are Geographical Indications? Discuss their significance for Punjab's farmers and artisans. (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Define GI: origin-linked quality and reputation; GI Act 1999.
- Punjab: basmati region, Phulkari.
- Benefits: premium prices, export branding, protection against imitation.
- Challenges: weak post-registration marketing, quality control, Pakistan's basmati claim in EU.
- Way forward: authorised-user registration, branding, e-commerce linkage.
- Conclusion: GI as a rural livelihood tool.

**3.** Biopiracy remains a threat to India's traditional knowledge. Examine the steps taken by India to protect it. (200 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Define biopiracy with turmeric, neem, basmati examples.
- Steps: TKDL (2001) shared with patent offices; Biological Diversity Act 2002 with access and benefit sharing; Section 3(p) of Patents Act on traditional knowledge.
- PPV&FR Act 2001 recognising farmers as conservers.
- Gaps: oral knowledge undocumented, benefit-sharing weakly enforced.
- Way forward: People's Biodiversity Registers, WIPO treaty engagement.
- Conclusion: defensive and positive protection combined.

</details>
