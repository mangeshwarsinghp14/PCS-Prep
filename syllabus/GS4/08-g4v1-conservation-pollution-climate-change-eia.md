## Short notes

- Conservation strategies: in-situ (national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, conservation and community reserves, biosphere reserves) and ex-situ (zoos, seed banks, gene banks, botanical gardens).
- Key laws: Wildlife (Protection) Act 1972 (amended 2022), Water Act 1974, Forest (Conservation) Act 1980 (amended 2023, renamed Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam), Air Act 1981, Environment (Protection) Act 1986, Biological Diversity Act 2002 (amended 2023), National Green Tribunal Act 2010.
- Judicial principles: polluter pays and precautionary principle (Vellore Citizens Welfare Forum, 1996); right to pollution-free environment under Article 21 (Subhash Kumar, 1991); broad meaning of 'forest' (T.N. Godavarman, 1996).
- M.K. Ranjitsinh v Union of India (2024): Supreme Court recognised a right against the adverse effects of climate change under Articles 14 and 21.
- Pollution types: air (PM2.5, NO2, SO2), water (sewage, industrial effluents, agricultural run-off), soil (pesticides, heavy metals), noise, plastic and e-waste.
- National Clean Air Programme (2019) targets reduction in particulate concentration in non-attainment cities; Commission for Air Quality Management (CAQM) Act 2021 covers NCR and adjoining areas including Punjab.
- Degradation drivers: deforestation, land degradation and desertification, wetland loss, overgrazing, invasive species, unsustainable groundwater use.
- Climate change regime: UNFCCC (1992), Kyoto Protocol (1997), Paris Agreement (2015, nationally determined contributions).
- India's updated NDC (2022): reduce emissions intensity of GDP by 45% by 2030 from 2005 level; about 50% of installed power capacity from non-fossil sources by 2030; net zero by 2070.
- Domestic climate policy: National Action Plan on Climate Change (2008) with eight missions; State Action Plans; Mission LiFE (Lifestyle for Environment); Green Credit Programme; carbon credit trading scheme.
- Environmental Impact Assessment (EIA): EIA Notification 2006 — stages screening, scoping, public consultation, appraisal; Category A projects cleared by MoEFCC, Category B by State-level authority (SEIAA).
- EIA weaknesses: consultant-driven reports, poor public hearings, weak post-clearance monitoring, cumulative impacts ignored; ex-post facto clearances criticised by the SC (Alembic Pharmaceuticals, 2020).
- Strengthening EIA: independent accredited agencies, strategic and cumulative EIA, open data, local-language disclosure, compliance monitoring with penalties.
- Way forward: integrate climate risk in planning, nature-based solutions, circular economy, community conservation, strengthen pollution control boards.

## Punjab angle

- Punjab has six Ramsar wetlands — Harike, Kanjli, Ropar, Keshopur-Miani, Beas Conservation Reserve and Nangal; the Indus river dolphin in the Beas is Punjab's state aquatic animal.
- Forest cover is under 4% of geographical area (ISFR), among the lowest in India; Kandi belt is protected under the Punjab Land Preservation Act 1900.
- Stubble burning: satellite-recorded farm fires fell by roughly 70% between 2023 and 2024, yet remain a major winter air-quality issue under CAQM oversight.
- River pollution: Buddha Nullah (Ludhiana) carries industrial and domestic effluents into the Sutlej; the Kali Bein clean-up led by Sant Balbir Singh Seechewal is a model of community action.
- The proposed textile park at Mattewara near the Sutlej and Mattewara forest was dropped by the state in 2022 after environmental protests — an EIA/public participation example.

## Deep dive

### EIA in depth — process, history, controversies

- 1978–79: river valley projects first appraised environmentally; 1994: first statutory EIA notification under the Environment (Protection) Act; 2006: current notification (decentralised, Category A by MoEFCC, Category B by State-level SEIAA; B1 needs full EIA, B2 does not).
- Four stages: screening, scoping (terms of reference), public consultation (public hearing conducted by the State Pollution Control Board plus written comments) and appraisal by Expert or State Appraisal Committees; six-monthly compliance reports after clearance.
- Draft EIA Notification 2020 drew protest for allowing post-facto clearance, cutting the public-hearing notice period from 30 to 20 days, exempting 'strategic' projects and moving to annual compliance reports (PPSC 2021 asked about it).
- Supreme Court: Alembic Pharmaceuticals (2020) held ex post facto clearances alien to environmental law; Vanashakti (May 2025, two judges) struck down the 2017 notification and 2021 office memorandum, but a three-judge bench recalled that verdict 2:1 on 18 November 2025; on 29 July 2026 a three-judge bench (CJI Surya Kant) held that prior clearance remains the rule and post-facto clearance can be allowed only through a statutory notification under Section 3 of the Environment (Protection) Act, quashing the 2021 office memorandum prospectively while protecting clearances already granted.
- PARIVESH (2018; PARIVESH 2.0 in 2023) is the single-window portal for environment, forest, wildlife and CRZ clearances.
- Strategic Environmental Assessment (for policies and plans) and cumulative impact assessment (river-basin studies for the Teesta or the Alaknanda–Bhagirathi) are the reforms most often recommended.
- Case studies: Niyamgiri (2013 — gram sabhas decided on Vedanta's bauxite mine), POSCO in Odisha, Hasdeo Arand coal blocks, Ken–Betwa link through Panna Tiger Reserve, the Great Nicobar project.
- Public trust doctrine (M.C. Mehta v Kamal Nath, 1997): the state holds natural resources in trust and cannot hand them over for private use against public interest.

### Climate governance architecture — India and the world

- Institutions: PM's Council on Climate Change (2007), NAPCC (2008) with eight missions, State Action Plans on Climate Change, National Adaptation Fund for Climate Change (2015).
- India submitted its first Adaptation Communication at COP28 (2023) and its Long-Term Low-Emission Development Strategy at COP27 (2022).
- NDC for 2031–35 (approved March 2026): 47% emissions-intensity cut by 2035 from 2005, 60% non-fossil installed capacity, 3.5–4 billion tonnes CO2e additional carbon sink.
- Finance tools: Sovereign Green Bonds (first issued January 2023), the Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (2023) and the Green Credit Programme (2023).
- Courts: M.K. Ranjitsinh v Union of India (2024) recognised a right against the adverse effects of climate change under Articles 14 and 21; the ICJ's advisory opinion of 23 July 2025 held that states have legal obligations to protect the climate system.
- Mission LiFE (October 2022) pushes sustainable lifestyles; UNEA-6 (2024) adopted a resolution on sustainable lifestyles backed by India.
- Global context: COP30 Belém (November 2025) called for tripling adaptation finance by 2035 and adopted 59 adaptation indicators but no fossil-fuel roadmap; COP31 at Antalya (9–20 November 2026) runs under Türkiye's presidency with Australia chairing negotiations.
- Data to quote: WMO — 2025 was about 1.43 °C above pre-industrial and 2015–2025 were the eleven warmest years; IMD — about 2,760 Indians died from extreme weather in 2025.

### Pollution governance — gaps and reforms

- Pollution control boards face staff vacancies, weak laboratories and a 'consent' culture focused on paperwork rather than ambient outcomes.
- Decriminalisation: the Jan Vishwas Act 2023 and the Water (Amendment) Act 2024 replaced jail terms for many violations with adjudicated monetary penalties — critics fear weaker deterrence, supporters expect faster enforcement.
- The NGT has imposed heavy environmental compensation on states — about ₹2,080 crore on Punjab in 2022 for solid and liquid waste mismanagement.
- Air: NCAP's 40% PM10 reduction target for 2025-26; CAQM's directions on stubble, industrial fuels and GRAP; environmental compensation for stubble burning doubled in November 2024.
- Online continuous emission/effluent monitoring is mandatory for 17 categories of highly polluting industries; data transparency is uneven.
- Waste: Extended Producer Responsibility for plastics, e-waste and batteries; legacy dumpsites remain a major urban problem.
- Way forward: outcome-based targets, real-time public data, independent audits, fiscal incentives to cities that show air-quality gains, and stronger local bodies.

### Conservation — debates and case studies

- Fortress conservation versus community conservation: the Forest Rights Act 2006 and critical wildlife habitats, voluntary relocation from tiger reserves and co-management models.
- Human–wildlife conflict: Kerala declared it a state-specific disaster in 2024; elephant corridors and leopard conflict in Maharashtra and Uttarakhand are standard examples.
- Linear infrastructure: Great Indian Bustard deaths on power lines led to Supreme Court orders (2021, modified 2024); wildlife underpasses on NH-44 through the Pench landscape are a positive model.
- Forest law debate: the 2023 Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam versus the Godavarman definition of forest, which the Supreme Court kept alive in 2024.
- Nature-based programmes: MISHTI (mangroves, 2023), Amrit Dharohar (Ramsar wetlands, 2023), Nagar Van Yojana (urban forests, 2020), Green India Mission and 'Ek Ped Maa Ke Naam' (2024).
- Global: CBD COP17 (Yerevan, October 2026) found the world off-track on the 23 KMGBF targets and adopted the 'Yerevan Pathway'; India's 2024 NBSAP has 23 national targets.
- Ecosystem accounting and payments for ecosystem services can value what conservation protects — useful for 'way forward' paragraphs.

### Punjab — data and cases for answers

- Forest and tree cover is only about 6% of Punjab's area (2,985 sq km of 50,362 sq km, ISFR 2021); the Kandi belt is protected under the Punjab Land Preservation Act 1900.
- Six Ramsar sites (Harike, Kanjli, Ropar, Nangal, Keshopur-Miani, Beas Conservation Reserve); neighbouring Chandigarh's Sukhna lake was added in 2026.
- Indus river dolphin (state aquatic animal) — only 3 counted in the Beas in the 2025 national survey; threats are barrages, sand mining and pollution.
- Rivers: Buddha Nullah (Ludhiana) and other drains pollute the Sutlej; the NGT's Justice Jasbir Singh committee (2018) monitored Punjab's rivers; the Kali Bein revival by Sant Seechewal is the community model.
- Air: stubble burning, CAQM oversight, in-situ machinery, ex-situ use in CBG plants (Lehragaga) and boilers.
- Climate: Punjab State Action Plan on Climate Change (2014); terminal heat in wheat (2022), floods in 2023 and 2025, and groundwater stress make Punjab a climate-vulnerable agrarian state.
- EIA/public participation: Mattewara textile park withdrawal (2022).

### Answer frameworks and keywords

- Structure for a 250-word answer: define → data point (with year) → causes/dimensions → India's response (law, scheme, court) → Punjab example → way forward.
- Keywords that fetch marks: intergenerational equity, polluter pays, precautionary principle, sustainable development, just transition, environmental justice, carrying capacity, nature-based solutions.
- Pair every criticism with a reform: weak EIA → independent accredited consultants and cumulative assessment; weak boards → staffing and real-time data.
- Use a simple diagram: EIA stages flow chart or a pressure–state–response table.
- Quote institutions correctly: NGT (2010), CAQM (2021), NTCA (2006), NBA (2003), CGWA (1997).
- End with a balanced line linking growth and ecology (e.g., Mission LiFE and green jobs).

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** Stubble burning in Punjab is both an environmental and an economic problem. Suggest a multi-pronged strategy to end it. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: paddy–wheat cycle, short window, residue volume.
- Impacts: air quality, soil carbon loss, health costs.
- In-situ: Happy Seeder, Super SMS, bio-decomposer; ex-situ: biomass power, CBG plants, bio-ethanol, pellets.
- Crop diversification and short-duration varieties (PR-126).
- Incentives and enforcement: CRM subsidies, cash incentives, red-entry penalties, CAQM directions.
- Awareness through panchayats, PAU and KVKs.
- Conclusion: economics-driven solution with farmer partnership.

</details>

**2.** Critically examine the effectiveness of Environmental Impact Assessment in India. (200 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- EIA Notification 2006 and its stages.
- Strengths: legal requirement, public hearing, conditions.
- Weaknesses: consultant bias, poor hearings, weak monitoring, ex-post clearances (Alembic 2020).
- Example: Mattewara textile park debate in Punjab.
- Reforms: independent agencies, cumulative EIA, transparency.
- Conclusion: EIA as a planning tool, not a formality.

</details>

**3.** Discuss India's climate commitments under the Paris Agreement and the challenges in meeting them. (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Updated NDC 2022: 45% emissions intensity cut, 50% non-fossil capacity by 2030; net zero 2070.
- Progress: renewables growth, LiFE, Green Credit.
- Challenges: coal dependence, finance, technology, adaptation needs, just transition.
- Equity: CBDR-RC principle.
- Conclusion: development-climate balance.

</details>
