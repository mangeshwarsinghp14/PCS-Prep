## Short notes

- Disaster Management Act 2005, Section 2(d): a catastrophe, mishap, calamity or grave occurrence from natural or man-made causes, or by accident or negligence, causing substantial loss of life, property or environment, beyond the coping capacity of the affected community.
- UNDRR view: a serious disruption of the functioning of a community due to hazardous events interacting with exposure, vulnerability and capacity.
- Key terms: hazard (potential damaging event), exposure (people/assets in harm's way), vulnerability (susceptibility), capacity (resources to cope); Risk is often expressed as Hazard × Exposure × Vulnerability, reduced by capacity.
- A hazard becomes a disaster only when it hits a vulnerable, exposed population — an earthquake in an empty desert is not a disaster.
- Nature of disasters: sudden or slow onset, often cascading (earthquake → landslide → dam breach), disproportionately hurting the poor, women, elderly and disabled.
- Classification by origin: natural (geophysical — earthquake, tsunami, volcano; hydrological — flood, landslide; meteorological — cyclone, cloudburst; climatological — drought, heat wave; biological — epidemics, pest attacks) and man-made (industrial, chemical, nuclear, fire, transport accidents, stampedes, terror-related).
- Classification by speed: rapid-onset (earthquake, flash flood) vs slow-onset (drought, desertification, sea-level rise).
- High Powered Committee on Disaster Management (J.C. Pant, 1999) listed 31 disasters under five groups: water and climate, geological, chemical–industrial–nuclear, accident-related, and biological.
- Notified disasters eligible for SDRF/NDRF relief: cyclone, drought, earthquake, fire, flood, tsunami, hailstorm, landslide, avalanche, cloudburst, pest attack, frost/cold wave; states may use part of SDRF for locally notified disasters.
- India's vulnerability (NDMA): about 59% of landmass prone to moderate-to-very-high earthquakes, about 12% (40 million hectares) flood-prone, about 68% of cultivable area drought-prone, and most of the coastline cyclone/tsunami-prone.
- Global frameworks: Hyogo Framework (2005–15), Sendai Framework for DRR (2015–30) with four priorities and seven targets; India's National Disaster Management Plan (2016, revised 2019) aligned to Sendai.
- India's DRR leadership: Prime Minister's 10-point agenda on DRR (2016) and the Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure (CDRI, 2019).
- Shift in approach: from relief-centric to proactive risk reduction, preparedness and 'build back better'.
- Climate change increases frequency and intensity of floods, heat waves, cloudbursts and droughts; COVID-19 was notified as a disaster under the DM Act in 2020.
- Answer tip: always distinguish hazard and disaster, give one classification table and one Indian example per type.

## Punjab angle

- Floods are Punjab's main natural hazard — the 2023 floods (Sutlej, Ghaggar, Beas) and the 2025 floods, widely called the worst since 1988, affected all 23 districts.
- Northern sub-Himalayan districts (Pathankot, Gurdaspur, Hoshiarpur, Rupnagar) lie in seismic Zone IV, higher than the Zone III south-western plains (IS 1893:2016, still in force after the 2025 revision was withdrawn).
- Man-made disasters in Punjab: Amritsar Joda Phatak train accident during Dussehra (2018), Batala firecracker factory explosion (2019), Ludhiana Giaspura gas leak (2023), and spurious liquor tragedies (2020 in Majha districts; 2025 in Majitha, Amritsar).
- Heat waves, hailstorms and pest attacks (whitefly, pink bollworm, locust threat in 2020) hit Punjab's agriculture.

## Deep dive

### Disaster management cycle and core concepts

- Pre-disaster: prevention, mitigation and preparedness; during: response; post-disaster: relief, recovery, rehabilitation and reconstruction — with 'build back better' as the guiding principle.
- Risk ≈ hazard × exposure × vulnerability ÷ capacity; residual risk remains after mitigation and must be covered by preparedness and insurance.
- Cascading, compound and 'Natech' (natural hazard triggering technological accidents) events are increasingly common — e.g., the 2023 South Lhonak GLOF destroying the Teesta-III dam.
- Sendai Framework (2015–30): four priorities — understand risk, strengthen risk governance, invest in resilience, enhance preparedness and build back better — and seven targets (a–g) on mortality, affected people, economic loss, critical infrastructure, national/local strategies, international cooperation and early warning.
- UN 'Early Warnings for All' (2022) aims to cover everyone by 2027; the UN General Assembly's 2023 political declaration completed the Sendai mid-term review.
- International Day for Disaster Risk Reduction is 13 October; UNDRR publishes the Global Assessment Report.

### Institutional framework after the 2025 amendment

- DM Act 2005 (assent 23 December 2005): NDMA (Prime Minister chairs), National Executive Committee (Union Home Secretary), SDMA (Chief Minister), State Executive Committee (Chief Secretary), DDMA (District Collector/Deputy Commissioner chairs, with an elected local representative as co-chair), NIDM and NDRF (16 battalions).
- Disaster Management (Amendment) Act 2025 (Act 10 of 2025): introduced August 2024, passed by the Lok Sabha in December 2024 and the Rajya Sabha on 25 March 2025, gazetted on 29 March 2025.
- Key changes: NDMA and SDMAs themselves prepare national and state plans; they periodically assess risks including emerging climate risks; maintain national and state disaster databases (risks, fund allocation, spending, preparedness); and recommend minimum relief standards.
- NDMA can assess states' preparedness, conduct post-disaster audits and frame regulations with Central approval.
- Statutory status for the National Crisis Management Committee (Cabinet Secretary) and the High Level Committee (chaired by the minister of the administrative ministry) that approves central assistance and mitigation-fund releases.
- States may create Urban Disaster Management Authorities for state capitals and municipal-corporation cities (Municipal Commissioner chairs, Collector vice-chairs) and a statutory State Disaster Response Force.
- Critiques: more centralisation, overlapping bodies, no definition of a 'national disaster', and state concerns over funding norms.
- Nodal ministries by hazard: Home Affairs (most natural disasters), Agriculture (drought, hailstorm, pest attack), Health (biological), Environment (chemical), Atomic Energy (nuclear), Civil Aviation and Railways (their accidents).

### Financing disasters — 15th Finance Commission design

- National and State Disaster Risk Management Funds split 80% for response (NDRF/SDRF) and 20% for mitigation (NDMF/SDMF).
- The response share is further split: 40% response and relief, 30% recovery and reconstruction, 30% preparedness and capacity building.
- Centre–state sharing for SDRMF is 75:25, and 90:10 for north-eastern and Himalayan states.
- Only centrally notified disasters (cyclone, drought, earthquake, fire, flood, tsunami, hailstorm, landslide, avalanche, cloudburst, pest attack, frost/cold wave) qualify for full SDRF use; states can spend a limited share on locally notified disasters such as heat waves or lightning.
- Risk transfer: crop insurance (PMFBY), parametric insurance pilots and catastrophe bonds remain under-used in India.
- Second ARC's third report, 'Crisis Management' (2006), is the standard source for reform ideas.

### Classification tables with Indian examples

- Geophysical: earthquakes (Bhuj 2001), tsunami (2004), volcanic activity (Barren Island) — Hydrological: floods (Kerala 2018, Punjab 2025), landslides (Wayanad 2024), GLOFs (South Lhonak 2023).
- Meteorological: cyclones (Odisha 1999, Amphan 2020), cloudbursts, hailstorms, lightning — Climatological: droughts, heat waves, cold waves.
- Biological: epidemics (COVID-19 was notified under the DM Act in 2020), pest attacks (2020 locust alert), animal disease (lumpy skin disease, 2022); extraterrestrial: solar storms affecting power grids and satellites.
- Man-made: industrial (Bhopal 1984, Visakhapatnam styrene leak 2020), fire (Uphaar 1997), stampede (Prayagraj Maha Kumbh, January 2025), rail (Balasore, June 2023), air (Air India AI171 crash at Ahmedabad, June 2025), structural collapse.
- NDMA vulnerability profile: about 59% of land prone to moderate-to-very-high earthquakes, about 12% (40 million ha) flood-prone, about 68% of cultivable area drought-prone, most of the coastline exposed to cyclones and tsunamis; GSI rates about 12–13% of land landslide-prone.
- Seismic zoning: IS 1893:2016 zones II–V remain in force — the 2025 revision that added a Zone VI for the Himalayan arc was withdrawn by BIS in March 2026.

### Punjab disaster profile

- Institutions: Punjab State Disaster Management Authority (Chief Minister chairs), Department of Revenue, Rehabilitation and Disaster Management, a state SDRF, and DDMAs headed by Deputy Commissioners with SDMs running sub-divisional control rooms.
- Floods are the dominant hazard — major events in 1955, 1988, 1993, 2019 (Sutlej breach near Jalandhar–Ropar), 2023 and 2025.
- 2025 floods: all 23 districts declared flood-hit on 2 September 2025; about 1,400 villages and 3.5 lakh people affected at that point; heavy rain upstream in Himachal and Jammu & Kashmir with high inflows into Ranjit Sagar and Pong; regarded as the worst since 1988.
- Seismic: Pathankot, Gurdaspur, Hoshiarpur and Rupnagar lie in Zone IV; the rest of the plains in Zone III.
- Other hazards: heat and cold waves, dense fog (road and rail accidents), hailstorms, lightning, pest attacks on cotton, industrial accidents (Ludhiana's Giaspura gas leak, 2023; Batala factory explosion, 2019), the 2018 Amritsar train accident and spurious-liquor tragedies.
- Biological: dengue and water-borne disease surges after floods; lumpy skin disease in cattle (2022).

**1.** Distinguish between hazard, vulnerability and disaster. Classify disasters with suitable examples from India. (250 words)

- Risk equation and the idea that disasters are not 'natural'.

- Indian examples for each: Bhuj 2001, Kedarnath 2013, Odisha cyclones, Bhopal 1984, COVID-19.

**2.** Explain the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction and India's alignment with it. (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Sendai 2015–30: four priorities — understanding risk, governance, investment in resilience, preparedness and build back better.
- Seven targets on mortality, affected people, losses, infrastructure, strategies, cooperation, early warning.
- India: NDMP 2016/2019, PM's 10-point agenda, CDRI.
- Gaps: local plans, data.
- Conclusion: shift to risk-informed development.

</details>

**3.** 'Man-made disasters in Punjab are largely preventable.' Discuss with examples. (200 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Examples: Joda Phatak (2018), Batala blast (2019), Giaspura gas leak (2023), hooch tragedies.
- Common causes: regulatory failure, illegal units, crowd management lapses, poor inspection.
- Prevention: licensing and inspection, zoning, crowd plans, excise enforcement, public awareness.
- Accountability and quick relief.
- Conclusion: enforcement and preparedness culture.

</details>
