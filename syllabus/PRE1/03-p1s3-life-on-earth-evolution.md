## Short notes

- Earth formed about 4.54 billion years ago; the oldest evidence of life (cyanobacterial stromatolites) is about 3.5 billion years old.
- Oparin-Haldane hypothesis: life arose by chemical evolution from simple molecules in a 'primordial soup' under a reducing atmosphere (CH4, NH3, H2, water vapour).
- Miller-Urey experiment (1953) produced amino acids by passing electric sparks through such a gas mixture, supporting chemical evolution.
- Photosynthetic cyanobacteria released oxygen; the Great Oxidation Event (about 2.4 billion years ago) made aerobic life and the ozone layer possible.
- Lamarck proposed inheritance of acquired characters (use and disuse; giraffe neck) - now rejected.
- Darwin's theory of natural selection ('On the Origin of Species', 1859): variation, overproduction, struggle for existence, survival of the fittest; Alfred Russel Wallace reached similar ideas independently.
- Hugo de Vries proposed mutation theory (evening primrose, Oenothera); the modern synthetic theory combines genetics, mutation, recombination, selection and isolation.
- Homologous organs (same origin, different function: forelimbs of human, whale, bat) show divergent evolution; analogous organs (different origin, same function: wings of bird and insect) show convergent evolution.
- Vestigial organs (human appendix, wisdom teeth, tail bone/coccyx) and fossils are evidence of evolution; Archaeopteryx is a connecting link between reptiles and birds.
- Living examples of natural selection: industrial melanism in peppered moths in England, antibiotic resistance in bacteria and pesticide resistance in insects.
- Adaptive radiation: Darwin's finches of the Galapagos and Australian marsupials evolved into many forms from a common ancestor.
- Mass extinctions: the end-Permian (about 252 million years ago) was the largest; the end-Cretaceous (about 66 million years ago, asteroid impact) wiped out non-avian dinosaurs.
- Modern humans (Homo sapiens) arose in Africa roughly 3 lakh years ago; earlier relatives include Australopithecus, Homo habilis, Homo erectus and Neanderthals.
- Classification: R.H. Whittaker's five kingdoms (1969) - Monera, Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia; Carl Woese's three domains - Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya.
- Oceans cover about 71% of Earth's surface; marine phytoplankton produce a large share (often estimated about half) of the world's oxygen.
- Marine life is grouped as plankton (drifting), nekton (active swimmers) and benthos (bottom dwellers); most life is in the sunlit photic zone (top ~200 m); deep hydrothermal vents support chemosynthetic communities.
- Coral reefs are built by coral polyps living with symbiotic algae (zooxanthellae); warming seas cause coral bleaching when the algae are expelled.
- Terrestrial life is organised into biomes (tropical rainforest - richest in species, grassland, desert, taiga, tundra); desert plants (xerophytes) show spines, thick cuticle and sunken stomata; amphibians were the first land vertebrates.

## Punjab angle

- The Siwalik hills along Punjab's north-east (Hoshiarpur, Ropar, Pathankot belt) are rich in Tertiary mammal fossils; the Saketi fossil park lies nearby in Himachal's Sirmaur district.
- Harike wetland at the Sutlej-Beas confluence and the Beas Conservation Reserve show freshwater aquatic life, including the Indus river dolphin, Punjab's state aquatic animal.

## Deep dive

### Geological time and life milestones (approximate years ago — event)

- 4.54 billion — Earth forms; about 4.4 billion — oldest known mineral grains (zircons from Jack Hills, Australia).
- About 3.5–3.7 billion — earliest traces of life; stromatolites built by microbial mats.
- About 2.4 billion — Great Oxidation Event; about 2 billion — eukaryotic cells, which acquired mitochondria and chloroplasts by endosymbiosis (Lynn Margulis's theory).
- About 539 million — Cambrian explosion; most animal body plans appear (Burgess Shale fossils).
- About 470 million — plants colonise land; about 375 million — Tiktaalik, a fish-to-tetrapod transitional fossil; about 360 million — amphibians; about 312 million — reptiles with the shelled (amniotic) egg.
- About 252 million — end-Permian extinction, the largest; the Mesozoic (Triassic, Jurassic, Cretaceous) follows as the 'age of reptiles'.
- About 150 million — Archaeopteryx (Jurassic); about 130 million — flowering plants spread.
- About 66 million — end-Cretaceous extinction (Chicxulub asteroid impact, Mexico; Deccan Traps volcanism also implicated); the Cenozoic becomes the 'age of mammals'.
- About 7–6 million — human and chimpanzee lineages split; about 3.2 million — 'Lucy' (Australopithecus afarensis, Ethiopia); about 300,000 — oldest Homo sapiens fossils (Jebel Irhoud, Morocco).
- About 11,700 — the Holocene begins; in March 2024 geologists voted against formally adding an 'Anthropocene' epoch.
- The 'Big Five' extinctions: end-Ordovician, Late Devonian, end-Permian, end-Triassic, end-Cretaceous; many scientists describe today's human-driven losses as a sixth.

### Evolution — mechanisms and modern evidence

- Hardy–Weinberg principle (1908): allele frequencies stay constant unless mutation, gene flow, genetic drift, selection or non-random mating act — any change signals evolution.
- Genetic drift is strongest in small populations: the founder effect and bottlenecks (cheetahs have very low genetic diversity).
- Selection types: stabilising (human birth weight), directional (peppered moth, antibiotic resistance), disruptive (two extremes favoured).
- Speciation: allopatric through geographic isolation (Darwin's finches); sympatric through polyploidy, common in plants such as bread wheat.
- Molecular evidence: a universal genetic code and close DNA similarity (humans and chimpanzees share about 98–99% of DNA sequence).
- Svante Pääbo won the 2022 Nobel in Medicine for sequencing the Neanderthal genome and identifying Denisovans; people outside Africa carry about 1–2% Neanderthal DNA.
- Convergent evolution: camera-type eyes of octopus and humans; flippers of penguins and dolphins. Coevolution: flowers and pollinators; mimicry (a harmless species copying a harmful one).
- Darwin sailed on HMS Beagle (1831–36); Herbert Spencer coined 'survival of the fittest'; the Modern Synthesis (1930s–40s: Fisher, Haldane, Wright, Dobzhansky) united Darwin and Mendel.
- J.B.S. Haldane, a founder of population genetics and co-author of the origin-of-life hypothesis, moved to India and became an Indian citizen in 1961.
- Punctuated equilibrium (Eldredge and Gould, 1972) proposes long stable periods broken by rapid change.

### Marine life — zones, ecosystems and threats

- Depth zones: sunlit epipelagic (0–200 m), twilight mesopelagic (200–1,000 m), bathypelagic, abyssal and hadal (trenches; the Mariana Trench is nearly 11 km deep).
- Many deep-sea animals produce their own light (bioluminescence); hydrothermal-vent communities found in 1977 on the Galapagos Rift live on chemosynthetic bacteria, not sunlight.
- Upwelling zones (Peru, Somali coast, Arabian Sea) bring nutrients up and support the richest fisheries; El Nino weakens Peruvian upwelling and fish catches.
- Coral reefs cover under 1% of the seabed but shelter roughly a quarter of marine species; India's reefs are in the Gulf of Mannar, Gulf of Kachchh, Lakshadweep and Andaman and Nicobar.
- Seagrass meadows feed dugongs; India notified its first Dugong Conservation Reserve in Palk Bay, Tamil Nadu, in 2022. Olive Ridley turtles nest en masse (arribada) at Gahirmatha and Rushikulya, Odisha.
- Threats: overfishing, plastics and microplastics, warming-driven bleaching, acidification, oxygen-poor 'dead zones' and harmful algal blooms.
- UNCLOS (1982) governs ocean zones; the High Seas Treaty (BBNJ, adopted 2023) on biodiversity beyond national jurisdiction entered into force in January 2026; India signed it in 2024.
- India's Deep Ocean Mission (2021) includes Samudrayaan, which aims to take three people to 6,000 m in the Matsya-6000 submersible.

### Terrestrial life — biomes and adaptations

- Biomes are set mainly by temperature and rainfall; tropical rainforests cover a small share of land but hold over half of known species, on nutrient-poor soils.
- Desert plants such as cacti and pineapple use CAM photosynthesis, opening stomata at night to save water; camels store fat (not water) in humps and produce concentrated urine.
- Tundra has permafrost, mosses and lichens; taiga has needle-leaved conifers; Himalayan altitude zones mimic the change from tropics to tundra.
- Dormancy strategies: hibernation in winter (bears, frogs) and aestivation in summer (snails, lungfish); long migrations such as the Amur falcon's stopover in Nagaland.
- Extremophiles: Thermus aquaticus from hot springs supplies Taq polymerase used in PCR; halophiles live in salt pans; tardigrades survive extreme drying and radiation.
- India has four of the world's 36 biodiversity hotspots — Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Western Ghats–Sri Lanka and Sundaland (Nicobar) — and about 7–8% of recorded species on 2.4% of world land.
- Living fossils and links often asked: coelacanth (fish), Ginkgo (plant), horseshoe crab, Peripatus (annelid–arthropod link), platypus (egg-laying mammal).

### Punjab links

- The Siwalik belt along Punjab's north-east (Hoshiarpur, Ropar, Pathankot) has yielded Miocene–Pliocene mammal and ape fossils such as Sivapithecus, ancient elephants and giraffids.
- Palaeobotanist Birbal Sahni was born in Bhera (undivided Punjab) in 1891; the Birbal Sahni Institute of Palaeosciences, Lucknow, carries his name.
- Punjab has six Ramsar wetlands — Harike, Kanjli and Ropar (2002) and Beas Conservation Reserve, Keshopur-Miani and Nangal (2019).
- The Indus river dolphin, Punjab's state aquatic animal, survives as a tiny population in the Beas between Talwara and Harike.
- Chhatbir Zoo (Mahendra Chaudhary Zoological Park) and the Bir Moti Bagh and Takhni-Rehmapur sanctuaries are useful examples of ex-situ and in-situ conservation.
- Punjab's state animal is the blackbuck, state bird the baaz (northern goshawk) and state tree the shisham.

### Common traps

- Neanderthals were a sister group of modern humans, not our direct ancestors; humans did not evolve from today's chimpanzees — both share a common ancestor.
- Pterosaurs (flying reptiles) and plesiosaurs (marine reptiles) were not dinosaurs; whales and dolphins are mammals, not fish.
- Fossils are dated mostly by the rocks around them (radiometric dating); carbon-14 is useless for dinosaur fossils (far older than 50,000 years).
- Analogous organs show convergent evolution; homologous organs show divergent evolution — a favourite reversal in options.
- Archaeopteryx links reptiles and birds, Tiktaalik links fish and four-legged animals — options often swap them.
- 'Use and disuse' and the giraffe-neck story belong to Lamarck; Darwin explained the same trait through variation and natural selection.

**1.** The wings of a bat and the forelimbs of a whale are examples of:

(a) Convergent evolution  
(b) Homologous organs  
(c) Vestigial organs  
(d) Analogous organs

**(b) Homologous organs** — They share the same basic bone structure and origin but do different jobs - divergent evolution.

**2.** The theory of 'inheritance of acquired characters' was proposed by:

(a) Jean-Baptiste Lamarck  
(b) Hugo de Vries  
(c) Gregor Mendel  
(d) Charles Darwin

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) Jean-Baptiste Lamarck** — Lamarck argued use and disuse of organs produced heritable changes; this is not supported by genetics.

**3.** The Miller-Urey experiment (1953) produced which molecules from simple gases?

(a) Amino acids  
(b) Proteins with enzymes  
(c) Living cells  
(d) DNA

**(a) Amino acids** — Electric sparks in a CH4, NH3, H2 and water-vapour mixture yielded amino acids, supporting chemical evolution.

</details>

(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

(a) Reptiles and birds  
(b) Amphibians and reptiles  
(c) Reptiles and mammals

**(a) Reptiles and birds** — It had feathers like birds and teeth, claws and a bony tail like reptiles.

**6.** Development of antibiotic resistance in bacteria is an example of:

(a) Analogous evolution  
(b) Spontaneous generation  
(c) Natural selection  
(d) Inheritance of acquired characters

**(c) Natural selection** — Resistant variants survive the antibiotic and multiply, so the population changes over generations.

**(d) Stable allele frequencies if no evolution acts** — It is the null model of population genetics.
