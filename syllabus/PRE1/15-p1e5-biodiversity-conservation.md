## Short notes

- Biodiversity is variety of life at three levels - genetic, species and ecosystem; the term was popularised by E.O. Wilson in the 1980s.
- Alpha diversity is within a community, beta diversity is change between communities, gamma diversity is across a whole region.
- India is one of 17 megadiverse countries, with about 2.4% of the world's land but roughly 7-8% of recorded species; it has 10 biogeographic zones.
- Biodiversity hotspots (Norman Myers): at least 1,500 endemic vascular plants and at least 70% original habitat lost; India has parts of four of the 36 - Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Western Ghats-Sri Lanka and Sundaland (Nicobar Islands).
- Threats: habitat loss and fragmentation (biggest), overexploitation, invasive alien species (Lantana, water hyacinth, Parthenium, African catfish), pollution, climate change, co-extinction.
- IUCN Red List categories: Extinct, Extinct in the Wild, Critically Endangered, Endangered, Vulnerable, Near Threatened, Least Concern, Data Deficient; the first three threatened categories are CR, EN, VU.
- Keystone species have outsized effects on their ecosystem; flagship species attract support (tiger); umbrella species protect many others through their habitat; endemic species are found only in one region.
- In-situ conservation: national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, conservation reserves and community reserves (added in 2002), biosphere reserves (core, buffer, transition zones), sacred groves, Ramsar wetlands (India had 103 by October 2026).
- Ex-situ conservation: zoos, botanical gardens, seed and gene banks (NBPGR, New Delhi; Svalbard Global Seed Vault), cryopreservation in liquid nitrogen at -196 C, tissue culture.
- Wild Life (Protection) Act 1972 (amended 2022, schedules cut to four and CITES provisions added); Forest (Conservation) Act 1980, renamed Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam in 2023; Environment (Protection) Act 1986.
- Biological Diversity Act 2002 (amended 2023): three-tier structure - National Biodiversity Authority (Chennai), State Biodiversity Boards and Biodiversity Management Committees, which maintain People's Biodiversity Registers.
- Convention on Biological Diversity (Rio, 1992) with the Cartagena Protocol (biosafety of GM organisms) and Nagoya Protocol (access and benefit sharing, 2010).
- Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (CBD COP15, December 2022) includes the '30x30' target - protect 30% of land and 30% of oceans by 2030.
- Other conventions: CITES (1973, trade in wild species), CMS/Bonn Convention (migratory species), Ramsar Convention (1971, wetlands, signed in Ramsar, Iran), UNESCO World Heritage Convention.
- Project Tiger (1973) began with nine reserves; the 2022 tiger estimation found about 3,682 tigers (around 75% of the world's wild tigers); Jim Corbett (1936) was India's first national park.
- Project Elephant (1992); Project Cheetah relocated cheetahs from Namibia (September 2022) and South Africa to Kuno National Park, Madhya Pradesh; Gandhi Sagar sanctuary is the second site; India launched the International Big Cat Alliance in 2023.
- The Gangetic river dolphin is India's national aquatic animal (2009); Project Dolphin focuses on river and marine dolphins.
- Community conservation: Bishnois of Rajasthan and Punjab protect blackbuck and khejri trees; the Chipko movement (1973) protected Himalayan forests.

## Punjab angle

- Punjab's state symbols: blackbuck (state animal), baaz/northern goshawk (state bird), shisham (state tree) and Indus river dolphin (state aquatic animal, found in the Beas).
- Abohar Wildlife Sanctuary (Fazilka) protects blackbuck on village land with active support of the Bishnoi community; other sanctuaries include Harike, Bir Moti Bagh and Takhni-Rehmapur.
- Punjab's forest cover is among the lowest of all states (under 4% of its area per Forest Survey of India reports), with most remaining forests in the Shivalik (Kandi) belt.
- Harike and other Punjab wetlands host large numbers of migratory waterbirds every winter.

## How PPSC asks it

UPSC/PPSC frequently test in-situ vs ex-situ examples, convention-subject matching, IUCN categories, hotspots and species projects. Memorise conventions with their year and subject, plus Punjab's protected areas and state symbols.

## Deep dive

### Protected-area network — counts and categories (check the date)

- Four PA categories under the Wild Life (Protection) Act 1972: national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, conservation reserves and community reserves (the last two added by the 2002 amendment).
- Together these cover roughly 5% of India's geographical area — far below the '30x30' global target, which is why OECMs (community and private conserved areas) are being counted.
- Tiger reserves: 58 by 2026 — recent additions were Guru Ghasidas–Tamor Pingla (Chhattisgarh, 2024), Ratapani (MP, 2024) and Madhav (MP, March 2025, hailed as the 58th); Madhya Pradesh has the most.
- Largest tiger reserve: Nagarjunsagar-Srisailam (Andhra Pradesh–Telangana region); among the smallest: Bor (Maharashtra).
- Tiger estimation 2022: about 3,682 tigers (range 3,167–3,925), around 75% of the world's wild tigers; top states Madhya Pradesh (785), Karnataka (563), Uttarakhand (560), Maharashtra (444); the next four-yearly estimation cycle is under way.
- Biosphere reserves: 18 notified nationally (first: Nilgiri, 1986); 13 are in UNESCO's World Network after the Cold Desert BR (Spiti–Lahaul, Himachal; about 7,770 sq km) was added on 27 September 2025.
- Ramsar sites: 103 by October 2026 (latest included Sukhna, Chandigarh, and Sauj Jheel, Uttar Pradesh).
- Eco-sensitive zones: the Supreme Court (June 2022) ordered a 1 km minimum ESZ around every PA, then relaxed the blanket rule in April 2023.

### Laws, amendments and institutions (year — what changed)

- 1972 — Wild Life (Protection) Act; 1976 — 42nd Amendment moved 'forests' and 'protection of wild animals and birds' to the Concurrent List and added Articles 48A and 51A(g).
- 1980 — Forest (Conservation) Act; 2023 amendment renamed it Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam, exempted strategic linear projects within 100 km of borders, zoos, safaris and eco-tourism; in February 2024 the Supreme Court ordered that the broad 'dictionary meaning' of forest (Godavarman, 1996) continue to apply.
- 2002 — Biological Diversity Act; National Biodiversity Authority set up in Chennai (2003); 2023 amendment decriminalised offences and eased rules for AYUSH users.
- 2006 — Forest Rights Act (individual and community forest rights; critical wildlife habitats) and the WPA amendment creating the National Tiger Conservation Authority; Wildlife Crime Control Bureau followed in 2007.
- 2016 — Compensatory Afforestation Fund Act (CAMPA funds); 2017 — Wetlands (Conservation and Management) Rules.
- 2022 — WPA amendment: schedules cut from six to four (I and II animals, III plants, IV CITES specimens), CITES implemented in Indian law, invasive species regulated.
- National Board for Wildlife is chaired by the Prime Minister; its Standing Committee, chaired by the environment minister, clears projects inside PAs.

### Species recovery programmes and recent counts

- 1973 — Project Tiger (9 reserves); 1975 — crocodile conservation project (gharial, mugger and saltwater crocodile; Bhitarkanika and Satkosia in Odisha); 1992 — Project Elephant; 2009 — Project Snow Leopard.
- 2020 — Project Lion and Project Dolphin announced; 2022 — Project Cheetah (Namibia and South Africa to Kuno, MP), with Gandhi Sagar sanctuary (MP) as the second site from 2025.
- Vultures: veterinary diclofenac banned in 2006; aceclofenac and ketoprofen banned for animals in 2023; Vulture Action Plan 2020–25.
- Asiatic lions: the 2025 count in Gujarat recorded 891 lions across Gir and the Saurashtra landscape.
- Snow leopards: the first national assessment (released January 2024) estimated 718 in India; leopards (2022): about 13,874.
- River dolphins: India's first nationwide estimate (released March 2025) counted about 6,327, nearly all Ganges dolphins — only 3 Indus dolphins were recorded, all in Punjab's Beas.
- International Big Cat Alliance was launched by India in April 2023 for seven big cats, with its headquarters in India.
- Great Indian Bustard (fewer than about 150 left) is threatened by overhead power lines in Rajasthan and Gujarat; the Supreme Court ordered undergrounding in 2021 and modified the order in 2024 by setting up an expert committee.

### Biogeography and hotspots — deeper

- India's ten biogeographic zones (Rodgers and Panwar, WII): Trans-Himalaya, Himalaya, Desert, Semi-arid, Western Ghats, Deccan Peninsula, Gangetic Plain, North-East, Islands and Coasts; the Deccan Peninsula is the largest.
- Endemism is highest in the Western Ghats (amphibians, freshwater fish), the North-East (orchids) and the Andaman–Nicobar islands.
- There are 36 global biodiversity hotspots (Forests of East Australia added in 2016); India overlaps four.
- Like-Minded Megadiverse Countries group was formed at Cancún in 2002 — India is a member.
- Ecotone = transition zone between two ecosystems; the 'edge effect' gives ecotones more species than either side.
- Hotspot logic: high endemism plus severe habitat loss; megadiversity logic: sheer species richness — keep the two ideas separate in answers.

### IUCN, CITES, CMS, CBD — what gets asked

- Critically Endangered examples relevant to India: Great Indian Bustard, Jerdon's courser, Bengal florican, white-bellied heron, Indian, white-rumped, slender-billed and red-headed vultures, pygmy hog, Namdapha flying squirrel, Malabar civet.
- CITES (1973; India a party since 1976): Appendix I bans commercial trade, II regulates, III is a single country's listing. CoP20 at Samarkand, Uzbekistan (November–December 2025) added 77 species, moved whale shark, oceanic whitetip and manta/devil rays to Appendix I, and India blocked an EU bid to list guggul.
- CMS/Bonn Convention (India party since 1983): India hosted COP13 at Gandhinagar in 2020 (GIB mascot 'Gibi'); COP15 at Campo Grande, Brazil, ended on 29 March 2026 with 40 species added to the appendices.
- CBD COP16 (Cali, 2024) created the Cali Fund for benefits from digital sequence information and a permanent body on indigenous peoples; India released its updated NBSAP with 23 national targets aligned to the Kunming-Montreal framework.
- CBD COP17 (Yerevan, Armenia, ended 31 October 2026): global review found the world off-track on the 23 KMGBF targets; parties adopted the 'Yerevan Pathway' (cut harmful subsidies, align budgets), nearly 50 decisions and about US$2 billion in announced finance; COP18 goes to Ecuador in 2028.
- Secretariats: CBD in Montreal, CITES in Geneva (UNEP), CMS in Bonn, Ramsar in Gland (hosted by IUCN — Ramsar is not a UN convention).

### Punjab biodiversity file

- Punjab has about a dozen wildlife sanctuaries but no notified national park; important ones include Abohar (Fazilka; an 'open' sanctuary on Bishnoi village land protecting blackbuck), Harike, Bir Moti Bagh (Patiala), Takhni-Rehmapur (Hoshiarpur), Jhajjar-Bacholi (Rupnagar) and Kathlaur-Kushlian (Pathankot).
- Conservation reserves: Beas, Ropar wetland, Ranjit Sagar Dam and Rakh Sarai Amanat Khan; community reserves include Keshopur-Miani (Gurdaspur), Lalwan and Siswan.
- Indus dolphin: the 2018 Punjab survey of the Talwara–Harike stretch counted 5–11 animals including a calf; the 2025 national estimate recorded only 3 — a red flag for sand mining, barrages and pollution.
- Forest and tree cover is only about 6% of Punjab's area (2,985 sq km of 50,362 sq km in ISFR 2021), mostly in the Shivalik–Kandi belt; Punjab lost 456 sq km of forest and tree cover between ISFR 2019 and 2021.
- Harike and Keshopur host large flocks of winter migrants on the Central Asian Flyway (bar-headed geese, ducks, cranes).

### Traps and PPSC patterns

- Biosphere reserves are not created under the Wild Life (Protection) Act — they are a MoEFCC scheme under UNESCO's Man and the Biosphere programme (1971).
- National parks allow no grazing or private rights; sanctuaries may allow some regulated activity; community reserves are on private or community land.
- Hotspots number 36, not 34 or 35; India overlaps four (Himalaya, Indo-Burma, Western Ghats–Sri Lanka, Sundaland via Nicobar).
- Gangetic dolphin = national aquatic animal (2009); Indus dolphin = Punjab's state aquatic animal (2019).
- PPSC 2025 asked about Similipal, the Nagoya Protocol, tiger reserve–state pairs and biosphere reserve fauna — keep a state-wise table of tiger reserves and biosphere reserves.
- PPSC 2018 asked IUCN criteria, the Raptor MoU and the Indian vulture — expect conventions and threatened species every year.

## Practice MCQs (12)

**1.** Which of the following is an example of ex-situ conservation?

(a) National park  
(b) Seed bank  
(c) Sacred grove  
(d) Biosphere reserve

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Seed bank** — Ex-situ conservation protects species outside their natural habitat, such as in seed banks and zoos.

</details>

**2.** The Nagoya Protocol deals with:

(a) International trade in endangered species  
(b) Conservation of migratory wild animals  
(c) Access to genetic resources, benefit sharing  
(d) Biosafety of genetically modified organisms

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Access to genetic resources, benefit sharing** — Nagoya (2010) is on access and benefit sharing; Cartagena is on biosafety.

</details>
