## Short notes

- Primary pollutants are emitted directly (SO2, NOx, CO, particulate matter, VOCs); secondary pollutants form in the air (ground-level ozone, PAN, sulphate aerosols, acid rain).
- PM2.5 (fine particles 2.5 micrometres or smaller) reach deep into the lungs and bloodstream; PM10 are coarser; India's annual PM2.5 standard is 40 micrograms/m^3 (24-hour 60), WHO's 2021 annual guideline is 5.
- National Ambient Air Quality Standards (2009) cover 12 pollutants; the National AQI (2014) uses 8 pollutants and six bands: good (0-50), satisfactory, moderate, poor, very poor, severe (401-500).
- National Clean Air Programme (2019) targets particulate reduction in non-attainment cities; Delhi-NCR uses the Graded Response Action Plan and the Commission for Air Quality Management (2021).
- Classical (London) smog is sulphurous and forms in cold, humid winters; photochemical (Los Angeles) smog forms from NOx and hydrocarbons in sunlight, producing ozone and PAN.
- Acid rain (pH below 5.6) comes from SO2 and NOx forming sulphuric and nitric acids; it damages forests, lakes and marble monuments (e.g., the Taj Mahal's 'marble cancer').
- Carbon monoxide binds haemoglobin far more strongly than oxygen, forming carboxyhaemoglobin and starving tissues of oxygen.
- Bhopal gas tragedy (2-3 December 1984) was caused by methyl isocyanate leaking from the Union Carbide plant; it led to the Environment (Protection) Act 1986.
- Water pollution indicators: high BOD means heavy organic pollution and low dissolved oxygen; nutrient run-off (nitrates, phosphates) causes eutrophication and algal blooms.
- Toxic-element diseases: Minamata disease (mercury, Japan), Itai-itai (cadmium, Japan), blue baby syndrome (nitrate in water), fluorosis (fluoride), arsenicosis (arsenic), lead poisoning (nerve and brain damage in children).
- Bioaccumulation is build-up of a toxin within an organism; biomagnification is its increase at higher trophic levels - DDT caused thin eggshells in fish-eating birds.
- Toxicity is measured by LD50 (dose lethal to 50% of test animals) - a lower LD50 means a more toxic substance; Indian pesticide labels use colour triangles: red (extremely toxic), yellow, blue, green (least).
- Global chemical conventions: Stockholm (persistent organic pollutants, 2001), Basel (hazardous waste movement, 1989), Rotterdam (prior informed consent for chemicals, 1998), Minamata (mercury, 2013).
- Noise is measured in decibels; ambient standards (Noise Pollution Rules 2000) for daytime are 50 dB in silence zones, 55 residential, 65 commercial, 75 industrial.
- Plastic: identified single-use plastic items banned from 1 July 2022; minimum carry-bag thickness raised to 120 micrometres (December 2022); Extended Producer Responsibility applies to plastics and e-waste.
- Waste rules: Solid Waste Management Rules 2016, E-Waste (Management) Rules 2022, Bio-Medical Waste Rules 2016; e-waste contains lead, mercury and cadmium.
- Laws: Water Act 1974 (CPCB set up under it), Air Act 1981, Environment (Protection) Act 1986, National Green Tribunal Act 2010.
- Radioactive pollution (Chernobyl 1986, Fukushima 2011) causes ionising-radiation damage; thermal pollution from power plants lowers oxygen in water; light pollution disturbs wildlife.

## Punjab angle

- Paddy-straw burning in October-November is a major seasonal air-pollution source; farm-fire counts in Punjab have fallen markedly since 2021-22 with in-situ (Happy Seeder, Super SMS) and ex-situ (biomass, CBG) residue management.
- Budha Nullah in Ludhiana carries industrial and dairy effluent to the Sutlej; Sant Balbir Singh Seechewal led the community clean-up of the Kali Bein rivulet.
- Uranium, arsenic, nitrate and fluoride have been reported in groundwater of several Malwa and border districts, linked with health concerns.
- Heavy pesticide and fertiliser use in the Malwa cotton-paddy belt raises concerns about residues in soil, water and food.

## Deep dive

### Air quality — standards, indices and programmes

- NAAQS (2009) cover 12 pollutants: PM10, PM2.5, SO2, NO2, CO, ozone, ammonia, lead, benzene, benzo(a)pyrene, arsenic and nickel.
- National AQI uses 8 of them: PM10, PM2.5, NO2, SO2, CO, ozone, ammonia and lead; the AQI reflects the worst sub-index.
- WHO Air Quality Guidelines (2021): PM2.5 annual 5 µg/m³ and 24-hour 15; PM10 annual 15 and 24-hour 45; NO2 annual 10.
- National Clean Air Programme (January 2019): about 130 non-attainment and million-plus cities; target revised to a 40% cut in PM10 by 2025-26 from 2017-18; PRANA portal tracks it; Swachh Vayu Survekshan ranks cities each September.
- Punjab's NCAP cities include Ludhiana, Jalandhar, Amritsar, Patiala, Mandi Gobindgarh, Khanna, Naya Nangal, Dera Bassi and Dera Baba Nanak.
- GRAP stages for Delhi-NCR: I Poor (201–300), II Very Poor (301–400), III Severe (401–450), IV Severe+ (above 450).
- Commission for Air Quality Management Act 2021 covers NCR and adjoining areas including Punjab; in November 2024 environmental compensation for stubble burning was doubled (₹5,000, ₹10,000 or ₹30,000 per incident depending on land size).
- Vehicles moved straight from BS-IV to BS-VI on 1 April 2020; Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana (2016) cuts household (indoor) smoke through LPG.

### Water and soil pollution — indicators and data

- CPCB primary criteria for outdoor bathing water: dissolved oxygen at least 5 mg/l, BOD 3 mg/l or less, faecal coliform below 2,500 MPN per 100 ml, pH 6.5–8.5.
- BOD measures oxygen used by microbes to break down organic matter; COD measures oxygen needed to chemically oxidise all matter — COD is always higher.
- CPCB's 2022 assessment identified 311 polluted river stretches on 279 rivers based on BOD.
- Drinking water limits (BIS IS 10500): fluoride 1.0 mg/l acceptable (1.5 permissible), arsenic 0.01 mg/l, nitrate 45 mg/l, uranium 0.03 mg/l.
- Microplastics are pieces under 5 mm (PPSC 2025 asked); nanoplastics are smaller than 1 micrometre and can cross cell membranes.
- Soil pollution sources: cadmium from phosphate fertilisers, pesticide residues, heavy metals from industrial effluent and e-waste, and plastic mulch fragments.
- Eutrophication creates algal blooms and 'dead zones' (Gulf of Mexico); harmful algal blooms ('red tides') can poison fish and shellfish.

### Toxicology vocabulary and occupational diseases

- LD50 (lethal dose for 50% of test animals) and LC50 (lethal concentration); acute toxicity is short-term, chronic is long-term low-level exposure.
- Maximum residue limits for pesticides in food are set by FSSAI; pesticides are registered under the Insecticides Act 1968 (a Pesticide Management Bill has been pending since 2020).
- Endocrine disruptors such as bisphenol-A, phthalates and DDT mimic or block hormones even at low doses.
- The original 'dirty dozen' POPs under the Stockholm Convention: aldrin, chlordane, DDT, dieldrin, endrin, heptachlor, hexachlorobenzene, mirex, toxaphene, PCBs, dioxins and furans.
- Disease pairs: Minamata (mercury), Itai-itai (cadmium), blackfoot disease (arsenic, Taiwan), plumbism (lead), fluorosis (fluoride), silicosis (silica dust in stone quarrying), byssinosis (cotton dust), asbestosis (asbestos), black lung or pneumoconiosis (coal dust).
- Endosulfan was banned nationwide by the Supreme Court in 2011 after the Kasaragod (Kerala) aerial spraying tragedy.

### Laws, courts and institutions (year — milestone)

- 1974 — Water Act (Central Board, renamed Central Pollution Control Board in 1988); the Water Cess Act 1977 was repealed in 2017 with the GST transition.
- 1981 — Air Act; 1986 — Environment (Protection) Act (umbrella law; Section 3 powers and Section 5 directions); 1991 — Public Liability Insurance Act; 2010 — National Green Tribunal Act (principal bench Delhi; zonal benches Bhopal, Pune, Kolkata, Chennai).
- 2023–24 — Jan Vishwas Act decriminalised many offences under the Air and Environment Acts; Water (Amendment) Act 2024 replaced imprisonment for minor violations with penalties.
- Rules: Hazardous and Other Wastes Rules 2016, Bio-Medical Waste Rules 2016, Construction and Demolition Waste Rules 2016, Plastic Waste Management Rules 2016 (amended 2021–22), Battery Waste Management Rules 2022, E-Waste (Management) Rules 2022 (in force from April 2023).
- Landmark cases: M.C. Mehta v Union of India (Oleum gas leak, 1986–87 — 'absolute liability'), Indian Council for Enviro-Legal Action (1996 — polluter pays, Bichhri), M.C. Mehta (Taj Trapezium, 1996; CNG for Delhi transport, 1998).
- Online Continuous Emission/Effluent Monitoring is mandatory for 17 categories of highly polluting industries.

### Global chemicals and waste regime

- Basel Convention (1989, in force 1992) on transboundary movement of hazardous wastes; the Ban Amendment is in force and plastic-waste amendments apply from 2021.
- Rotterdam Convention (1998) on prior informed consent for hazardous chemicals; Stockholm Convention (2001, in force 2004) on persistent organic pollutants.
- Minamata Convention on Mercury (2013, in force 2017); India ratified it in 2018.
- Global Framework on Chemicals (Bonn, 2023) replaced SAICM as the voluntary umbrella for chemical safety.
- Global plastics treaty: UNEA-5.2 (2022) gave the mandate; talks at Busan (2024) and Geneva (INC-5.2, August 2025) ended without agreement over production caps.
- Marine dumping: London Convention (1972) and MARPOL (1973/78) for ship pollution.

### Punjab pollution file

- Punjab Pollution Control Board (constituted 1975) enforces the Water, Air and EP Acts and runs the CETPs for Ludhiana's dyeing clusters.
- Paddy straw: Punjab produces about 20 million tonnes a year; options are in-situ (Happy Seeder, Super SMS, Surface Seeder), ex-situ (biomass power, CBG, boilers) and mandated co-firing of biomass pellets in thermal plants.
- Ludhiana's Giaspura gas leak (April 2023, hydrogen sulphide from sewers) killed 11 people — an example of sewer-industrial waste mixing.
- Buddha Nullah carries Ludhiana's industrial and dairy effluent to the Sutlej; downstream pollution reaches Harike and the canals feeding south-west Punjab and Rajasthan.
- In 2018 the NGT set up a monitoring committee headed by Justice (retd) Jasbir Singh for Punjab's rivers and solid waste; in 2022 it imposed about ₹2,080 crore environmental compensation on Punjab for waste mismanagement.
- Groundwater contaminants: uranium and nitrate in Malwa, arsenic in parts of Majha (Amritsar, Tarn Taran, Gurdaspur), fluoride in south-west districts.
- Community model: Sant Balbir Singh Seechewal's Kali Bein clean-up and the 'Seechewal model' of pond wastewater treatment; he received the Padma Shri (2017).

### Traps

- NAAQS has 12 pollutants; AQI uses 8 — options often swap the two numbers.
- High BOD = more organic pollution and less oxygen; high DO = cleaner water.
- London smog is sulphurous and forms in cold, damp winters; photochemical smog needs sunlight (ozone, PAN).
- Basel = hazardous waste; Rotterdam = prior informed consent; Stockholm = POPs; Minamata = mercury; Montreal = ozone.
- Lower LD50 means more toxic, not less.
- Bioaccumulation happens inside one organism over time; biomagnification is the rise in concentration up the food chain — options often swap the terms.

**4.** The Stockholm Convention deals with:

(a) Transboundary movement of hazardous waste  
(b) Ozone-depleting substances  
(c) Persistent organic pollutants  
(d) Mercury pollution

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Persistent organic pollutants** — Stockholm (2001) targets POPs like DDT and PCBs; Basel covers hazardous waste and Minamata mercury.

**5.** Photochemical smog is mainly characterised by the presence of:

(a) Chlorofluorocarbons  
(b) Carbon monoxide only  
(c) Ozone and peroxyacetyl nitrate (PAN)  
(d) Sulphur dioxide and soot

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Ozone and peroxyacetyl nitrate (PAN)** — Sunlight acting on NOx and hydrocarbons produces ozone and PAN.

</details>

**(c) Extremely toxic** — Red = extremely toxic, yellow = highly toxic, blue = moderately toxic, green = slightly toxic.
