## Short notes

- Arrhenius view: acids give H+ (as H3O+) ions in water, bases give OH- ions; water-soluble bases are alkalis (NaOH, KOH).
- pH = negative log of H+ concentration; scale 0-14, 7 neutral at 25 C, below 7 acidic, above 7 basic; each unit is a tenfold change.
- Useful pH values: human blood about 7.4; stomach is strongly acidic (HCl, pH about 1.5-3.5); tooth enamel starts decaying below pH 5.5; rain below pH 5.6 is acid rain.
- Indicators: litmus (from lichen) turns red in acid, blue in base; phenolphthalein colourless in acid, pink in base; methyl orange red in acid, yellow in base; turmeric turns reddish-brown in base.
- Natural acids: citric (lemon, orange), lactic (curd), tartaric (tamarind, grapes), oxalic (tomato, spinach), acetic (vinegar), methanoic/formic (ant and nettle sting), malic (apple), ascorbic (amla, citrus).
- Neutralisation: acid + base gives salt + water. Uses: antacids such as milk of magnesia (Mg(OH)2) for acidity; baking soda on ant stings; lime for acidic soils; gypsum for alkaline/sodic soils.
- Metal + dilute acid gives salt + hydrogen gas; carbonate/bicarbonate + acid gives CO2, which turns lime water milky.
- Strong acids: HCl, H2SO4 (king of chemicals), HNO3. Always add acid to water slowly, never water to acid, because dilution is highly exothermic.
- Aqua regia (3 parts conc. HCl : 1 part conc. HNO3) dissolves gold and platinum.
- Chlor-alkali process: electrolysis of brine (NaCl solution) gives NaOH, Cl2 and H2.
- Key salts: baking soda NaHCO3 (antacid, baking powder, soda-acid fire extinguisher); washing soda Na2CO3.10H2O (removes permanent hardness); bleaching powder CaOCl2 (disinfects water); plaster of Paris CaSO4.1/2H2O (from gypsum CaSO4.2H2O, used for casts).
- Water of crystallisation: blue copper sulphate CuSO4.5H2O turns white on heating as it loses water.
- Corrosion is the slow electrochemical eating away of metals by air, moisture and chemicals; iron rusts only when both oxygen and water are present; salt water speeds it up.
- Rust is hydrated iron(III) oxide (Fe2O3.xH2O); silver tarnishes black (silver sulphide); copper gains a green coat (basic copper carbonate).
- Prevention: painting, oiling, greasing, galvanising (zinc coating), tinning, chrome plating, anodising aluminium, alloying (stainless steel = iron + chromium + nickel) and sacrificial protection with Zn or Mg.
- Soaps are sodium or potassium salts of long-chain fatty acids, made by saponification of oils/fats with alkali; glycerol is the by-product.
- Cleaning action: soap molecules form micelles - hydrophobic hydrocarbon tails hold oily dirt, ionic heads face water - so dirt is emulsified and rinsed away.
- Hard water (Ca and Mg salts) forms insoluble scum with soap; temporary hardness (bicarbonates) is removed by boiling, permanent hardness (chlorides, sulphates) by washing soda or ion exchange; detergents (sulphonate salts) work even in hard water.

## Punjab angle

- Large areas of south-west Punjab have saline and sodic (alkaline) soils and brackish groundwater; gypsum application is the standard reclamation method.
- High groundwater hardness and salinity in Malwa districts make reverse-osmosis drinking-water plants common in villages.

## Deep dive

### Acid–base theories, strength and industrial chemistry

- Brønsted–Lowry (1923): an acid donates a proton, a base accepts one; every acid has a conjugate base. Water is amphoteric — it can act as either.
- Lewis (1923): an acid accepts an electron pair (BF3, AlCl3), a base donates one (NH3) — explains reactions without H+ ions.
- Strong vs weak refers to the degree of ionisation (HCl strong, acetic acid weak); concentrated vs dilute refers only to the amount of water.
- Industrial processes: Haber (ammonia, iron catalyst, about 450 C and 200 atm) — base of urea; Contact (sulphuric acid, V2O5 catalyst, via oleum); Ostwald (nitric acid, platinum catalyst).
- Amphoteric oxides such as Al2O3 and ZnO react with both acids and bases.
- Buffers resist pH change; blood stays near pH 7.35–7.45 through the carbonic acid–bicarbonate buffer, and departures cause acidosis or alkalosis.
- Approximate pH ladder: lemon juice about 2, vinegar about 3, coffee about 5, normal rain about 5.6, milk about 6.5, sea water about 8, baking-soda solution about 8–9, milk of magnesia about 10, household ammonia about 11–12, bleach or lye about 13.

### Salts and chemicals in daily life — Lucent-style table

- Iodised salt carries potassium iodate (National Iodine Deficiency Disorders Control Programme); double-fortified salt adds iron as well.
- Potash alum is a coagulant that settles mud in water and is used as an aftershave antiseptic.
- Blue vitriol (CuSO4.5H2O) with lime makes Bordeaux mixture, a fungicide; green vitriol is FeSO4.7H2O; Epsom salt MgSO4.7H2O; Glauber's salt Na2SO4.10H2O.
- Food preservatives: sodium benzoate and potassium metabisulphite; monosodium glutamate (MSG) is a flavour enhancer.
- Calcium carbide (it releases acetylene) is banned for ripening fruit under FSSAI rules; ethylene gas is the permitted ripening agent.
- Silver nitrate makes the indelible ink used in elections (made by Mysore Paints and Varnish Ltd); silver bromide was the light-sensitive chemical in photographic film.
- Potassium permanganate and bleaching powder disinfect wells; quicklime (CaO) becomes slaked lime (Ca(OH)2) used in whitewash, which hardens to shiny CaCO3 by absorbing CO2.
- Gypsum is added to cement to slow setting; urea contains about 46% nitrogen; India made all urea neem-coated in 2015 to slow nitrogen release and stop diversion to industry.

### Acid rain, soils and water quality

- Acid rain forms when SO2 and NOx become sulphuric and nitric acids (pH below 5.6); it damages forests, lakes and marble — 'marble cancer' of the Taj Mahal led the Supreme Court (M.C. Mehta case) to create the Taj Trapezium Zone curbs.
- Oceans absorb roughly a quarter of human CO2 emissions; surface pH has fallen from about 8.2 to about 8.1 since pre-industrial times, harming corals and shell-forming organisms.
- Acidic soils (north-east, laterite areas) are treated with lime; alkaline/sodic soils (Punjab, Haryana, UP) with gypsum or pyrite. ICAR-CSSRI, Karnal, specialises in salt-affected soil reclamation.
- Fluoride above 1.5 mg/L causes dental and skeletal fluorosis; the Nalgonda technique (alum plus lime) removes fluoride cheaply.
- Arsenic in groundwater of the Indo-Gangetic plain causes skin lesions and cancers; nitrate excess causes 'blue baby syndrome'.
- Hard water: temporary hardness is removed by boiling or Clark's method (adding lime); permanent hardness by washing soda, ion-exchange (zeolite/permutit) or reverse osmosis.

### Corrosion — chemistry and protection

- Rusting is electrochemical: iron is oxidised at anodic spots, oxygen is reduced at cathodic spots, and dissolved salt speeds the current — so coastal areas and salted roads corrode vehicles faster.
- Galvanising works even after a scratch because zinc is more reactive and corrodes first (sacrificial protection); tin plating protects only while intact — a scratched tin can rusts quickly because tin is less reactive than iron.
- Cathodic protection: magnesium or zinc blocks are fixed to ship hulls, buried pipelines and water heaters so they corrode instead of the steel.
- Aluminium resists corrosion because a thin oxide film forms instantly; anodising thickens this film.
- The Iron Pillar at Mehrauli (Gupta period) resists rust thanks to high phosphorus content and a protective surface layer.
- A NACE International study estimated the global cost of corrosion at about 3.4% of world GDP (2013 data).
- Reactivity series (K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > H > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au): a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one from its salt solution.

### Soaps, detergents and cleaning — exam angles

- Sodium salts of fatty acids give hard (bar) soaps; potassium salts give soft or liquid soaps and shaving creams.
- Soap quality is graded by Total Fatty Matter (TFM) under BIS: Grade 1 at least 76%, Grade 2 at least 70%, Grade 3 at least 60%.
- Detergents (alkyl benzene sulphonates, alkyl sulphates) work in hard water because their calcium and magnesium salts stay dissolved.
- Branched-chain detergents resist biodegradation and cause river foam; phosphate builders feed algae and cause eutrophication — visible as froth on the Yamuna in Delhi.
- Soap molecules form micelles only above a critical concentration; bile salts emulsify fats in the gut in a similar way.
- Soap destroys the fatty envelope of viruses such as SARS-CoV-2; hand sanitisers need roughly 60–70% alcohol to be effective.
- Dry cleaning uses organic solvents such as tetrachloroethylene instead of water; glycerol, the by-product of soap making, is used in cosmetics and to make nitroglycerine (dynamite, Alfred Nobel).

### Punjab links

- Saline–sodic soils and waterlogging in south-west Punjab (Sri Muktsar Sahib, Fazilka, parts of Bathinda and Mansa) are reclaimed with gypsum and drainage.
- Arsenic in groundwater has been reported in Majha districts such as Amritsar, Tarn Taran and Gurdaspur, while Malwa faces uranium and fluoride problems — hence village RO plants.
- Effluents from Ludhiana's dyeing and electroplating units (acids, metals, dyes) pollute the Buddha Nullah, a tributary of the Sutlej, prompting rejuvenation projects.
- National Fertilizers Ltd runs urea units at Nangal and Bathinda in Punjab — ammonia from the Haber process is the starting point.
- Heavy nitrogen fertiliser use in the rice–wheat system raises nitrate in groundwater and slowly acidifies and degrades soils.
- Soil Health Cards report pH and electrical conductivity; PAU advises gypsum doses based on soil test results for sodic soils.

## Practice MCQs (12)

**1.** The chemical formula of plaster of Paris is:

(a) CaOCl2  
(b) CaSO4.2H2O  
(c) CaSO4.1/2H2O  
(d) Na2CO3.10H2O

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) CaSO4.1/2H2O** — Plaster of Paris is calcium sulphate hemihydrate, obtained by heating gypsum (CaSO4.2H2O).

</details>

**2.** The sting of an ant or nettle contains:

(a) Acetic acid  
(b) Citric acid  
(c) Methanoic (formic) acid  
(d) Oxalic acid

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Methanoic (formic) acid** — Formic acid causes the pain; a mild base like baking soda gives relief.

</details>

**3.** Consider: 1. Rusting of iron requires both oxygen and water. 2. Galvanisation protects iron by coating it with tin. Which is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) 1 only** — Rusting needs air and moisture together; galvanising uses zinc, not tin (tinning uses tin).

</details>

**4.** Which mixture is known as aqua regia?

(a) Concentrated HNO3 and HCl in 3:1  
(b) Concentrated HCl and HNO3 in 3:1  
(c) Concentrated HCl and H2SO4 in 3:1  
(d) Concentrated H2SO4 and HNO3 in 1:1

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Concentrated HCl and HNO3 in 3:1** — Three parts hydrochloric acid to one part nitric acid; it can dissolve gold and platinum.

</details>

**5.** Soap does not lather well in hard water because:

(a) Hard water contains dissolved oxygen  
(b) Calcium and magnesium ions form insoluble scum with soap  
(c) Hard water is strongly acidic  
(d) Soap molecules become non-polar in hard water

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Calcium and magnesium ions form insoluble scum with soap** — Ca2+ and Mg2+ react with soap to form insoluble precipitates, wasting soap.

</details>

**6.** Tooth decay starts when the pH in the mouth falls below about:

(a) 5.5  
(b) 7.0  
(c) 8.5  
(d) 3.0

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) 5.5** — Below pH 5.5, acids from bacteria acting on food begin to dissolve tooth enamel (calcium phosphate).

</details>

**7.** Consider the following statements:  
1. Galvanised iron stays protected from rusting even if the zinc layer is scratched.  
2. Tin-plated iron also stays protected after the tin layer is scratched.  
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) 1 only** — Zinc is more reactive and corrodes sacrificially; tin is less reactive than iron, so scratched tin plate rusts faster.

</details>

**8.** Consider the following pairs (process — product):  
1. Haber process — nitric acid  
2. Contact process — sulphuric acid  
3. Ostwald process — nitric acid  
Which of the pairs given above are correctly matched?

(a) 1 and 2 only  
(b) 2 and 3 only  
(c) 1 and 3 only  
(d) 1, 2 and 3

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) 2 and 3 only** — Pair 1 is wrong: the Haber process makes ammonia, while nitric acid comes from the Ostwald process, which oxidises ammonia. Pairs 2 and 3 are correct: the Contact process gives sulphuric acid and the Ostwald process gives nitric acid.

</details>

**9.** Which substance is commonly used as a coagulant to settle mud in drinking water?

(a) Calcium carbide  
(b) Washing soda  
(c) Potash alum  
(d) Baking soda

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Potash alum** — Alum makes suspended particles clump and settle.

</details>

**10.** Persistent foam seen on some Indian rivers is mainly caused by detergents that are:

(a) Made from readily degradable vegetable oils  
(b) Strongly acidic and corrosive in water  
(c) Branched-chain and poorly biodegradable  
(d) Completely insoluble in river water

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Branched-chain and poorly biodegradable** — Branched-chain surfactants resist bacterial breakdown and froth.

</details>

**11.** The indelible ink used in Indian elections contains:

(a) Potassium permanganate  
(b) Silver nitrate  
(c) Copper sulphate  
(d) Silver bromide

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Silver nitrate** — Silver nitrate reacts with skin proteins and darkens in light.

</details>

**12.** Human blood maintains a pH of about 7.4 mainly through the:

(a) Urea made in the liver  
(b) Carbonic acid–bicarbonate buffer  
(c) Bile salts from the liver  
(d) Hydrochloric acid of the stomach

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Carbonic acid–bicarbonate buffer** — The bicarbonate buffer neutralises excess acid or base in blood.

</details>
