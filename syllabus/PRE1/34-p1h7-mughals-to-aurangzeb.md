# Mughals to Aurangzeb

> Official syllabus line: The Mughals (political, social & cultural till Aurangzeb).

## Short notes

- Babur won the First Battle of Panipat (1526) against Ibrahim Lodi using the tulughma flanking tactic and artillery (gunners Ustad Ali and Mustafa).
- Babur then defeated Rana Sanga at Khanwa (1527), Medini Rai at Chanderi (1528) and the Afghans at Ghaghra (1529); his memoir Baburnama (Tuzuk-i-Baburi) is in Turki.
- Humayun lost to Sher Shah at Chausa (1539) and Kannauj (1540), lived in exile, and regained Delhi in 1555 after battles at Machhiwara and Sirhind; died in 1556 after a fall in his library.
- Sher Shah Suri (1540–45): silver rupiya, land measurement with patta and qabuliyat, sarais and the road from Sonargaon to Peshawar (later Grand Trunk Road); tomb at Sasaram.
- Akbar (1556–1605) was crowned at Kalanaur; Bairam Khan was regent; Hemu was defeated at the Second Battle of Panipat (1556).
- Akbar's Rajput policy: marriage alliance with Amber (1562), high mansabs for Rajputs; abolished pilgrim tax (1563) and jizya (1564); Maharana Pratap resisted (Haldighati, 1576).
- Religious policy: Ibadat Khana at Fatehpur Sikri (1575), Mahzar (1579), Din-i-Ilahi/Tauhid-i-Ilahi (1582), and sulh-i-kul (peace with all).
- Mansabdari system: ranks with zat (personal) and sawar (cavalry) numbers; mansabdars were paid by jagirs or cash; system was transferable, not hereditary.
- Revenue: Raja Todar Mal's zabt and dahsala system (1580) based on average produce and prices of ten years; Ain-i-Akbari of Abul Fazl describes it.
- Akbar's court: Abul Fazl (Akbarnama, Ain-i-Akbari), Faizi, Birbal, Tansen; Mahabharata translated into Persian as Razmnama; Buland Darwaza marks the Gujarat conquest.
- Jahangir (1605–27): chain of justice; Nur Jahan's influence; English envoys William Hawkins (1608) and Sir Thomas Roe (1615); peak of miniature painting (Ustad Mansur, Abul Hasan).
- Shah Jahan (1628–58): Taj Mahal, Red Fort and Jama Masjid of Shahjahanabad, Peacock Throne; pietra dura and white marble; war of succession ended with Aurangzeb's victory at Samugarh (1658).
- Aurangzeb (1658–1707): reimposed jizya (1679), banned music at court, compiled Fatawa-i-Alamgiri; annexed Bijapur (1686) and Golconda (1687).
- Aurangzeb faced revolts of Marathas (Shivaji; Treaty of Purandar, 1665), Jats, Satnamis, Rajputs and Sikhs; died at Ahmednagar (1707), buried at Khuldabad.
- Provincial administration: subas (12 under Akbar, later 15) under subedar, diwan (revenue), bakhshi (military pay), sadr (religious grants), qazi (justice), faujdar and kotwal.
- Culture: Humayun's tomb (built by Bega Begum) introduced the charbagh and double dome; Mughal painting began with Mir Sayyid Ali and Abdus Samad; Persian court culture blended with Indian traditions; Urdu developed.
- Economy and Europeans: thriving textiles and trade; the English got permission to set up a factory at Surat in 1613 under Jahangir.

## Punjab angle

- Akbar was crowned at Kalanaur (Gurdaspur) in 1556; Humayun's comeback victories at Machhiwara (Ludhiana) and Sirhind (1555) were fought in Punjab.
- Guru Arjan Dev was executed at Lahore in 1606 under Jahangir; Guru Hargobind then built the Akal Takht and adopted miri-piri (temporal and spiritual authority).
- Guru Tegh Bahadur was executed at Delhi in 1675 under Aurangzeb; Guru Gobind Singh created the Khalsa at Anandpur Sahib in 1699; the younger Sahibzadas were martyred at Sirhind.
- Sikh tradition records Akbar's visit to Guru Amar Das at Goindwal, where he ate in the langar.

## How PPSC asks it

Questions test battles and years, Akbar's administrative and religious measures, books and authors, and monuments by ruler. Punjab-linked Sikh-Mughal events are favourites for PPSC.

## Deep dive

### Ruler and battle file, 1526–1707 (year — event)

- Babur (1526–30): Panipat I (21 April 1526), Khanwa (1527, declared jihad, gave up wine, tamgha remitted), Chanderi (1528), Ghaghra (1529, Afghans of Bihar–Bengal).
- Humayun (1530–40, 1555–56): Chausa (1539) and Kannauj/Bilgram (1540) lost to Sher Khan; exile in Persia (Shah Tahmasp); retook Kabul, Kandahar; won Machhiwara and Sirhind (1555).
- Sur interregnum: Sher Shah (1540–45; died at Kalinjar), Islam Shah (1545–53); Hemu (Vikramaditya) won Delhi in 1556 before losing at Panipat II (5 November 1556).
- Akbar (1556–1605): Bairam Khan's regency ends 1560; 'petticoat government' (1560–62); conquests — Malwa (1561), Chittor (1568), Ranthambore (1569), Gujarat (1572–73), Bengal (1576), Kashmir (1586), Sind (1591), Asirgarh (1601).
- Haldighati (18 June 1576): Man Singh vs Maharana Pratap (Hakim Khan Sur on Pratap's side); Pratap continued resistance from Chavand.
- Jahangir (1605–27): Khusrau's revolt (1606); treaty with Mewar (1615, Amar Singh); Kangra fort (1620); Kandahar lost to Persia (1622); Mahabat Khan's coup (1626).
- Shah Jahan (1628–58): Ahmadnagar annexed (1633); Deccan settlement (1636, Golconda and Bijapur tributary); Balkh–Badakhshan campaign (1646–47) failed; Kandahar lost finally (1649–53).
- War of succession (1657–59): Dharmat and Samugarh (1658), Khajwa (1659), Deorai (1659); Aurangzeb crowned twice (1658, 1659); Dara Shikoh executed 1659.
- Aurangzeb (1658–1707): Jat (Gokula, 1669), Satnami (1672), Afghan (Yusufzai), Sikh and Maratha resistance; Treaty of Purandar with Shivaji (1665); Bijapur (1686) and Golconda (1687) annexed; died at Ahmednagar (1707), buried at Khuldabad.
- Ahom frontier: Mir Jumla's Assam campaign (1662); Battle of Saraighat (1671) — Lachit Borphukan defeated the Mughal force under Ram Singh.

### Administration and agrarian system — deeper layer

- Central ministers: wakil (prime minister, reduced after Bairam Khan), wazir/diwan-i-kul (finance), mir bakhshi (military pay, mansab records, intelligence), mir saman/khan-i-saman (royal karkhanas), sadr-us-sudur (grants, charity), qazi-ul-quzat (justice), muhtasib (public morals — enforced by Aurangzeb).
- Provinces (subas): 12 under Akbar (1580), about 21 under Aurangzeb; suba → sarkar (faujdar, amalguzar) → pargana (shiqdar, amil, qanungo) → village (muqaddam, patwari); kotwal in towns.
- Mansabdari: introduced 1573–75, zat and sawar ranks from 1595–96; mansabs from 10 to 10,000 for nobles (higher for princes); Jahangir created du-aspa sih-aspa ranks; Shah Jahan's 'month-scale' adjusted pay to actual jagir yield.
- Jagir (assignment of revenue, transferable every 3–4 years) vs khalisa (crown land); watan jagirs for Rajput chiefs in their homelands were hereditary.
- Revenue methods: zabt (measurement, dahsala of 1580 — average of 10 years' produce and prices, cash rates in dasturs), ghalla-bakhshi/batai (crop sharing), kankut (estimation), nasaq (assessment on past record).
- Land classes (Ain-i-Akbari): polaj (cultivated every year), parauti (left fallow 1–2 years), chachar (3–4 years), banjar (5 or more years).
- Madad-i-maash: tax-free land grants to scholars and religious men; Aurangzeb made them hereditary.
- Coinage: silver rupiya (Sher Shah's 178-grain standard continued), gold mohur, copper dam (40 dams = 1 rupee); Jahangir's zodiac coins; Akbar's 'Allahu Akbar' and Ilahi coins.
- 'Jagirdari crisis' (Satish Chandra) — shortage of paibaqi (unassigned) land vs growing number of mansabdars under Aurangzeb, aggravated by Deccan wars.

### Religious policy timeline (year — measure)

- 1562 — Akbar stops enslavement of prisoners of war; Amber marriage alliance; 1563 — pilgrim tax abolished; 1564 — jizya abolished.
- 1575 — Ibadat Khana at Fatehpur Sikri (open to all faiths from 1578); 1579 — Mahzar (Akbar as Imam-i-Adil, arbiter in disputes among ulema).
- 1580 — First Jesuit mission (Rudolf Acquaviva, Antonio Monserrate) reaches Fatehpur Sikri; 1582 — Tauhid-i-Ilahi (Din-i-Ilahi) discipleship; about 18 members, including Birbal.
- 1584 — Ilahi era (Tarikh-i-Ilahi) dating from his accession; Maktab Khana translations: Mahabharata as Razmnama, Ramayana, Lilavati (by Faizi), Atharva Veda.
- Jahangir — continued tolerance in general, 'chain of justice', but executed Guru Arjan Dev (1606) after Khusrau's revolt.
- Shah Jahan — dropped sijda; orders (1633) against new temples; patronage of orthodox and Sufi alike; Dara Shikoh's Majma-ul-Bahrain (1655) and Sirr-i-Akbar (1657, Persian Upanishads).
- Aurangzeb — banned court music and jharokha darshan (1668–69), orders against temples including Vishwanath, Varanasi and Keshava Rai, Mathura (1669–70); reimposed jizya (1679); compiled Fatawa-i-Alamgiri.
- Balance for Mains: Aurangzeb also gave grants to temples (e.g., Umananda, Mahakaleshwar, Balaji — firmans exist) and employed more Hindu (largely Maratha and Rajput) mansabdars than Akbar.

### Sources, literature and art (author/artist — work — reign)

- Babur — Baburnama/Tuzuk-i-Baburi (Turki), describes Hindustan's flora, fauna, people; Gulbadan Begum — Humayun-nama (Persian).
- Abul Fazl — Akbarnama (3 vols; vol 3 = Ain-i-Akbari); Badauni — Muntakhab-ut-Tawarikh (critical of Akbar); Nizamuddin Ahmad — Tabaqat-i-Akbari.
- Jahangir — Tuzuk-i-Jahangiri (autobiography); Shah Jahan's reign — Padshahnama (Abdul Hamid Lahori); Aurangzeb's reign — Maasir-i-Alamgiri (Saqi Mustaid Khan), Muntakhab-ul-Lubab (Khafi Khan, written secretly).
- European travellers: Ralph Fitch (Akbar), William Hawkins and Thomas Roe (Jahangir), Peter Mundy, François Bernier (Shah Jahan–Aurangzeb; 'Travels in the Mogul Empire'), Tavernier (gem trade), Niccolao Manucci ('Storia do Mogor').
- Painting: Mir Sayyid Ali and Abdus Samad (brought by Humayun) led the Hamzanama project; Daswanth and Basawan under Akbar; Jahangir's ateliers — Ustad Mansur (natural history), Abul Hasan ('Nadir-uz-Zaman'), Bishandas (portraits).
- Music: Tansen (Gwalior, trained by Swami Haridas by tradition) at Akbar's court; despite Aurangzeb's ban, many Persian treatises on Indian music date from his reign.
- Architecture: Humayun's tomb (1560s–70s; char-bagh, double dome), Agra fort, Fatehpur Sikri (Buland Darwaza after Gujarat), Sikandra (Akbar's tomb), Itimad-ud-Daula (Nur Jahan; early pietra dura), Taj Mahal (1632–53), Red Fort and Jama Masjid Delhi, Badshahi mosque Lahore (1673), Bibi ka Maqbara (Aurangabad).

### Punjab under the Mughals and the Guru–Mughal relationship

- Lahore suba was among the richest; Akbar held court at Lahore (1585–98) to watch the north-west; Jahangir is buried at Shahdara (Lahore).
- Akbar was crowned at Kalanaur (Gurdaspur) in February 1556; Sikh tradition records his visit to Guru Amar Das at Goindwal and a land grant to Guru Ram Das (Amritsar).
- 1604 — Adi Granth installed at Harmandir Sahib; 1606 — Guru Arjan Dev martyred at Lahore; Guru Hargobind adopts miri–piri, builds Akal Takht (1606–09), held at Gwalior fort and released (Bandi Chhor).
- 1628–34 — Guru Hargobind's battles with Mughal forces (Amritsar, Kartarpur and others); shift to Kiratpur; Guru Har Rai's sympathy for Dara Shikoh; Guru Harkrishan dies at Delhi (1664).
- 1675 — Guru Tegh Bahadur executed at Delhi (Chandni Chowk, now Gurdwara Sis Ganj); 1699 — Khalsa founded at Anandpur Sahib (Baisakhi).
- 1704–05 — siege of Anandpur; battle of Chamkaur; younger Sahibzadas (Zorawar Singh, Fateh Singh) bricked alive at Sirhind on Wazir Khan's order; Khidrana (Muktsar) — the 'Chali Mukte'.
- 1706 — final recension of the Granth at Damdama Sahib (Talwandi Sabo); Zafarnama sent to Aurangzeb; 1708 — Guru Gobind Singh dies at Nanded, Guruship vested in the Granth.
- Infrastructure: Nurmahal sarai (Jalandhar district, built under Nur Jahan, c. 1618–20); Sher Shah–Mughal highway through Sirhind, Ludhiana, Phillaur with kos minars; Ali Mardan Khan's canals for Shah Jahan, including the Hasli canal to Lahore.

### Historiography — Mughal decline and the nature of the state

- Jadunath Sarkar: Aurangzeb's religious bigotry alienated Rajputs, Marathas, Sikhs, Jats — the 'religious policy' explanation.
- Satish Chandra: jagirdari crisis — too many claimants for too little revenue; factional struggle among nobles.
- Irfan Habib ('The Agrarian System of Mughal India'): heavy revenue demand drove peasant revolts — agrarian crisis.
- M. Athar Ali: changing composition of nobility (more Deccanis and Marathas) weakened cohesion.
- Muzaffar Alam and C.A. Bayly (revisionists): the 18th century saw regional prosperity and successor states rather than all-round decline.
- Mughal state as a 'gunpowder empire' (M. Hodgson) and as a centralised–bureaucratic state (Aligarh school) vs 'patrimonial–bureaucratic' or 'segmentary' models.
- Sulh-i-kul (universal peace) of Akbar is now read as statecraft for a plural empire, not just personal religiosity.

### PPSC-style asks and traps

- Babur's first invasion of India was 1519 (Bajaur); Panipat I came after four earlier expeditions.
- Sher Shah's revenue reforms preceded Todar Mal's — Todar Mal served under Sher Shah first; the rupiya was Sher Shah's, not Akbar's.
- Buland Darwaza commemorates the Gujarat conquest (not Khandesh); Akbar's tomb is at Sikandra, Jahangir's at Lahore, Aurangzeb's at Khuldabad.
- Itimad-ud-Daula's tomb was built by Nur Jahan, not Shah Jahan; Taj's chief architect is traditionally Ustad Ahmad Lahori.
- Din-i-Ilahi was a discipleship order, not a new religion with a book or priests.
- Mahzar (1579) was drafted by Shaikh Mubarak (father of Abul Fazl and Faizi).
- Dara Shikoh was a Qadiri disciple (of Mulla Shah and influenced by Mian Mir).
