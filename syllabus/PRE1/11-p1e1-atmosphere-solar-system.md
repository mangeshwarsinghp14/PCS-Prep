## Short notes

- Dry air by volume: nitrogen about 78%, oxygen about 21%, argon about 0.93%, carbon dioxide about 0.04% (over 420 ppm in the 2020s); plus traces of neon, helium, ozone, hydrogen.
- Water vapour varies from near 0 to about 4% and decreases with height and towards the poles; dust particles act as hygroscopic nuclei for condensation.
- Troposphere: lowest layer, about 8 km at poles to 18 km at equator; contains almost all weather and water vapour; temperature falls about 6.5 C per km (normal lapse rate).
- Stratosphere: up to about 50 km; contains the ozone layer (roughly 15-35 km) that absorbs UV radiation; temperature rises with height; calm, so preferred by jet aircraft at its base.
- Mesosphere: up to about 80-85 km; coldest layer (around -90 to -100 C at its top); meteors burn here.
- Thermosphere/ionosphere (about 80-400 km): ionised layers reflect radio waves back to Earth; auroras occur here; the exosphere is the outermost, thinnest layer.
- Ozone depletion by CFCs and halons creates the Antarctic ozone hole each southern spring; Vienna Convention (1985), Montreal Protocol (1987) and Kigali Amendment (2016, phasing down HFCs); World Ozone Day 16 September.
- Solar system: the Sun holds about 99.8% of its mass; eight planets; Pluto was reclassified as a dwarf planet by the IAU in 2006.
- Inner rocky planets: Mercury (smallest, closest), Venus (hottest due to thick CO2 greenhouse, rotates east to west), Earth, Mars (red due to iron oxide, has Olympus Mons).
- Outer planets: Jupiter (largest) and Saturn (least dense, prominent rings) are gas giants; Uranus (rotates on its side) and Neptune are ice giants; the asteroid belt lies between Mars and Jupiter.
- Indian missions: Mangalyaan (Mars orbit 2014), Chandrayaan-3 landed near the lunar south pole on 23 August 2023 (National Space Day), Aditya-L1 solar observatory placed at the L1 point in January 2024.
- Insolation is incoming short-wave solar radiation; Earth intercepts only about one two-billionth of the Sun's output; solar constant about 1,361 W/m^2 at the top of the atmosphere.
- Insolation depends on angle of the Sun's rays, length of day, transparency of atmosphere and Earth-Sun distance (perihelion about 3 January, aphelion about 4 July).
- Heat budget (NCERT model, 100 units): about 35 units reflected to space (planetary albedo), 14 absorbed by the atmosphere, 51 by the surface; Earth and atmosphere radiate back 65 units, so there is no net gain or loss.
- The atmosphere is heated mainly from below by long-wave terrestrial radiation, through conduction, convection (vertical), advection (horizontal, e.g., the 'loo') and latent heat.
- Albedo: Earth's average is about 0.3; fresh snow and ice reflect most light, forests and oceans little - melting ice lowers albedo and amplifies warming.
- Temperature generally decreases from equator to poles; the hottest places are subtropical deserts, not the equator (cloudiness); isotherms bend over continents and oceans.
- Temperature inversion (warmer air above cooler air) forms on long, clear, calm winter nights and in valleys (air drainage), producing fog and frost and trapping pollutants.

## Punjab angle

- Winter temperature inversions over the Punjab plains trap smoke and moisture, causing dense fog and smog in December-January, worsened by crop-residue burning in October-November.
- Hot, dry westerly 'loo' winds in May-June raise temperatures in south-west Punjab (Bathinda, Fazilka, Muktsar) to well above 45 C on some days.

## Deep dive

### Atmosphere numbers and facts beyond NCERT

- About half the mass of the atmosphere lies below roughly 5.5 km and about 99% below roughly 30 km — so 'thin' upper layers matter for radio and satellites, not for weather.
- Homosphere (up to about 80–90 km) has uniform gas proportions because of mixing; above it the heterosphere is layered by molecular weight (nitrogen, oxygen, helium, hydrogen).
- Tropopause temperature is about −80 °C over the equator but about −45 °C over the poles: the tropopause is higher at the equator, so air there cools for longer — a favourite statement trap.
- In dry air argon is the third most abundant gas and carbon dioxide the fourth; water vapour is excluded from 'dry air' tables, which is why it is missing from the top-four list.
- Ozone is measured in Dobson Units; a column of about 300 DU is normal and values below 220 DU are treated as 'ozone hole' conditions.
- About 90% of all ozone is in the stratosphere ('good ozone'); ground-level (tropospheric) ozone is a secondary pollutant and a greenhouse gas formed from NOx and VOCs in sunlight.
- Ionosphere layers: D (absorbs medium-wave radio by day and fades at night — why AM stations travel farther after dark), E and F (F1/F2) which reflect short-wave signals.
- The Kármán line at 100 km is the conventional edge of space; low-Earth-orbit satellites (about 160–2,000 km) move through the thermosphere and exosphere.
- Van Allen radiation belts — zones of charged particles trapped by Earth's magnetic field — were discovered in 1958 from Explorer-1 data.
- Clouds by height: noctilucent clouds form in the mesosphere (about 80 km, the highest clouds); nacreous or polar stratospheric clouds form in the winter polar stratosphere and help destroy ozone.

### Ozone regime timeline (year — event)

- 1974 — Molina and Rowland warn that CFCs can destroy stratospheric ozone.
- 1985 — British Antarctic Survey scientists (Farman, Gardiner, Shanklin) report the Antarctic ozone hole; Vienna Convention for the Protection of the Ozone Layer adopted.
- 1987 — Montreal Protocol signed on 16 September (hence World Ozone Day); later amendments: London 1990 (Multilateral Fund), Copenhagen 1992 (HCFCs, methyl bromide).
- 1995 — Nobel Prize in Chemistry to Crutzen, Molina and Rowland for ozone chemistry.
- 2009 — Montreal Protocol becomes the first UN treaty ratified by every member state (197 parties).
- 2016 — Kigali Amendment adds HFCs (ozone-safe but powerful greenhouse gases); India, in the later group, freezes HFC use in 2028 and cuts 85% by 2047; India ratified Kigali in 2021.
- India phased out CFC production and use ahead of the 2010 deadline and phased out HCFC-141b (foam-blowing) by 1 January 2020; India Cooling Action Plan (2019) has a 20-year horizon.
- Ozone-depleting substances: CFCs, halons, carbon tetrachloride, methyl chloroform, HCFCs, methyl bromide; nitrous oxide is now the largest ozone-depleting emission but is not controlled by the Montreal Protocol.
- Mechanism: polar stratospheric clouds in the dark Antarctic winter activate chlorine; when sunlight returns in September–October the hole forms.
- UNEP/WMO assessment (2023): ozone over Antarctica should return to 1980 values around 2066, over the Arctic around 2045 and elsewhere around 2040 if current policies hold.

### Solar system — facts PPSC/UPSC like

- One astronomical unit is about 149.6 million km; sunlight takes about 8 minutes 20 seconds to reach Earth.
- The Sun is a G-type main-sequence star; its visible surface (photosphere) is about 5,500–6,000 °C while the corona exceeds a million degrees — the 'coronal heating problem' that Aditya-L1 studies.
- The Sun shines by fusing hydrogen into helium; sunspots are cooler, darker patches; activity follows an approximately 11-year cycle (Cycle 25 peaked around 2024–25).
- Order of planets by size: Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune, Earth, Venus, Mars, Mercury; Earth is the densest planet, Saturn the least dense.
- Venus and Uranus rotate east to west (retrograde); a day on Venus (about 243 Earth days) is longer than its year (about 225 days).
- Mercury and Venus have no moons; Mars has two (Phobos, Deimos); Saturn now has the largest number of confirmed moons after discoveries in 2023–25.
- Ganymede (Jupiter) is the largest moon; Titan (Saturn) is the only moon with a thick atmosphere; Europa and Enceladus have sub-surface oceans.
- The IAU recognises five dwarf planets: Ceres (in the asteroid belt), Pluto, Haumea, Makemake and Eris; the Kuiper belt lies beyond Neptune and the Oort cloud is the source of long-period comets.
- Earth's axis is tilted about 23.5°; perihelion (about 147 million km) is in early January and aphelion in early July — seasons come from tilt, not distance.
- Lagrange points: five balance points in the Sun–Earth system; L1 gives an uninterrupted view of the Sun (Aditya-L1, SOHO), L2 is used by the James Webb Space Telescope.

### Indian space science missions (year — mission — why it matters)

- 2008 — Chandrayaan-1: Moon Impact Probe; NASA's M3 instrument on board helped detect water/hydroxyl on the lunar surface.
- 2013–14 — Mars Orbiter Mission (Mangalyaan): launched November 2013, entered Mars orbit on 24 September 2014; India first Asian country to reach Mars orbit and first to do so on the first attempt.
- 2015 — AstroSat: India's first multi-wavelength space observatory.
- 2019 — Chandrayaan-2: orbiter continues to work; Vikram lander crashed during descent.
- 2023 — Chandrayaan-3 soft-landed near the lunar south pole on 23 August (site named Shiv Shakti point); the Pragyan rover confirmed sulphur in lunar soil.
- 2023–24 — Aditya-L1 launched 2 September 2023 and placed in a halo orbit around Sun–Earth L1 (about 1.5 million km away) on 6 January 2024; it carries seven payloads including the VELC coronagraph.
- 2024 — XPoSat (launched 1 January 2024): India's first dedicated X-ray polarimetry mission for black holes and neutron stars.
- Planned — Venus Orbiter Mission (approved September 2024) and a Chandrayaan-4 lunar sample-return mission; Gaganyaan is India's human spaceflight programme.

### Heat balance and temperature — deeper concepts

- Latitudinal imbalance: roughly between 40°N and 40°S there is a net surplus of radiation and poleward a deficit; winds and ocean currents carry the surplus towards the poles.
- Earth currently has a small positive energy imbalance because of extra greenhouse gases; about 90% of this extra heat is absorbed by the oceans.
- The 'thermal equator' (belt of highest mean temperature) lies north of the geographical equator because the Northern Hemisphere has more land.
- Diurnal range is largest in deserts (dry, clear air) and smallest over oceans and coasts; this is the essence of continentality versus maritime influence.
- World records (WMO-recognised): highest air temperature 56.7 °C at Death Valley, USA (1913); lowest −89.2 °C at Vostok, Antarctica (1983).
- India: Phalodi (Rajasthan) recorded 51.0 °C in May 2016; a 52.9 °C reading at Delhi's Mungeshpur in May 2024 was later attributed to a sensor error — a good trap.
- Inversion types: radiation (ground) inversion on calm clear nights, valley inversion with frost pockets, advection inversion, frontal inversion and subsidence inversion under high pressure.
- Urban heat island: cities stay warmer than rural surroundings, especially at night, because concrete stores heat and vegetation is scarce.

### Punjab angle and PPSC traps

- Punjab lies roughly between 29.5°N–32.5°N and 73.9°E–76.8°E; the Tropic of Cancer does not pass through it (it crosses Gujarat, Rajasthan, MP, Chhattisgarh, Jharkhand, West Bengal, Tripura and Mizoram).
- Continental location far from the sea gives Punjab a large annual range: May–June maxima above 45 °C in the south-west and near-freezing winter nights with ground frost that damages potato and vegetables.
- Winter radiation inversions plus calm air trap smoke from residue burning and vehicles — explains dense fog and smog days at Amritsar, Ludhiana and Bathinda.
- PPSC 2025 asked a 'match the layers of the atmosphere' question — learn one defining feature of each layer, not just heights.
- Trap: the hottest planet is Venus (greenhouse effect), not Mercury; meteors burn in the mesosphere, auroras glow in the thermosphere.
- Trap: ozone in the stratosphere protects life; ozone at ground level harms crops (it lowers wheat and rice yields in the Indo-Gangetic plain) and human lungs.
- Trap: CFC replacements (HFCs) are ozone-safe but climate-damaging — that is why Kigali sits under the Montreal Protocol rather than the UNFCCC.

## Practice MCQs (12)

**1.** Which layer of the atmosphere reflects radio waves back to Earth?

(a) Mesosphere (meteor layer)  
(b) Ionosphere (thermosphere)  
(c) Troposphere (weather layer)  
(d) Stratosphere (ozone layer)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Ionosphere (thermosphere)** — Ionised gas layers in the thermosphere reflect radio waves, enabling long-distance radio.

</details>

**2.** The second most abundant gas in dry air by volume is:

(a) Argon  
(b) Nitrogen  
(c) Oxygen  
(d) Carbon dioxide

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Oxygen** — Nitrogen about 78%, oxygen about 21%, argon about 0.93%.

</details>

**3.** Consider: 1. Temperature decreases with height in the troposphere. 2. Temperature increases with height in the stratosphere because ozone absorbs UV radiation. Which is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Both 1 and 2** — Troposphere cools at about 6.5 C/km; stratospheric warming is due to ozone absorbing ultraviolet rays.

</details>

**4.** Which is the hottest planet in the solar system?

(a) Mars  
(b) Jupiter  
(c) Venus  
(d) Mercury

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Venus** — Venus's dense CO2 atmosphere creates a runaway greenhouse effect, making it hotter than Mercury.

</details>

**5.** Temperature inversion is most likely on:

(a) Days with strong convection  
(b) Long, clear, calm winter nights  
(c) Cloudy summer afternoons  
(d) Windy days with heavy rain

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Long, clear, calm winter nights** — Rapid ground cooling under clear, calm skies chills the lowest air below the air above.

</details>

**6.** The Montreal Protocol (1987) deals with:

(a) Conservation of wetlands  
(b) Trade in endangered species  
(c) Reducing greenhouse gas emissions of developed countries  
(d) Phasing out ozone-depleting substances

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) Phasing out ozone-depleting substances** — It controls CFCs, halons and similar chemicals; the Kigali Amendment added HFCs.

</details>

**7.** Consider the following statements:  
1. The tropopause is at roughly the same height over the equator and over the poles.  
2. The temperature at the tropopause is higher over the equator than over the poles.  
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) Neither 1 nor 2** — Statement 1 is wrong: the tropopause is about 18 km high over the equator but only about 8 km over the poles. Statement 2 is wrong: being higher, the equatorial tropopause is colder (about −80 °C) than the polar one (about −45 °C).

</details>

**8.** Consider the following statements:  
1. Nitrous oxide emissions are regulated under the Montreal Protocol.  
2. HFCs do not deplete the ozone layer but are controlled under the Kigali Amendment.  
3. India has to freeze HFC consumption in 2028.  
Which of the statements given above are correct?

(a) 1 and 2 only  
(b) 2 and 3 only  
(c) 1 and 3 only  
(d) 1, 2 and 3

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) 2 and 3 only** — Kigali covers HFCs; India's freeze year is 2028; nitrous oxide is not regulated by the Montreal Protocol.

</details>

**9.** Aditya-L1 observes the Sun from a halo orbit around:

(a) Sun–Earth L1 point, about 1.5 million km away  
(b) Sun–Earth L2 point, behind Earth, away from the Sun  
(c) A geostationary orbit about 36,000 km up  
(d) A low Earth orbit about 600 km above Earth

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) Sun–Earth L1 point, about 1.5 million km away** — L1 lies between Earth and Sun and gives an uninterrupted view of the Sun.

</details>

**10.** Which two planets rotate in a retrograde (east-to-west) direction?

(a) Venus and Uranus  
(b) Mars and Jupiter  
(c) Mercury and Neptune  
(d) Saturn and Uranus

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) Venus and Uranus** — Venus and Uranus spin opposite to most planets.

</details>

**11.** Noctilucent clouds, the highest clouds of the atmosphere, form in the:

(a) Troposphere  
(b) Exosphere  
(c) Stratosphere  
(d) Mesosphere

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) Mesosphere** — They form near the cold mesopause at about 80 km.

</details>

**12.** The Antarctic ozone hole is most pronounced during:

(a) March–April (southern autumn)  
(b) June–July (southern winter)  
(c) December–January (southern summer)  
(d) September–October (southern spring)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) September–October (southern spring)** — Returning sunlight in spring activates chlorine on polar stratospheric clouds.

</details>
