## Short notes

- Renaissance means 'rebirth' of classical Greek and Roman learning; it spread across Europe from about the 14th to the 16th century, beginning in Italy (especially Florence).
- Causes: wealthy Italian city-states and patrons such as the Medici, contact with the East through the Crusades and trade, scholars fleeing after the fall of Constantinople (1453), and the printing press.
- Johannes Gutenberg's movable-type printing press (Mainz, mid-15th century) spread books and ideas cheaply and quickly.
- Humanism put human beings and worldly life at the centre: Petrarch (father of humanism), Erasmus (In Praise of Folly), Thomas More (Utopia), Machiavelli (The Prince, written 1513).
- Literature in vernacular languages: Dante (Divine Comedy), Boccaccio (Decameron), later Shakespeare and Cervantes (Don Quixote).
- Art: realism, perspective and anatomy — Leonardo da Vinci (Mona Lisa, The Last Supper), Michelangelo (Sistine Chapel ceiling, David), Raphael, Botticelli.
- Science: Copernicus proposed the sun-centred model (1543); Kepler gave laws of planetary motion; Galileo used the telescope; Vesalius founded modern anatomy (1543).
- Reformation: Martin Luther's 95 Theses (1517) against the sale of indulgences started Protestantism; the Catholic Counter-Reformation included the Jesuits (Ignatius Loyola, 1540).
- Age of discovery: Columbus (1492), Vasco da Gama to India (1498), Magellan's expedition circled the globe (1519–22).
- Industrial Revolution: shift from hand to machine production, beginning in Britain around 1760; the term was popularised by Arnold Toynbee.
- Why Britain first: coal and iron deposits, capital from trade and colonies, colonial markets and raw materials, agricultural revolution and enclosures freeing labour, banks, navy and political stability.
- Textile inventions: flying shuttle (John Kay, 1733), spinning jenny (Hargreaves, c. 1764), water frame (Arkwright, 1769), spinning mule (Crompton, 1779), power loom (Cartwright, 1785), cotton gin (Eli Whitney, USA, 1793).
- Steam power: Newcomen's engine (1712) for mines; James Watt's improved engine with a separate condenser (patent 1769) powered factories.
- Transport: Stockton–Darlington railway (1825), George Stephenson's Rocket (1829), Liverpool–Manchester line (1830); canals and steamships followed.
- Iron and steel: Abraham Darby's coke smelting (1709), Henry Cort's puddling process, Bessemer's steel process (1856).
- Social effects: urbanisation, factory system, child and women labour, slums; leading to Factory Acts, trade unions, Chartism and socialist ideas (Marx and Engels, Communist Manifesto, 1848).
- Impact on India: British machine-made cloth ruined Indian handicrafts (de-industrialisation); India became a supplier of raw cotton and a market for British goods.

## Punjab angle

- British industrial demand for raw materials and food shaped colonial Punjab's canal colonies (from the 1890s), which produced wheat and cotton for export.

## Deep dive

### Renaissance timeline (year — event)

- 1304–74 — Petrarch, 'father of humanism', collects classical manuscripts; c. 1320 — Dante completes the Divine Comedy in the Tuscan vernacular.
- 1348–53 — Black Death kills perhaps a third of Europe; Boccaccio's Decameron is set during the plague.
- 1397 — Medici Bank founded in Florence; 1434 — Cosimo de' Medici dominates Florence; 1469–92 — Lorenzo 'the Magnificent' patronises artists.
- 1420–36 — Brunelleschi builds the dome of Florence cathedral and works out linear perspective; Alberti writes on painting (1435).
- c. 1450s — Gutenberg's movable-type press at Mainz; Gutenberg Bible c. 1455; by 1500 presses in over 200 European towns.
- 1453 — Ottomans take Constantinople; Greek scholars carry texts to Italy; 1486 — Pico della Mirandola's 'Oration on the Dignity of Man'.
- 1495–98 — Leonardo's Last Supper; 1503–06 — Mona Lisa; 1504 — Michelangelo's David; 1508–12 — Sistine Chapel ceiling; 1509–11 — Raphael's School of Athens.
- 1511 — Erasmus, 'In Praise of Folly'; 1513 — Machiavelli writes 'The Prince'; 1516 — Thomas More, 'Utopia'; 1527 — sack of Rome ends the High Renaissance in Italy.
- 1543 — Copernicus, 'On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres', and Vesalius, 'On the Fabric of the Human Body'; 1609–10 — Galileo's telescope; 1687 — Newton's Principia (Scientific Revolution).

### Personalities file (name — field — work/idea)

- Petrarch — poet/scholar — sonnets to Laura; humanist study of Cicero.
- Leonardo da Vinci — painter, engineer — notebooks with flying machines and anatomy; Last Supper (Milan), Mona Lisa.
- Michelangelo — sculptor/painter/architect — Pieta, David, Sistine ceiling and Last Judgement, dome of St Peter's.
- Raphael — painter — School of Athens (Plato and Aristotle at the centre), Madonnas; Botticelli — Birth of Venus, Primavera.
- Giorgio Vasari — 'Lives of the Artists' (1550) — coined the idea of a 'rinascita' (rebirth) of art.
- Erasmus (Netherlands) — Christian humanism, Greek New Testament (1516); Albrecht Dürer (Germany) — engravings; Jan van Eyck — oil painting technique.
- Shakespeare (England), Cervantes (Spain, Don Quixote 1605), Montaigne (France, Essays) — Renaissance literature outside Italy.
- Copernicus (Poland), Kepler (Germany, laws of planetary motion 1609–19), Galileo (Italy, tried by the Inquisition 1633), Francis Bacon (England, inductive method, Novum Organum 1620).

### Reformation and Counter-Reformation (year — event)

- 1517 — Luther's Ninety-Five Theses at Wittenberg against sale of indulgences (Tetzel); 1521 — Diet of Worms declares Luther an outlaw; he translates the Bible into German.
- 1520s — Zwingli in Zurich; Anabaptists; 1524–25 — German Peasants' War, which Luther opposed.
- 1534 — Act of Supremacy: Henry VIII becomes head of the Church of England; 1536 — Calvin's 'Institutes', Geneva model, predestination.
- 1540 — Society of Jesus (Ignatius Loyola) approved; Jesuits (Francis Xavier in Goa, 1542) spread Catholic education and missions to Asia.
- 1545–63 — Council of Trent reforms Catholic discipline, rejects Protestant doctrines; 1559 — Index of Prohibited Books.
- 1555 — Peace of Augsburg: 'cuius regio, eius religio' (the ruler's religion is the subject's religion) for Lutheran and Catholic princes.
- 1618–48 — Thirty Years' War; 1648 — Peace of Westphalia: sovereignty of states and the modern state system.
- Significance: vernacular Bibles and printing spread literacy; Max Weber linked the 'Protestant ethic' to the spirit of capitalism (1905).

### Industrial Revolution — invention timeline (year — inventor — device)

- 1701 — Jethro Tull, seed drill; 1709 — Abraham Darby, smelting iron with coke (Coalbrookdale).
- 1712 — Thomas Newcomen, atmospheric steam engine for pumping mines; 1769 — James Watt patents the separate condenser (rotary engine with Matthew Boulton, 1780s).
- 1733 — John Kay, flying shuttle; 1764 — James Hargreaves, spinning jenny; 1769 — Richard Arkwright, water frame (Cromford mill, 1771); 1779 — Samuel Crompton, spinning mule.
- 1784 — Henry Cort, puddling and rolling of iron; 1785 — Edmund Cartwright, power loom; 1793 — Eli Whitney (USA), cotton gin.
- 1804 — Richard Trevithick's steam locomotive; 1825 — Stockton–Darlington railway; 1829–30 — George Stephenson's 'Rocket' and the Liverpool–Manchester line.
- 1837 — Cooke and Wheatstone telegraph; 1844 — Morse telegraph line (USA); 1856 — Bessemer steel process.
- 1851 — Great Exhibition at the Crystal Palace, London — Britain as the 'workshop of the world'.
- Second Industrial Revolution (c. 1870–1914): steel, electricity (Edison's bulb 1879), chemicals, internal-combustion engine (Benz 1885), Ford's moving assembly line (1913).

### Why Britain first, and how industry spread

- Agricultural Revolution: enclosures, four-course (Norfolk) rotation popularised by 'Turnip' Townshend, Robert Bakewell's selective breeding — more food, labour freed for towns.
- Coal and iron close together; rivers and canals (Bridgewater canal, 1761) for cheap transport.
- Capital: Atlantic trade, slave trade profits, colonial revenue (Indian historians cite the 'Plassey plunder' debate), Bank of England (1694), joint-stock companies.
- Institutions: political stability after 1688, patent laws, a strong navy protecting markets, scientific societies (Royal Society, 1660).
- Markets: colonies supplied raw cotton and bought finished goods; British tariffs protected against Indian textiles (calico Acts of 1700 and 1721).
- Spread: Belgium first on the Continent, then France; Germany after the Zollverein (1834) and unification (1871); USA after the Civil War; Japan after the Meiji Restoration (1868); Russia in the 1890s (Witte, Trans-Siberian Railway).
- The term 'Industrial Revolution' was popularised by Arnold Toynbee's lectures (published 1884); later historians stress gradual growth rather than a sudden break.

### Social effects, new ideas and the India link

- Urbanisation: Manchester grew from a town of about 25,000 (1770s) to over 300,000 (1850s); slums, cholera, child labour.
- Labour reaction: Luddites (1811–16) broke machines; Combination Acts (1799–1800) banned unions, repealed 1824; Chartists (1838–48) demanded universal male suffrage.
- Factory Acts: 1802 (apprentices), 1833 (factory inspectors, limits on child labour), 1847 (Ten Hours Act).
- Ideas: Adam Smith ('Wealth of Nations', 1776, laissez-faire), Malthus (population, 1798), Ricardo (rent, comparative advantage), Bentham (utilitarianism), Robert Owen (New Lanark, cooperatives), Marx and Engels ('Communist Manifesto', 1848), Engels ('Condition of the Working Class in England', 1845).
- India: British machine-made cloth ruined Indian handloom exports — 'deindustrialisation'; India became an exporter of raw cotton, indigo, jute and opium.
- Railways in India (1853) served export of raw material and import of manufactures; Dadabhai Naoroji and R.C. Dutt later built the 'drain' critique on this.
- Punjab link: canal colonies from the 1880s–90s grew wheat and cotton for imperial markets; Lancashire's demand shaped crop choices.

### PPSC-style asks and traps

- Power loom = Cartwright (1785); spinning mule = Crompton (1779); water frame = Arkwright; flying shuttle = Kay; spinning jenny = Hargreaves.
- Watt improved (not invented) the steam engine; Newcomen's engine came earlier (1712).
- The Renaissance began in Italy (Florence), the Industrial Revolution in Britain; the Reformation in Germany.
- Copernicus's book (1543) appeared the same year as Vesalius's anatomy — both are 'Scientific Revolution' markers.
- Gutenberg's press was in Mainz (Germany), not Italy; Caxton brought printing to England (1476).
- 'Utopia' — Thomas More; 'The Prince' — Machiavelli; 'Praise of Folly' — Erasmus; 'Leviathan' (1651) — Hobbes (not Renaissance Italy).

**17.** The Bessemer process, patented in 1856, made possible:

(a) Smelting of iron ore using coke  
(b) Use of steam power to drive cotton mills  
(c) Cheap mass production of steel from pig iron  
(d) Mechanised spinning of fine cotton yarn

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Cheap mass production of steel from pig iron** — Henry Bessemer blew air through molten pig iron to burn off carbon, making steel cheap and plentiful for rails and machines. Coke smelting was Abraham Darby's earlier breakthrough (1709).

</details>

**18.** The Luddites in early 19th-century England were:

(a) Farmers who resisted land enclosures  
(b) Textile workers who smashed new machines  
(c) Factory owners who opposed labour laws  
(d) Reformers who demanded votes for workers

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Textile workers who smashed new machines** — Between 1811 and 1816, Luddite hand-weavers and framework knitters in the Midlands and North destroyed machines they blamed for low wages and lost jobs. The demand for workers' votes belongs to the later Chartist movement.

</details>
