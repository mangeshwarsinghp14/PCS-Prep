## Short notes

- Greenhouse effect is natural: gases trap outgoing long-wave radiation; without it Earth's average temperature would be about -18 C instead of about 15 C.
- Main greenhouse gases: water vapour, CO2, methane (CH4), nitrous oxide (N2O), fluorinated gases (HFCs, PFCs, SF6, NF3) and ozone; the Kyoto basket had six (CO2, CH4, N2O, HFCs, PFCs, SF6), with NF3 added later.
- Per tonne, methane traps far more heat than CO2 (roughly 28 times over 100 years) and N2O about 265 times; SF6 has the highest global warming potential.
- CO2 has risen from about 280 ppm before industrialisation to over 420 ppm today; sources: fossil fuels (coal is most carbon-intensive, natural gas least), cement, deforestation.
- Methane comes from paddy fields, livestock (enteric fermentation), landfills and fossil-fuel leaks; N2O mainly from nitrogen fertilisers.
- IPCC AR6 (2021-23): human activity has warmed the planet by about 1.1 C (2011-2020 vs 1850-1900); WMO reported 2024 as the warmest year on record, about 1.55 C above pre-industrial.
- Impacts: glacier retreat, sea-level rise, more heatwaves and extreme rain, ocean acidification, coral bleaching, crop stress and new disease patterns.
- UNFCCC adopted at the 1992 Rio Earth Summit (in force 1994) with the principle of 'common but differentiated responsibilities'; COPs are held annually.
- Kyoto Protocol (1997, in force 2005) set binding targets only for developed (Annex I) countries and created three flexible mechanisms: Clean Development Mechanism, Joint Implementation and International Emissions Trading.
- Clean Development Mechanism: developed countries invest in emission-cutting projects in developing countries and earn Certified Emission Reductions (1 CER = 1 tonne CO2-equivalent); India was a major host of CDM projects.
- Carbon credits are tradable certificates for one tonne of CO2e reduced or removed; compliance markets are mandated by law, voluntary markets serve companies' own pledges.
- Paris Agreement (COP21, 2015): warming well below 2 C, pursuing 1.5 C; NDCs every five years; Article 6 carbon markets (6.4 mechanism succeeds CDM); COP29 Baku (2024) set a USD 300 billion/year finance goal by 2035.
- India's updated NDC (2022): 45% lower emissions intensity of GDP than 2005 by 2030; 50% non-fossil power capacity by 2030 (reached in 2025); 2.5-3 billion tonnes CO2e extra carbon sink; net zero by 2070.
- Indian carbon market: Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act 2022 enabled the Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (2023), administered with the Bureau of Energy Efficiency; the earlier PAT scheme traded energy-saving certificates; the Green Credit Programme (2023) rewards voluntary green actions.
- National Action Plan on Climate Change (2008) has eight missions - Solar, Enhanced Energy Efficiency, Sustainable Habitat, Water, Himalayan Ecosystem, Green India, Sustainable Agriculture, Strategic Knowledge.
- Renewables: solar (International Solar Alliance launched by India and France in 2015, HQ Gurugram), wind (Gujarat, Tamil Nadu lead), hydro, biomass, geothermal (Puga valley, Ladakh) and tidal (Gulf of Khambhat/Kutch potential).
- Key schemes: PM-KUSUM (2019) for solar pumps and farm solar plants; PM Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana (2024) for rooftop solar in 1 crore homes; National Green Hydrogen Mission (2023) targets 5 million tonnes a year by 2030.
- Loss and Damage Fund was operationalised at COP28 (Dubai, 2023), which also called for transitioning away from fossil fuels in energy systems.

## Punjab angle

- Paddy straw is increasingly used in biomass power and compressed biogas (CBG) plants; Verbio's CBG plant at Lehragaga (Sangrur) is among the largest in Asia.
- PEDA (Punjab Energy Development Agency) is the nodal agency for renewable energy, including canal-top and rooftop solar and PM-KUSUM solar pumps.
- Rice paddies, livestock and heavy nitrogen fertiliser use make agriculture a large source of methane and nitrous oxide in Punjab; direct-seeded rice reduces methane and water use.

## Deep dive

### Climate negotiations timeline (year — meeting — outcome)

- 1992 — Rio Earth Summit adopts the UNFCCC (in force 21 March 1994) with Annex I/Annex II lists and the 'common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities' principle.
- 1997 — COP3 Kyoto: Kyoto Protocol (in force 16 February 2005); first commitment period 2008–12; Doha Amendment (2012) set a second period 2013–20.
- 2002 — COP8 New Delhi: Delhi Ministerial Declaration put development and adaptation on the agenda.
- 2009–10 — COP15 Copenhagen pledges USD 100 billion a year by 2020; COP16 Cancun creates the Green Climate Fund (headquartered at Songdo, South Korea).
- 2013 — COP19 Warsaw: Warsaw International Mechanism for Loss and Damage and the Warsaw Framework for REDD+.
- 2015 — COP21 Paris Agreement (in force 4 November 2016); India ratified on 2 October 2016.
- 2021 — COP26 Glasgow: Glasgow Climate Pact ('phase down' of unabated coal power — wording changed from 'phase out'); India announces Panchamrit and net zero by 2070.
- 2022–23 — COP27 Sharm el-Sheikh agrees a Loss and Damage fund; COP28 Dubai operationalises it, completes the first Global Stocktake and calls for tripling renewables, doubling efficiency and 'transitioning away from fossil fuels' in energy systems.
- 2024 — COP29 Baku: New Collective Quantified Goal of at least USD 300 billion a year by 2035 (with a call to scale up to USD 1.3 trillion); rules for the Article 6.4 crediting mechanism adopted.
- 2025 — COP30 Belém (November): 'Global Mutirão' package — call to triple adaptation finance by 2035, 59 indicators for the Global Goal on Adaptation, a just-transition mechanism; no fossil-fuel roadmap in the negotiated text; Brazil launched the Tropical Forest Forever Facility.
- 2026 — COP31 Antalya, Türkiye, 9–20 November 2026: Türkiye's environment minister Murat Kurum presides, Australia's Chris Bowen chairs the negotiations under a hosting compromise; pre-COP held in Fiji in October 2026.
- Next: COP32 (2027) is slated for Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.

### India's climate commitments — then and now

- 2008 — National Action Plan on Climate Change with eight missions; every state/UT later prepared a State Action Plan on Climate Change.
- 2015 — INDC: cut emissions intensity of GDP by 33–35% from 2005 by 2030, 40% non-fossil power capacity, 2.5–3 billion tonnes CO2e extra carbon sink.
- 2021 — Panchamrit at Glasgow: 500 GW non-fossil capacity by 2030, 50% of energy needs from renewables, 1 billion tonnes less projected emissions, 45% intensity cut, net zero by 2070.
- August 2022 — updated NDC formalised 45% intensity cut and 50% non-fossil capacity by 2030 plus the LiFE mass movement; November 2022 — Long-Term Low-Emission Development Strategy submitted.
- Progress: India's fourth Biennial Update Report (December 2024) reported a 36% fall in emissions intensity of GDP between 2005 and 2020.
- Non-fossil sources crossed 50% of installed capacity in mid-2025 (five years early) and about 54% (over 300 GW) by August 2026.
- ISFR 2023 put the additional forest carbon sink at 2.29 billion tonnes CO2e over 2005, short of the 2.5–3 billion tonne goal.
- NDC for 2031–35 (approved by the Union Cabinet in March 2026): 47% cut in emissions intensity of GDP from 2005 by 2035, 60% non-fossil installed capacity by 2035, an additional 3.5–4 billion tonnes CO2e sink, plus qualitative goals such as climate-resilient infrastructure.

### Carbon markets and credits — in detail

- Kyoto's three flexible mechanisms: Clean Development Mechanism (Article 12, credits called CERs), Joint Implementation (Article 6, ERUs) and International Emissions Trading (Article 17, AAUs).
- India hosted the second-largest number of CDM projects after China, mostly renewable energy and energy efficiency.
- Paris Article 6: 6.2 lets countries trade 'internationally transferred mitigation outcomes' bilaterally; 6.4 creates a UN-supervised crediting mechanism (Paris Agreement Crediting Mechanism) that succeeds the CDM; 6.8 covers non-market approaches.
- India's Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (notified June 2023 under the Energy Conservation (Amendment) Act 2022): Bureau of Energy Efficiency administers it, Grid Controller of India runs the registry; from 2025 emission-intensity targets were notified for energy-intensive sectors such as aluminium, cement, chlor-alkali and pulp and paper.
- Older Indian instruments: Perform, Achieve and Trade (PAT, from 2012) with tradable energy-saving certificates, and Renewable Energy Certificates (2010).
- Green Credit Programme (October 2023, under the Environment (Protection) Act) rewards voluntary actions; tree plantation on degraded land was the first activity.
- EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism moved from reporting (October 2023) to the definitive phase on 1 January 2026 — a concern for Indian steel and aluminium exporters.
- Voluntary market standards include Verra (VCS) and Gold Standard; integrity concerns (over-crediting of forest projects) are a live debate.

### Energy transition — data and schemes

- India is the third-largest emitter in absolute terms but its per-capita emissions are less than half the world average — the core of its equity argument.
- India ranked third in the world in installed renewable capacity in 2026; total installed power capacity was about 552 GW in August 2026.
- Mega projects: Bhadla Solar Park (Rajasthan), Pavagada (Karnataka) and the Khavda Renewable Energy Park in Kutch, Gujarat, planned at about 30 GW — set to be the world's largest.
- Offshore wind: target of 30 GW by 2030; viability-gap funding approved in 2024 for the first 1 GW off Gujarat and Tamil Nadu.
- Nuclear Energy Mission (Budget 2025-26): about ₹20,000 crore for small modular reactors; target of 100 GW nuclear capacity by 2047.
- National Green Hydrogen Mission (January 2023, ₹19,744 crore): 5 million tonnes a year by 2030; SIGHT programme subsidises electrolysers and green hydrogen.
- Ethanol: India reached about 20% blending in petrol in 2025, ahead of the original 2030 target; SATAT (2018) promotes compressed biogas.
- Rooftop and farm solar: PM Surya Ghar Muft Bijli Yojana (February 2024, about ₹75,021 crore, 1 crore homes, up to 300 free units a month) and PM-KUSUM (2019).

### Climate science — numbers to quote (with year)

- IPCC was set up in 1988 by WMO and UNEP; Special Report on 1.5 °C (2018); Sixth Assessment cycle completed with the Synthesis Report in 2023; the Seventh cycle is under way.
- WMO: atmospheric CO2 averaged 423.9 ppm in 2024 — about 152% of the pre-industrial level, with the largest one-year rise (3.5 ppm) since measurements began in 1957.
- WMO: 2024 was the warmest year on record (about 1.55 °C above 1850–1900); 2025 was about 1.43 °C above pre-industrial, the second or third warmest; 2015–2025 were the eleven warmest years.
- IMD: 2024 was India's warmest year since 1901 (+0.65 °C over the 1991–2020 average); 2025 ranked eighth warmest (+0.28 °C).
- IMD: extreme weather killed about 2,760 people in India in 2025 — over 1,370 from heavy rain, floods, cloudbursts and landslides and over 1,310 from lightning and thunderstorms.
- MoES 'Assessment of Climate Change over the Indian Region' (2020): India's average temperature rose about 0.7 °C over 1901–2018.
- Short-lived climate pollutants — black carbon, methane, tropospheric ozone and HFCs — offer quick cooling benefits if cut.
- Feedbacks to explain: ice-albedo, water vapour, permafrost methane release and weakening ocean and forest sinks.

### Punjab — emissions, energy and vulnerability

- Punjab State Action Plan on Climate Change was first prepared in 2014 by the Punjab State Council for Science and Technology.
- Residue burning releases CO2, methane, nitrous oxide and black carbon; diverting paddy straw to biomass power and CBG plants turns an emission source into fuel.
- Flooded paddy (methane) and heavy urea use (nitrous oxide) make Punjab's farm emissions high per hectare; direct-seeded rice and alternate wetting and drying cut both water and methane.
- Climate stress already visible: terminal heat in March cuts wheat yields (as in 2022), and extreme monsoon rain upstream produced the 2023 and 2025 floods.
- PSPCL's thermal plants run on coal brought from Jharkhand (including its captive Pachhwara Central mine), so Punjab's power sector is coal-dependent despite solar potential in the south-west.

### PPSC-style asks and common traps

- CDM belongs to the Kyoto Protocol; the Article 6.4 mechanism belongs to the Paris Agreement — do not mix them.
- Glasgow said 'phase down' (not 'phase out') unabated coal; Dubai said 'transition away from fossil fuels' in energy systems.
- Green Climate Fund HQ: Songdo, South Korea; International Solar Alliance HQ: Gurugram; the Loss and Damage fund's board is hosted by the Philippines with the World Bank as interim trustee.
- Do not confuse India's 2022 NDC (45% by 2030) with the 2031–35 NDC (47% by 2035).
- PPSC 2025 asked which gases are greenhouse gases and what a carbon credit means — revise both the Kyoto basket and credit definitions.
- Carbon credit = one tonne CO2e reduced or removed; carbon offset = using that credit to compensate for one's own emissions.
