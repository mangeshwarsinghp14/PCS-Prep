## Short notes

- Standard sea-level pressure is 1013.25 mb; pressure falls with height (roughly 1 mb per 10 m near the surface); isobars join places of equal pressure.
- Pressure belts: equatorial low (doldrums, ITCZ), subtropical highs near 30 degrees (horse latitudes), subpolar lows near 60 degrees, polar highs.
- Coriolis force deflects winds to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and left in the Southern (Ferrel's law); it is zero at the equator and maximum at the poles.
- General circulation has three cells per hemisphere - Hadley, Ferrel and Polar - producing trade winds (easterlies), westerlies and polar easterlies.
- Southern Hemisphere westerlies are very strong over open ocean: 'Roaring Forties', 'Furious Fifties', 'Shrieking Sixties'.
- Jet streams: the subtropical westerly jet steers western disturbances to north India in winter; its shift north and a tropical easterly jet are linked to monsoon onset.
- Local winds: loo (hot, dry, north India), Chinook (Rockies, 'snow eater') and Foehn (Alps) are warm and dry; Mistral (France) and Bora (Adriatic) are cold; Sirocco blows from the Sahara; Harmattan (West Africa, 'the doctor').
- Land and sea breezes and mountain-valley breezes arise from daily differential heating; cold air drains downslope at night (katabatic wind).
- Air masses meet along fronts - cold, warm, stationary and occluded; temperate (extra-tropical) cyclones form along fronts and give western disturbances.
- Tropical cyclones need warm sea surface (above about 26-27 C), Coriolis force (so not within about 5 degrees of the equator), low vertical wind shear and a pre-existing disturbance; they weaken over land.
- Cyclones rotate anticlockwise in the Northern Hemisphere; called hurricanes (Atlantic/East Pacific), typhoons (West Pacific), cyclones (Indian Ocean); the eye is calm; cyclone names come from a WMO/ESCAP panel list.
- El Nino (warm central-east Pacific) usually weakens the Indian monsoon; La Nina tends to strengthen it; a positive Indian Ocean Dipole favours good monsoon rain.
- Water cycle: evaporation and transpiration, condensation into clouds, precipitation, infiltration to groundwater, runoff to rivers and seas - driven by solar energy.
- About 97% of Earth's water is in oceans; only about 2.5-3% is fresh, most of it locked in ice caps and glaciers, then groundwater; rivers and lakes hold a tiny share.
- Relative humidity is the percentage of moisture present compared with full capacity at that temperature; at dew point air is saturated and condensation forms dew, frost, fog, mist or clouds.
- Cloud types: cirrus (high, wispy), cumulus (puffy, vertical), stratus (layered), nimbus (rain-bearing); cumulonimbus brings thunderstorms.
- Rainfall types: convectional (equatorial afternoons), orographic (windward side wet, leeward rain shadow - e.g., Mumbai vs Pune) and cyclonic/frontal.
- The south-west monsoon normally reaches Kerala around 1 June and covers all of India by about 8 July (IMD revised normal dates in 2020).

## Punjab angle

- Western disturbances bring winter rain and hailstorms to Punjab in December-February; timely winter rain is valuable for the rabi wheat crop.
- Most of Punjab's rain comes from the south-west monsoon (July-September); rainfall rises from the dry south-west (Fazilka, Bathinda) to the wetter Shivalik foothills (Pathankot, Gurdaspur, Hoshiarpur).
- Heavy August-September 2025 monsoon rain in Punjab and upstream Himachal caused severe floods in Punjab; the Economic Survey 2025-26 records agriculture contracting by 2.5% largely due to the floods.

## Deep dive

### Indian monsoon — mechanism and drivers

- Thermal concept (Halley, 1686): summer heating of land creates low pressure that draws moist sea air — useful but incomplete.
- ITCZ shift (Flohn): in July the ITCZ moves to about 20–25°N over the Gangetic plain as the 'monsoon trough', pulling in the south-east trades as south-west winds after they cross the equator.
- Jet streams: the subtropical westerly jet lies south of the Himalaya in winter; its jump north of the Tibetan Plateau in early June and the appearance of the tropical easterly jet over the peninsula mark the burst of the monsoon.
- The Tibetan Plateau acts as an elevated heat source in summer, creating an upper-level anticyclone that strengthens the easterly jet.
- The Somali (Findlater) jet is the low-level cross-equatorial current off East Africa that carries most moisture into the Arabian Sea branch; the Mascarene High near Madagascar is its source.
- Intra-seasonal swings: the Madden–Julian Oscillation (30–60 day eastward pulse) and the north–south movement of the monsoon trough cause 'active' and 'break' spells.
- Break in monsoon: the trough shifts to the Himalayan foothills — heavy rain in the hills and north-east, dry spells over the plains.
- Ocean links: El Niño usually weakens and La Niña strengthens the monsoon; a positive Indian Ocean Dipole can offset an El Niño (as in 1997 and 2019).
- IMD categories: normal = 96–104% of the Long Period Average; LPA for the country (1971–2020 base) is about 87 cm; deficient below 90%, excess above 110%.
- IMD statement: the 2025 south-west monsoon delivered 108% of LPA and annual rainfall was 110% of LPA.
- Forecasting: Monsoon Mission (2012) introduced dynamical models; Mission Mausam (approved September 2024, about ₹2,000 crore) aims at a 'weather-ready, climate-smart' India with more radars and better models.

### Western disturbances, seasonal winds and local weather of north India

- Western disturbances are extra-tropical low-pressure systems from the Mediterranean–Caspian region, steered east by the subtropical westerly jet; they give winter rain ('mahawat') in Punjab and snow in the Himalaya.
- A western disturbance meeting the monsoon current can produce extreme rain in the Himalaya — cited for Kedarnath (June 2013) and Himachal (July 2023).
- Pre-monsoon local storms: 'andhi' dust storms in Punjab–Haryana–Rajasthan, 'kalbaisakhi' (nor'westers) in Bengal and Odisha, 'bardoli chheerha' in Assam, 'mango showers' in Kerala–coastal Karnataka, 'cherry blossom' (coffee) showers in Karnataka.
- IMD heat-wave rule for plains: maximum at least 40 °C and 4.5–6.4 °C above normal (severe if 6.5 °C or more above), or an actual maximum of 45 °C or more.
- Cold-wave and dense-fog spells in December–January follow clear skies after a western disturbance passes; fog grounds flights at Amritsar and Chandigarh and slows trains on the Ambala–Amritsar line.
- Hailstorms in February–March from western disturbances can flatten ripening wheat in Punjab — a major farm-insurance trigger.
- IMD's Meteorological Centre at Chandigarh issues district forecasts for Punjab; PAU Ludhiana issues agromet advisories twice a week.

### Tropical cyclones — Indian data bank

- The Bay of Bengal gets roughly four times as many cyclones as the Arabian Sea, but Arabian Sea activity has risen in recent decades (2019 saw five Arabian Sea cyclones).
- Peak seasons are pre-monsoon (April–May) and post-monsoon (October–December); the monsoon's strong vertical wind shear suppresses cyclones in July–August.
- IMD scale by maximum sustained wind: depression 31–49 km/h, deep depression 50–61, cyclonic storm 62–88, severe 89–117, very severe 118–166, extremely severe 167–221, super cyclonic storm 222 km/h or more.
- RSMC New Delhi (IMD) is the WMO Regional Specialised Meteorological Centre for the North Indian Ocean; names come from a 2020 list of 169 names (13 each from 13 member countries) used in sequence.
- 2025 season (IMD): two severe cyclonic storms (Shakti, Montha) and two cyclonic storms (Senyar, Ditwah) formed over the North Indian Ocean.
- Storm surge is worst at the head of the Bay of Bengal (Odisha–West Bengal–Bangladesh) because of the shallow shelf and funnel-shaped coast.
- Landmark storms: Odisha super cyclone (1999), Phailin (2013), Hudhud (2014), Fani (2019), Amphan (2020, super cyclone in the Bay), Tauktae (2021, Arabian Sea), Biparjoy (2023, long-lived Arabian Sea storm that hit Kutch).

### Water cycle and moisture — numbers and processes

- About 86% of global evaporation is from the oceans; the oceans lose more water by evaporation than they gain by rain, and the difference returns from land as river runoff.
- Average residence time of water: about 9 days in the atmosphere, weeks in rivers, years to decades in lakes, up to thousands of years in deep groundwater and ice sheets.
- Rising air cools adiabatically: about 9.8 °C per km when dry, about 5–6 °C per km once saturated (latent heat release) — the gap explains conditional instability and towering cumulonimbus.
- Humidity measures: absolute (grams per cubic metre), specific (grams per kg — unaffected by temperature change) and relative (percentage of saturation — falls as air warms).
- Precipitation growth: the ice-crystal (Bergeron) process in cold clouds and collision–coalescence in warm tropical clouds; hail needs strong updrafts in cumulonimbus.
- Cloud seeding uses silver iodide or salt particles to speed droplet growth; it cannot create clouds where none exist.
- Processes to name in answers: evaporation, transpiration (together evapotranspiration), sublimation, condensation, advection, precipitation, infiltration, percolation, runoff.

### Pressure and winds — extra concepts

- Above the friction layer winds blow almost parallel to isobars (geostrophic wind) because pressure-gradient force and Coriolis force balance.
- Buys Ballot's law: in the Northern Hemisphere, stand with your back to the wind and low pressure is on your left.
- Closely spaced isobars mean a steep pressure gradient and strong winds; anticyclones turn clockwise in the Northern Hemisphere and bring clear, calm, stable weather.
- Walker circulation is the east–west loop over the equatorial Pacific; the Southern Oscillation Index (Tahiti minus Darwin pressure) tracks it, and the Oceanic Niño Index tracks sea-surface temperature.
- Recent ENSO run: a rare 'triple-dip' La Niña (2020–23) was followed by El Niño in 2023–24, which helped make 2024 the warmest year on record.
- Jet streams flow in wavy Rossby waves; a wavier jet can lock weather in place, producing long heat waves or cold spells.
- Tornadoes are small, intense vortices over land (rare in India but recorded in Bengal and Odisha); waterspouts are their over-water equivalent.

### Punjab weather profile and PPSC traps

- Rainfall rises from under about 400 mm in the south-west (Fazilka, Abohar, Bathinda) to over 1,000 mm in the Shivalik foothills (Pathankot, Gurdaspur, Hoshiarpur) — distance from the Bay branch and orographic lift explain the gradient.
- Roughly three-quarters of Punjab's annual rain falls in July–September; western disturbances supply most of the rest in winter.
- Punjab's seasons: cold (November–March), hot (April–June), rainy (July–September) and a short post-monsoon transition in October.
- August–September 2025: heavy rain over the upper catchments in Himachal and Jammu & Kashmir swelled the Ravi, Beas and Sutlej and produced Punjab's worst floods since 1988.
- PPSC 2025 tested Ferrel-cell surface winds — remember surface westerlies blow from the subtropical high towards the subpolar low and are deflected to the right (east) in the Northern Hemisphere.
- Trap: trade winds are easterlies; westerlies dominate between about 35° and 60° latitude.
- Trap: 'loo' is a hot dry wind of the north Indian plains in May–June; 'mahawat' is winter rain from western disturbances, not a wind.

## Practice MCQs (12)

**1.** Tropical cyclones do not form within about 5 degrees of the equator mainly because:

(a) There is no water vapour  
(b) Sea surface temperature is too low  
(c) Pressure is too high  
(d) Coriolis force is negligible there

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) Coriolis force is negligible there** — Without enough Coriolis force, air cannot spin into a cyclonic vortex.

</details>

**2.** The 'Chinook' is a warm, dry local wind of the:

(a) Rocky Mountains  
(b) Andes  
(c) Alps  
(d) Sahara

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) Rocky Mountains** — Chinook descends the eastern Rockies and melts snow, hence 'snow eater'; Foehn is its Alpine counterpart.

</details>

**3.** Consider:  
1. El Nino years are usually associated with above-normal Indian monsoon rainfall.  
2. A positive Indian Ocean Dipole means warmer-than-normal sea surface temperatures in the eastern Indian Ocean near Sumatra and cooler waters in the west.  
Which is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) Neither 1 nor 2** — Statement 1 is wrong: El Nino years are usually linked to weaker, below-normal Indian monsoon rainfall. Statement 2 is wrong: in a positive IOD the western Indian Ocean is warmer and the eastern part near Sumatra is cooler than normal, a phase that generally helps the Indian monsoon.

</details>

**4.** Winter rainfall in Punjab is mainly caused by:

(a) Western disturbances  
(b) North-east monsoon  
(c) Loo winds  
(d) Tropical cyclones from the Bay of Bengal

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) Western disturbances** — Low-pressure systems from the Mediterranean region, carried by the westerly jet, bring winter rain to north-west India.

</details>

**5.** The leeward side of a mountain range receiving little rainfall is called a:

(a) Monsoon trough  
(b) Rain-shadow area  
(c) Windward zone  
(d) Doldrum

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Rain-shadow area** — Descending air on the leeward side warms and dries, as in Pune behind the Western Ghats.

</details>

**6.** In the Northern Hemisphere, winds in a tropical cyclone circulate:

(a) Clockwise  
(b) From east to west only  
(c) Anticlockwise  
(d) Straight towards the centre without turning

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Anticlockwise** — Coriolis deflection to the right produces anticlockwise rotation around the low in the Northern Hemisphere.

</details>

**9.** The low-level cross-equatorial current that carries moisture into the Arabian Sea branch of the south-west monsoon is the:

(a) Tropical easterly jet  
(b) Polar front jet  
(c) Somali (Findlater) jet  
(d) Subtropical westerly jet

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Somali (Findlater) jet** — The Somali jet flows along the East African coast at low levels.

**10.** Pre-monsoon showers in Karnataka that help coffee plants flower are called:

(a) Kalbaisakhi  
(b) Mango showers  
(c) Cherry blossom showers  
(d) Mahawat

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Cherry blossom showers** — Mango showers help mangoes in Kerala; kalbaisakhi are Bengal storms; mahawat is winter rain in north India.

**11.** According to Buys Ballot's law, if you stand with your back to the wind in the Northern Hemisphere, low pressure lies to your:

(a) Front  
(b) Left  
(c) Back  
(d) Right

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Left** — Coriolis deflection makes winds circulate anticlockwise around lows in the Northern Hemisphere.

</details>

**12.** Which index, based on the pressure difference between Tahiti and Darwin, is used to monitor ENSO?
