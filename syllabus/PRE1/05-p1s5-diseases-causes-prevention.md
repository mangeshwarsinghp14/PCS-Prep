## Short notes

- Diseases are communicable (caused by pathogens and transmitted) or non-communicable (lifestyle, genetic, deficiency, degenerative).
- Bacterial diseases: tuberculosis, typhoid, cholera, tetanus, diphtheria, whooping cough, leprosy, plague, bacterial pneumonia; treated with antibiotics.
- Viral diseases: common cold, influenza, measles, mumps, chickenpox, polio, rabies, hepatitis, dengue, chikungunya, HIV/AIDS, COVID-19, Japanese encephalitis; antibiotics do not act on viruses.
- Protozoan diseases: malaria (Plasmodium, spread by female Anopheles), kala-azar (Leishmania, sandfly), amoebic dysentery (Entamoeba histolytica), sleeping sickness (Trypanosoma, tsetse fly).
- Fungal: ringworm, athlete's foot, candidiasis. Helminths: roundworm (Ascaris), tapeworm, filariasis (Wuchereria, spread by Culex mosquito).
- Aedes aegypti (day-biting) spreads dengue, chikungunya, Zika and yellow fever; Culex spreads filariasis and Japanese encephalitis.
- Routes: airborne/droplet (TB, flu, measles); faeco-oral water and food (cholera, typhoid, hepatitis A and E, polio); blood and sexual (HIV, hepatitis B and C); vectors; zoonoses from animals (rabies, Nipah, bird flu, anthrax).
- Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin (1928); Edward Jenner developed the smallpox vaccine (1796); Louis Pasteur developed rabies vaccine and germ theory work; smallpox was declared eradicated by WHO in 1980.
- Immunity: innate (skin, mucus, fever, phagocytes) and acquired; active (infection or vaccination, long-lasting) vs passive (ready-made antibodies, e.g., colostrum/breast milk, antiserum).
- Vaccines contain killed/weakened pathogens, toxoids, proteins or mRNA that train memory cells; herd immunity protects the unvaccinated.
- Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) arises from misuse and overuse of antibiotics; India's 'Red Line' campaign marks prescription-only antibiotics.
- Lifestyle (non-communicable) diseases: type 2 diabetes, hypertension, coronary heart disease, stroke, obesity, many cancers and COPD; NCDs cause over 60% of deaths in India.
- Main NCD risk factors: tobacco, harmful alcohol use, unhealthy diet (high salt, sugar, trans-fat), physical inactivity and air pollution.
- Type 1 diabetes is insulin-dependent (autoimmune loss of beta cells); type 2 is mainly insulin resistance linked to obesity and inactivity.
- WHO BMI cut-offs: 25 overweight, 30 obese; lower cut-offs are often advised for Asian Indians because of higher risk at lower BMI.
- Genetic disorders: haemophilia and colour blindness (X-linked), sickle cell anaemia and thalassaemia (autosomal recessive); India launched the National Sickle Cell Anaemia Elimination Mission in 2023 with a 2047 goal.
- Prevention: safe water, sanitation, hand-washing, vector control, vaccination, balanced diet, exercise, tobacco control, early screening (BP, sugar, cancers).

## Punjab angle

- Malwa region has reported high cancer incidence; the Bathinda-Bikaner passenger train became popularly known as the 'cancer train' for patients travelling to Bikaner for treatment.
- Mukh Mantri Punjab Cancer Rahat Kosh scheme gives financial aid for cancer treatment; Mukh Mantri Sehat Yojana (rollout began September 2025) offers up to Rs 10 lakh cashless treatment per family.
- Substance abuse is a major public health issue in Punjab; the state's 'Yudh Nashian Virudh' campaign (2025) targets drug supply and de-addiction.
- Dengue outbreaks recur in Punjab in post-monsoon months (September-November) when Aedes breeding peaks.

## Deep dive

### Extended pathogen–disease–vector table

- Bacterial (beyond NCERT): anthrax, leptospirosis (rat urine, rises after floods), scrub typhus (Orientia, spread by mite larvae), brucellosis (from cattle and raw milk), botulism (Clostridium toxin), plague (rat flea).
- Viral (recent in India): Nipah (fruit bats; Kerala outbreaks from 2018), Kyasanur Forest Disease or 'monkey fever' (ticks, Karnataka), Chandipura (sandfly, Gujarat 2024), mpox, Zika (Aedes).
- Malaria: Plasmodium vivax and P. falciparum are both common in India; falciparum causes severe and cerebral malaria. Anopheles stephensi is the main urban vector.
- Fungal: mucormycosis ('black fungus') surged in 2021 among COVID-19 patients with uncontrolled diabetes and steroid use; Candida auris is a drug-resistant hospital fungus.
- Helminths: hookworm causes anaemia; India was certified free of guinea-worm disease in 2000; pork tapeworm larvae in the brain (neurocysticercosis) are a common cause of epilepsy.
- Prions are misfolded infectious proteins — mad cow disease (BSE) and Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease.
- Vectors to remember: Phlebotomus sandfly (kala-azar, Chandipura), Xenopsylla rat flea (plague), ticks (KFD, Lyme), mites (scrub typhus), Culex (filariasis, JE), Aedes (dengue, chikungunya, Zika).

### Non-communicable and lifestyle disease — data and programmes

- WHO: NCDs killed at least 43 million people in 2021, about three-quarters of non-pandemic deaths worldwide.
- ICMR-INDIAB study (2023): about 101 million Indians have diabetes and about 136 million prediabetes; hypertension affects roughly 315 million.
- Hypertension is defined as 140/90 mm Hg or more; the India Hypertension Control Initiative began in 2017, and the '75/25' initiative (2023) aimed to put 75 million people with hypertension or diabetes on standard care by 2025.
- Common cancers: breast and cervix in women, lung and mouth in men; oral cancer is closely tied to smokeless tobacco. Tobacco control law: COTPA 2003; e-cigarettes banned in 2019.
- Air pollution (PM2.5) is a major NCD risk; global burden studies attribute well over a million Indian deaths a year to it.
- Mental health: the Mental Healthcare Act 2017 effectively decriminalised attempted suicide; Tele-MANAS helpline 14416.
- Occupational diseases: silicosis (stone quarrying), asbestosis, byssinosis (cotton dust), black lung (coal dust); environmental: Minamata (mercury), itai-itai (cadmium), fluorosis.

### Discoveries and scientists — timeline

- 1796 — Edward Jenner's cowpox-based smallpox vaccine; 1854 — John Snow traced London cholera to a water pump (birth of epidemiology).
- 1860s — Louis Pasteur's germ theory and pasteurisation; 1882 — Robert Koch identified the TB bacillus (24 March is World TB Day).
- 1892 and 1897 — Waldemar Haffkine made cholera and plague vaccines in India (Haffkine Institute, Mumbai).
- 1897 — Ronald Ross, working in Secunderabad, showed mosquitoes transmit malaria (Nobel 1902); 20 August is World Mosquito Day.
- 1922 — U.N. Brahmachari in Kolkata developed urea stibamine against kala-azar.
- 1928 — Fleming's penicillin; 1943 — Waksman's streptomycin, the first anti-TB drug; 1955 and 1961 — Salk's injectable and Sabin's oral polio vaccines.
- 1983 — HIV identified by Montagnier and Barré-Sinoussi (Nobel 2008); 1986 — India's first HIV cases detected in Chennai by Dr Suniti Solomon's team.
- Recent Nobels: 2015 artemisinin (Tu Youyou), 2020 hepatitis C virus, 2023 mRNA vaccine technology (Karikó and Weissman).

### Prevention science and public-health tools

- Levels of prevention: primordial (stop risk factors arising), primary (vaccines, education), secondary (screening and early treatment, e.g. VIA test for cervical cancer), tertiary (rehabilitation).
- Endemic (constantly present), epidemic (sudden excess), pandemic (worldwide); incidence counts new cases, prevalence all existing cases.
- R0 is the average number infected by one case in a fully susceptible population; measles has one of the highest (about 12–18).
- Quarantine separates exposed but healthy people; isolation separates the sick.
- Vector control: removing stagnant water, larvivorous fish (Gambusia, guppy), Bti larvicide, treated bed nets, indoor spraying; Wolbachia-infected Aedes are being tried abroad.
- Pasteurisation heats milk to 63 C for 30 minutes or 72 C for 15 seconds; low-osmolarity ORS plus zinc is the standard for childhood diarrhoea.
- Antimicrobial stewardship: WHO's AWaRe list (Access, Watch, Reserve antibiotics); India banned colistin as an animal growth promoter in 2019; Kerala made the first state AMR action plan (2018).

### Punjab links

- Punjab began free hepatitis C treatment in 2016 through a dedicated relief fund — a model later reflected in the National Viral Hepatitis Control Programme (2018).
- Homi Bhabha Cancer Hospital and Research Centre units of Tata Memorial Centre at Sangrur and at New Chandigarh (Mullanpur, dedicated in August 2022) serve Punjab's cancer patients.
- Outpatient Opioid Assisted Treatment (OOAT) clinics, started in 2017, provide supervised treatment for opioid dependence; injecting drug use spreads HIV and hepatitis C.
- Dengue peaks after the monsoon in Ludhiana, Mohali, Patiala and Bathinda; the state runs weekly Friday anti-larval drives ('Har Shukarvar Dengue te Vaar').
- After the 2023 and 2025 floods, leptospirosis, scrub typhus and diarrhoeal disease risks rose, needing chlorination and surveillance.
- Pesticide exposure, contaminated groundwater and late diagnosis are studied as contributors to Malwa's cancer burden; population-based cancer registries give the data.

**1.** Which mosquito transmits dengue?

(a) Culex quinquefasciatus  
(b) Aedes aegypti  
(c) Female Anopheles  
(d) Sandfly

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Aedes aegypti** — Aedes aegypti, a day-biting mosquito, spreads dengue, chikungunya and Zika.

</details>

**2.** Which of the following diseases is caused by a protozoan?

(a) Typhoid  
(b) Kala-azar  
(c) Ringworm  
(d) Measles

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Kala-azar** — Kala-azar is caused by Leishmania donovani, spread by sandflies; typhoid is bacterial, measles viral, ringworm fungal.

</details>

**3.** Consider: 1. Hepatitis A and E spread mainly through contaminated food and water. 2. Hepatitis B spreads mainly through blood and body fluids. Which is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Both 1 and 2** — A and E are faeco-oral; B, C and D are blood-borne.

</details>

**4.** Antibiotics are NOT effective against:

(a) Cholera  
(b) Typhoid  
(c) Influenza  
(d) Tuberculosis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Influenza** — Influenza is viral; antibiotics act on bacteria.

</details>

**5.** Antibodies passed to a newborn through colostrum provide:

(a) Natural passive immunity  
(b) Natural active immunity  
(c) Innate immunity  
(d) Artificial active immunity

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) Natural passive immunity** — Ready-made maternal antibodies (mainly IgA) give short-term passive protection.

</details>

**6.** Which of the following is an X-linked recessive disorder?

(a) Haemophilia  
(b) Sickle cell anaemia  
(c) Down syndrome  
(d) Thalassaemia

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) Haemophilia** — Haemophilia genes lie on the X chromosome, so males are mostly affected; Down syndrome is a chromosomal disorder.

</details>

**7.** Consider the following statements:  
1. Mucormycosis ('black fungus') is a contagious disease that spreads from person to person like COVID-19.  
2. Mucormycosis is caused by a bacterium.  
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) Neither 1 nor 2** — Statement 1 is wrong: mucormycosis is not contagious; it is caught from fungal spores in the environment and surged in India's second COVID wave mainly among people with uncontrolled diabetes or on steroids. Statement 2 is wrong: it is caused by fungi (Mucorales moulds), not a bacterium.

</details>

**8.** Consider the following pairs (disease — cause):  
1. Byssinosis — cotton dust  
2. Silicosis — stone or quarry dust  
3. Minamata disease — cadmium  
Which of the pairs given above are correctly matched?

(a) 1 and 2 only  
(b) 2 and 3 only  
(c) 1 and 3 only  
(d) 1, 2 and 3

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) 1 and 2 only** — Minamata disease is caused by mercury; itai-itai by cadmium.

</details>

**9.** Who first showed that mosquitoes transmit malaria?

(a) Ronald Ross  
(b) Edward Jenner  
(c) Robert Koch  
(d) Alexander Fleming

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) Ronald Ross** — Ross did the work at Secunderabad in 1897.

</details>

**10.** Kyasanur Forest Disease ('monkey fever') is transmitted to humans by:

(a) Sandflies  
(b) Ticks  
(c) Rat fleas  
(d) Aedes mosquitoes

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Ticks** — Infected ticks transmit KFD virus in the Western Ghats.

</details>

**11.** Which of the following is a prion disease?

(a) Scrub typhus  
(b) Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease  
(c) Leptospirosis  
(d) Giardiasis

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease** — CJD is caused by misfolded prion proteins.

</details>

**12.** India's '75/25' initiative aimed to put 75 million people on standard care by 2025 for:

(a) Tuberculosis and HIV  
(b) Malaria and dengue
