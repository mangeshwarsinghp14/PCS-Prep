## Short notes

- Sufi orders in Punjab: Chishti (Baba Farid), Suhrawardi (Bahauddin Zakariya, Multan), Qadiri (Mian Mir, Lahore), Naqshbandi (Sheikh Ahmad Sirhindi).
- Ali Hujwiri (Data Ganj Bakhsh, Lahore, 11th c.) wrote Kashf-ul-Mahjub, the earliest Persian treatise on Sufism.
- Baba Farid (Sheikh Fariduddin Masud 'Ganj-i-Shakar', 1173–1266): Chishti saint of Ajodhan (Pakpattan); 4 shabads and 112 saloks in the Guru Granth Sahib; Faridkot is linked to his name.
- Mian Mir (Qadiri, Lahore): by tradition laid the foundation stone of Harmandir Sahib (1588) at Guru Arjan's request; Dara Shikoh was his admirer.
- Sheikh Ahmad Sirhindi (Mujaddid Alf-i-Sani, Naqshbandi): opposed Akbar's syncretic policies; his shrine Rauza Sharif is at Sirhind.
- Sufi poets: Shah Hussain (Lahore, kafis; Mela Chiraghan), Sultan Bahu (Qadiri), Bulleh Shah (1680–1757, Kasur; murshid Shah Inayat Qadiri), Waris Shah (Heer). Sakhi Sarwar (Lakhdata) was a popular saint.
- Bhakti saints in the Guru Granth Sahib include Kabir, Ravidas, Namdev, Trilochan, Beni, Dhanna, Sain, Pipa, Ramanand, Jaidev, Surdas, Parmanand, Sadhna, Bhikhan, plus Sheikh Farid.
- Guru Nanak (1469–1539): born at Talwandi Rai Bhoe (Nankana Sahib); worked at Daulat Khan Lodi's modikhana in Sultanpur Lodhi; enlightenment at Kali Bein; four udasis (travels); companion Bhai Mardana; settled at Kartarpur (Ravi), where he died.
- Guru Angad (1539–52): Khadur Sahib; popularised Gurmukhi; strengthened langar (Mata Khivi) and mall akharas.
- Guru Amar Das (1552–74): Goindwal baoli (84 steps); manji system (22 manjis); opposed sati and purdah; Anand Sahib; Akbar is said to have visited him.
- Guru Ram Das (1574–81): founded Ramdaspur/Chak Guru (later Amritsar) around 1577; composed the Lavan for Sikh marriage; started the masand system.
- Guru Arjan (1581–1606): built Harmandir Sahib (foundation 1588); compiled the Adi Granth in 1604 (scribe Bhai Gurdas, first granthi Baba Buddha); founded Tarn Taran and Kartarpur (Jalandhar); Sukhmani Sahib; martyred at Lahore in 1606 under Jahangir.
- Guru Hargobind (1606–44): miri-piri (two swords); built the Akal Takht (Akal Bunga, 1606); held in Gwalior fort — release celebrated as Bandi Chhor Divas; fought Mughal forces; shifted to Kiratpur Sahib.
- Guru Har Rai (1644–61): kept a cavalry and a herbal dispensary at Kiratpur. Guru Har Krishan (1661–64): youngest Guru; served smallpox patients in Delhi (Gurdwara Bangla Sahib).
- Guru Tegh Bahadur (1664–75): founded Chakk Nanaki (Anandpur Sahib, 1665); defended freedom of faith ('Hind di Chadar'); executed at Chandni Chowk, Delhi, in 1675 under Aurangzeb (Gurdwara Sis Ganj); 350th martyrdom anniversary in 2025.
- Guru Gobind Singh (1675–1708): born at Patna (1666); Battle of Bhangani (1688); created the Khalsa at Anandpur Sahib on Baisakhi 1699 (Panj Pyare); battles of Chamkaur (1704) and Khidrana/Muktsar (1705).
- Guru Gobind Singh wrote the Zafarnama to Aurangzeb; prepared the Damdami Bir at Talwandi Sabo (1706, scribe Bhai Mani Singh), adding Guru Tegh Bahadur's bani; vested Guruship in the Granth at Nanded (1708).
- Sahibzadas: Ajit Singh and Jujhar Singh fell at Chamkaur; Zorawar Singh and Fateh Singh were bricked alive at Sirhind on Wazir Khan's orders; 26 December is observed as Veer Bal Diwas (declared 2022).
- Guru Granth Sahib: 1,430 angs; arranged mainly by raga (31 ragas); contains the bani of six Gurus, 15 Bhagats, Bhatts and others; written in Gurmukhi script.

## Punjab angle

- Gurdwaras linked to Gurus in Punjab: Ber Sahib (Sultanpur Lodhi), Khadur Sahib, Goindwal Sahib, Tarn Taran, Kiratpur Sahib, Anandpur Sahib, Chamkaur Sahib, Fatehgarh Sahib, Muktsar, Damdama Sahib.

## How PPSC asks it

PPSC asks Guru–contribution–place matching, the sequence of Gurus, compilation of the Adi Granth/Damdami Bir, and Sufi order–saint pairs. Memorise dates of martyrdoms, the Khalsa (1699) and town foundations.

## Deep dive

### Sufi orders in Punjab: who, where, and what sets them apart

- Earliest Sufi presence: Shaikh Ismail Bukhari preached at Lahore in the early 11th century; Ali Hujwiri (Data Ganj Bakhsh, d. c.1072) followed in the Ghaznavid period. His Kashf-ul-Mahjub is in Persian.
- Suhrawardi order: Bahauddin Zakariya (c.1170–1262) made Multan its centre; successors Sadruddin Arif and Shaikh Ruknuddin (Rukn-i-Alam, whose Tughlaq-era tomb is a Multan landmark). Suhrawardis accepted state grants and kept close ties with rulers.
- Chishti chain: Moinuddin (Ajmer) → Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki (Delhi) → Baba Farid (Ajodhan/Pakpattan) → Nizamuddin Auliya (Delhi) and Alauddin Ali Ahmad Sabir (Kaliyar, founder of the Sabiri branch). Chishtis kept away from the court and used the local tongue.
- Farid's verses in the Adi Granth probably reached Guru Nanak through Farid's successors at Pakpattan (tradition names Shaikh Ibrahim). Some scholars attribute part of the corpus to Ibrahim ('Farid Sani'). This is a known authorship debate.
- Qadiri order: brought to India by Muhammad Ghaus of Uch (late 15th c.). Its Lahore centre was Mian Mir (1550–1635) and his disciple Mulla Shah Badakhshi. Dara Shikoh wrote the Sakinat-ul-Auliya about them. Sultan Bahu (1630–91) was also a Qadiri.
- Naqshbandi order: introduced by Khwaja Baqi Billah (d. 1603, Delhi). His disciple Shaikh Ahmad Sirhindi (1564–1624) wrote the Maktubat and argued for wahdat-ul-shuhud against Ibn Arabi's wahdat-ul-wujud. Jahangir briefly confined him at Gwalior (c.1619).
- Punjabi Sufi poetic forms: kafi (Shah Hussain 1538–99; Bulleh Shah 1680–1757), siharfi/abiat (Sultan Bahu), qissa (Waris Shah's Heer, 1766, written at Malka Hans). Bulleh Shah's murshid was Shah Inayat Qadiri of Lahore.
- Folk saints with mixed Hindu–Muslim followings: Sakhi Sarwar (Lakhdata; shrine at Nigaha, Dera Ghazi Khan; followers called Sultanias), Gugga Pir (Gugga Naumi fair) and Haidar Shaikh, the 15th-century founder figure of Malerkotla.
- Shah Hussain's shrine is known as 'Madho Lal Hussain'; the Mela Chiraghan (festival of lamps) is held there in Lahore.
- Trap: Mian Mir was Qadiri (not Chishti), Sirhindi was Naqshbandi, Zakariya was Suhrawardi and Farid was Chishti. Ali Hujwiri belonged to no Indian silsila and predates them all.

### Religious setting before Guru Nanak (PSEB Class XII Ch 3 / Class X Ch 2)

- Political setting: weak late-Lodi rule, Afghan factionalism, Babur's raids, oppressive officials and qazis. Guru Nanak described this as moral decline (kaljug) in his bani.
- Hindu sects in Punjab: Shaiva (Nath yogis of the Gorakhnath line), Vaishnava (Rama and Krishna bhakti) and Shakta (goddess shrines in the Shivaliks such as Jwalamukhi and Naina Devi).
- The Nath yogis' chief centre was Tilla Gorakhnath (Tilla Jogian, Jhelum). Guru Nanak's dialogue with siddhas is preserved as the Sidh Gosht.
- Islam in Punjab: Sunni ulema, Sufi khanqahs and shrine (dargah) culture; conversion mostly gradual, around Sufi centres and among artisans and peasants of the west.
- Social structure: varna and jati hierarchy, Khatri traders and officials, Jat peasantry, low-caste artisans; purdah, sati and female infanticide existed in some groups.
- Sant poets later included in the Guru Granth Sahib: Kabir, Ravidas, Namdev (shrine at Ghuman, Gurdaspur), Trilochan, Beni, Dhanna, Sain, Pipa, Sadhna, Ramanand, Parmanand, Surdas, Jaidev, Bhikhan and Sheikh Farid. Together they make the 15 Bhagats.
- The Guru Granth Sahib (standard print of 1,430 angs) has 36 contributors: 6 Gurus (1st–5th and 9th), 15 Bhagats, 11 Bhatts and 4 Sikhs (Mardana, Satta, Balwand, Sundar). Guru Arjan contributed the most hymns.
- Its hymns are arranged in 31 ragas; Jaijavanti was added with Guru Tegh Bahadur's bani in the Damdami Bir. Mul Mantar and Japji open the Granth outside the raga order.

### Guru-by-Guru: places, institutions and key facts

- Guru Nanak (1469–1539): Talwandi (Nankana Sahib); Sultanpur Lodhi (Ber Sahib, Kali Bein, c.1499); Eminabad (Bhai Lalo, Rori Sahib); Hasan Abdal (Panja Sahib). He founded Kartarpur on the Ravi c.1521–22, chose Bhai Lehna over his sons Sri Chand (Udasi founder) and Lakhmi Das, and wrote Japji, Asa di Var, Sidh Gosht, Babarvani, Onkar and the Barah Maha in Tukhari.
- Guru Angad (1504–52; Guru 1539–52): Khadur Sahib; standardised and popularised Gurmukhi; Mata Khivi ran the langar and is the only woman named in the Adi Granth (Satta–Balwand's Var); Mall Akhara for physical training; 63 saloks.
- Guru Amar Das (1479–1574; Guru 1552–74): Goindwal on the Beas, whose baoli with 84 steps was the first Sikh tirath; 22 manjis and 52 piris (women preachers); compulsory langar before audience; Anand Sahib; spoke against sati and purdah; fixed Baisakhi, Maghi and Diwali gatherings; the Goindwal pothis were precursors of the Adi Granth.
- Guru Ram Das (1534–81; Guru 1574–81): founded Ramdaspur (Guru ka Chak, c.1574–77) and its tank; invited traders of '52 trades'; composed the four Lavan of the Anand Karaj; the masand system began. The Guruship stayed in his Sodhi family thereafter.
- Guru Arjan (1563–1606; Guru 1581–1606): Harmandir with four doors; Tarn Taran (1590), Kartarpur in the Doaba (1594), Sri Hargobindpur and a baoli at Lahore. He compiled the Adi Granth at Ramsar (1603–04) and installed it in the Harmandir (1604) with Baba Buddha as granthi; wrote Sukhmani Sahib; organised dasvandh through the masands. Rivals: Prithi Chand (the Minas) and Chandu Shah.
- Guru Hargobind (1595–1644; Guru 1606–44): miri-piri; Akal Bunga (Akal Takht) facing the Harmandir; Lohgarh fort at Amritsar; release from Gwalior with 52 hill chiefs (Bandi Chhor Divas). Battles: Rohilla (c.1621), Amritsar and Lahira/Mehraj (1634), Kartarpur (1635, against Painde Khan). He then moved to Kiratpur.
- Guru Har Rai (1630–61; Guru 1644–61): Kiratpur; herbal dispensary and a stable of 2,200 horsemen by tradition; aided Dara Shikoh (1658). His son Ram Rai altered a line of bani at Aurangzeb's court and was disowned; Ram Rai's dera at Dehradun began the Ramraiya sect.
- Guru Har Krishan (1656–64; Guru 1661–64): called to Delhi; died of smallpox while tending the sick (site of Gurdwara Bangla Sahib, then Raja Jai Singh's bungalow). His last words 'Baba Bakale' pointed to the successor at Bakala.
- Guru Tegh Bahadur (1621–75; Guru 1664–75): recognised at Bakala by Makhan Shah Lubana; founded Chak Nanaki (Anandpur) in 1665 on land from the Kahlur (Bilaspur) raja; toured to Patna (where Gobind was born in 1666), Dhaka and Assam with Raja Ram Singh of Amber. Executed at Chandni Chowk in 1675 with Bhai Mati Das, Sati Das and Dayala. Bhai Jaita carried his head to Anandpur and Lakhi Shah Vanjara cremated his body (Rakab Ganj).
- Guru Gobind Singh (1666–1708; Guru 1675–1708): literary court at Paonta Sahib on the Yamuna (52 poets by tradition); Bhangani 1688 (against Fateh Shah of Garhwal); Nadaun 1691; Khalsa 1699; left Anandpur in Dec 1704; Chamkaur (elder Sahibzadas Ajit and Jujhar killed); Machhiwara; Zafarnama sent from Dina-Kangar; Muktsar 1705 (the 40 Mukte, Mai Bhago); Talwandi Sabo 1705–06; went south with Bahadur Shah; Nanded 1708.
- Younger Sahibzadas Zorawar Singh and Fateh Singh were executed at Sirhind (Dec 1704, traditional date) on Wazir Khan's orders; Sher Mohammad Khan of Malerkotla protested. 26 December has been observed as Veer Baal Diwas since 2022.

### Sikh institutions: origin and evolution

- Sangat and dharamsal (Guru Nanak at Kartarpur) were the community and its meeting place; the dharamsal later grew into the gurdwara.
- Langar or pangat began with Guru Nanak, was expanded under Guru Angad and Mata Khivi, and was made compulsory before an audience by Guru Amar Das: 'pangat before sangat'.
- Manji system (Guru Amar Das, 22 manjis plus piris) gave territorial preaching charges. Masands (from Guru Ram Das and Guru Arjan) collected dasvandh and kar-bhet. Guru Gobind Singh abolished the masands in 1699 because of their corruption and wrote hukamnamas directly to sangats.
- Scripture and centre: Harmandir and its sarovar (Guru Ram Das and Guru Arjan), the Adi Granth (1604), the Akal Takht (1606) and hukamnamas (written orders of the Gurus, now key primary sources).
- Miri-piri under Guru Hargobind brought two swords, an armed retinue, horses, the nagara and the nishan; followers began calling the Guru 'Sacha Patshah' (true king).
- Khalsa (Baisakhi 1699, Anandpur): Panj Pyare were Daya Ram (Lahore Khatri), Dharam Das (Delhi Jat), Himmat Rai (Jagannath Puri water-carrier), Mohkam Chand (Dwarka washerman) and Sahib Chand (Bidar barber). The rite was khande di pahul, followed by the Five Ks, the names Singh and Kaur, and a rahit with prohibitions (kurahits).
- In 1708 Guruship passed to the Granth (Guru Granth) and the collective Khalsa (Guru Panth). This is the doctrinal basis of the 18th-century Sarbat Khalsa and the gurmata.
- Five Takhts: Akal Takht (Amritsar), Keshgarh Sahib (Anandpur), Patna Sahib, Hazur Sahib (Nanded) and Damdama Sahib (Talwandi Sabo), which the SGPC accepted as the fifth Takht in 1966.
- Dissenting lines: Udasis (Sri Chand), Minas (Prithi Chand; the Miharban Janamsakhi), Dhirmalias (Dhir Mal of Kartarpur, who held the Kartarpur Bir) and Ramraiyas. The Khalsa rahit asked Sikhs to avoid them.

### Sources and historiography (PSEB Class XII Ch 2; Mains)

- Janamsakhis (hagiographic lives of Guru Nanak): Puratan (Colebrooke and Hafizabad versions), Bhai Bala, Miharban and Adi. They are useful for tradition but unreliable on chronology.
- Bhai Gurdas (Vars and Kabitts) is called the 'key to the Guru Granth' and is the best early account of the Panth. Bhai Nand Lal Goya wrote Persian poetry at Guru Gobind Singh's court.
- Persian sources: the Dabistan-i-Mazahib (c.1645–55) describes the masands and Sikh practice; Jahangir's Tuzuk records the order against Guru Arjan; the Khulasat-ut-Tawarikh (Sujan Rai Bhandari, 1695–96) describes Punjab; the Siyar-ul-Mutakhirin covers the 18th century.
- Sikh writings: Bachittar Natak (autobiographical, in the Dasam Granth), Sainapati's Gur Sobha (c.1711), Kesar Singh Chhibber's Bansavalinama (1769), Rattan Singh Bhangu's Panth Prakash (1841) and Santokh Singh's Suraj Prakash (1843).
- European writers: Malcolm (Sketch of the Sikhs, 1812), Prinsep (1834), Cunningham (1849) and Macauliffe (The Sikh Religion, 6 vols, 1909, with translations). All are public domain and listed in the reading links.
- Debate 1: was Guru Nanak a Sant/Bhakti reformer (W.H. McLeod's 'Sant synthesis') or the founder of a distinct, revealed faith (most Sikh scholars)? A balanced answer stresses his new institutions: Kartarpur, the succession and his bani.
- Debate 2: the 'militarisation' under Guru Hargobind. McLeod cites the influx of Jats; Grewal and Ganda Singh stress Mughal hostility after 1606. Most answers combine both.
- Debate 3: the authorship and compilation of the Dasam Granth (Bhai Mani Singh's role) remain contested. Mention it neutrally if asked.

### PPSC patterns and traps

- Place–Guru matching is the most common prelims format: Khadur (Angad), Goindwal (Amar Das), Ramdaspur (Ram Das), Tarn Taran (Arjan), Kiratpur (Hargobind and Har Rai), Anandpur (Tegh Bahadur), Paonta and Nanded (Gobind Singh).
- Compositions: Japji, Babarvani and Asa di Var (Nanak); Anand Sahib (Amar Das); Lavan (Ram Das); Sukhmani (Arjan); Jaap Sahib and the Persian Zafarnama (Gobind Singh).
- Two compilations: the Adi Granth (1604, scribe Bhai Gurdas, first granthi Baba Buddha) and the Damdami Bir (1706, Talwandi Sabo, scribe Bhai Mani Singh, adding Guru Tegh Bahadur's bani).
- Family links that are often tested: Tegh Bahadur was Hargobind's son; Har Rai was Hargobind's grandson; Har Krishan was Har Rai's son.
- Contemporaries: Nanak with the Lodis and Babur; Arjan with Akbar and Jahangir; Hargobind with Jahangir and Shah Jahan; Tegh Bahadur with Aurangzeb; Gobind Singh with Aurangzeb and Bahadur Shah.
- Machhiwara appears twice: Humayun's victory (1555) and Guru Gobind Singh's escape (1704). Do not confuse them.
- Anniversaries as current-affairs hooks: Guru Nanak's 550th Prakash Purb (2019, Kartarpur Corridor), Guru Tegh Bahadur's 400th birth anniversary (2021) and 350th martyrdom (2025), and Veer Baal Diwas (26 Dec, from 2022).

## Practice MCQs (16)

**1.** Baba Sheikh Farid belonged to which Sufi order?

(a) Qadiri  
(b) Naqshbandi  
(c) Suhrawardi  
(d) Chishti

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) Chishti** — Baba Farid was a disciple of Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki and is one of the great Chishti saints.

</details>

**2.** Sheikh Ahmad Sirhindi, called Mujaddid Alf-i-Sani, belonged to which order?

(a) Naqshbandi  
(b) Chishti  
(c) Suhrawardi  
(d) Qadiri

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) Naqshbandi** — This Naqshbandi saint opposed Akbar's religious syncretism; his shrine is at Sirhind.

</details>

**3.** Which Guru built the baoli at Goindwal and organised the manji system?

(a) Guru Angad  
(b) Guru Ram Das  
(c) Guru Amar Das  
(d) Guru Arjan

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Guru Amar Das** — Guru Amar Das set up 22 manjis and built the 84-step baoli at Goindwal.

</details>

**4.** The Akal Takht was established by:

(a) Guru Gobind Singh  
(b) Guru Hargobind  
(c) Guru Arjan  
(d) Guru Har Rai

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Guru Hargobind** — Guru Hargobind built the Akal Bunga (1606), symbolising miri (temporal authority).

</details>

**5.** Who was the scribe of the Adi Granth compiled in 1604?

(a) Bhai Gurdas  
(b) Bhai Nand Lal  
(c) Baba Buddha  
(d) Bhai Mani Singh

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) Bhai Gurdas** — Bhai Gurdas wrote the Adi Granth; Baba Buddha was the first granthi; Bhai Mani Singh was scribe of the Damdami Bir.

</details>

**6.** Consider the statements: 1. Guru Tegh Bahadur founded Chakk Nanaki, later Anandpur Sahib. 2. Guru Tegh Bahadur was executed at Lahore. Which is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2
