## Short notes

- Matter has mass and occupies space; classical states are solid (fixed shape and volume), liquid (fixed volume) and gas (neither fixed).
- Plasma (ionised gas: Sun, stars, lightning, neon signs) is the fourth state; Bose-Einstein condensate (near absolute zero) is the fifth, predicted by S.N. Bose and Einstein.
- Sublimation is direct solid-to-gas change: camphor, naphthalene, ammonium chloride, iodine and dry ice (solid CO2).
- Evaporation is a surface process at any temperature and causes cooling; it rises with temperature, surface area and wind speed, and falls as humidity rises.
- Latent heat is absorbed during a change of state without a rise in temperature; steam at 100 C burns more than boiling water because of latent heat of vaporisation.
- Kelvin = Celsius + 273 (precisely 273.15); absolute zero is 0 K (-273.15 C).
- Dalton's atomic theory (1808) treated atoms as indivisible; J.J. Thomson found the electron (1897) and proposed the plum-pudding model.
- Rutherford's gold-foil experiment (1911) showed a tiny dense positive nucleus; Bohr (1913) placed electrons in fixed energy shells; Chadwick found the neutron (1932).
- Atomic number (Z) = number of protons; mass number (A) = protons + neutrons; maximum electrons in a shell = 2n squared (K=2, L=8, M=18).
- Isotopes: same Z, different A (protium, deuterium, tritium). Uses: U-235 nuclear fuel, Co-60 cancer therapy, I-131 thyroid treatment, C-14 dating (half-life about 5,730 years).
- Isobars: same mass number, different atomic number (e.g., Ca-40 and Ar-40).
- Carbon is versatile because of tetravalency (four covalent bonds) and catenation (bonding with other carbon atoms in long chains, branches and rings).
- Allotropes of carbon: diamond (each C bonded to 4 others, hardest natural substance, non-conductor), graphite (each C bonded to 3, layered, conducts electricity, lubricant), fullerene C60, graphene.
- Hydrocarbons: alkanes CnH2n+2 (saturated, single bonds), alkenes CnH2n (double bond), alkynes CnH2n-2 (triple bond); members of a homologous series differ by CH2.
- Functional groups: alcohol (-OH), aldehyde (-CHO), ketone (>C=O), carboxylic acid (-COOH), halogens.
- Ethanol is the alcohol in drinks and a fuel blend; ethanoic (acetic) acid in 5-8% water solution is vinegar; acid + alcohol gives sweet-smelling esters (esterification).
- Saturated hydrocarbons burn with a clean blue flame; unsaturated ones give a sooty yellow flame. Hydrogenation of vegetable oil with nickel catalyst gives vanaspati ghee.
- Saponification: ester or fat + sodium hydroxide gives soap and glycerol; carbon also forms covalent compounds that are generally poor conductors with low melting points.

## Deep dive

### Atomic models and discoveries — timeline (year — event)

- c. 6th–2nd century BCE — Kanada (Vaisheshika school) spoke of indivisible 'parmanu'; Democritus used 'atomos'. Both were philosophical ideas, not experiments.
- 1803–1808 — John Dalton's atomic theory (atoms of an element identical, combine in whole-number ratios); later corrected by the discovery of isotopes and subatomic particles.
- 1869 — Mendeleev arranged elements by atomic mass and left gaps for undiscovered elements (eka-silicon was later found as germanium).
- 1886 — Goldstein observed canal (positive) rays, the first hint of the proton; Rutherford later named the proton (c. 1920).
- 1897 — J.J. Thomson measured the charge-to-mass ratio of cathode-ray particles (electrons); 1909 — Millikan's oil-drop experiment measured the electron's charge (about 1.6 x 10^-19 C).
- 1911 — Rutherford's alpha-scattering on gold foil: most alphas passed straight, a few bounced back, so the atom has a tiny dense nucleus.
- 1913 — Bohr's fixed energy levels explained the hydrogen spectrum; Moseley showed atomic number (not mass) orders the periodic table (modern periodic law).
- 1924–1927 — de Broglie's matter waves, Schrodinger's wave equation and Heisenberg's uncertainty principle replaced fixed orbits with probability 'orbitals'.
- 1924–1925 — S.N. Bose's photon statistics, extended by Einstein, predicted the Bose-Einstein condensate; first made in 1995 by Cornell and Wieman with rubidium atoms.
- 1932 — Chadwick discovered the neutron; 1938 — Hahn and Strassmann split uranium (nuclear fission).

### States of matter — applications asked in exams

- Pressure cooker: higher pressure raises water's boiling point (to about 120 C), so food cooks faster; in the hills low air pressure lowers the boiling point and cooking is slow.
- Dissolved impurities raise the boiling point and lower the freezing point — salt on icy roads, and an ice-salt mixture to freeze kulfi.
- Pressure lowers the melting point of ice (regelation) — why a wire passes through an ice block and why skating blades glide on a thin water film.
- Earthen pots (matka) cool water by evaporation through pores; desert coolers work well in dry heat but poorly in humid monsoon air.
- Dry ice (solid CO2) sublimes at about -78.5 C — used to ship frozen food and some vaccines, and for stage fog.
- LPG is mainly propane and butane liquefied under pressure; it is odourless, so a sulphur compound (ethyl mercaptan) is added to detect leaks. CNG is mainly methane compressed to about 200 bar.
- Liquid nitrogen (about -196 C) stores bull semen straws for artificial insemination and biological samples (cryopreservation).
- Plasma is used in fluorescent and neon lamps and arises naturally in lightning and the Sun; diffusion is fastest in gases, which is why incense smell spreads quickly.
- Colloids scatter light (Tyndall effect) — a sunbeam in a dusty room or headlights in fog; milk is an emulsion, fog a liquid aerosol, smoke a solid aerosol, shaving cream a foam, jelly a gel.
- Brownian motion (Robert Brown, 1827, pollen grains in water) gave visible evidence that particles of a fluid are in constant random motion.

### Radioactivity and nuclear applications

- Henri Becquerel discovered radioactivity in uranium (1896); Marie and Pierre Curie found polonium and radium (1898). Marie Curie won Nobel Prizes in Physics (1903) and Chemistry (1911).
- Alpha particles (helium nuclei) are stopped by paper, beta particles (electrons) by a thin aluminium sheet, gamma rays (electromagnetic) need thick lead or concrete.
- Half-life is the time for half the radioactive nuclei to decay; carbon-14 dating works up to roughly 50,000 years, while uranium-lead and potassium-argon methods date much older rocks.
- Medical isotopes: technetium-99m is the most widely used imaging isotope; iodine-131 treats thyroid disease; cobalt-60 gamma rays treat cancer and sterilise medical goods.
- Food irradiation with cobalt-60 delays sprouting of onions and potatoes and kills insects; BARC set up a demonstration facility for onions at Lasalgaon (Nashik).
- Fission (U-235 plus a neutron) runs nuclear reactors; fusion of hydrogen to helium powers the Sun. India is a member of the ITER fusion project in France.
- India's three-stage nuclear plan: pressurised heavy water reactors on natural uranium, then fast breeder reactors (Prototype Fast Breeder Reactor, Kalpakkam, core loading began March 2024), then thorium-based reactors using monazite sands of Kerala and Odisha.
- Heavy water (D2O) slows neutrons (moderator) in PHWRs; the SI unit of radioactivity is the becquerel and of absorbed radiation dose to people the sievert.

### Carbon beyond NCERT — allotropes, materials, fuels

- Fullerene C60 (football-shaped buckminsterfullerene) was discovered in 1985 by Kroto, Curl and Smalley (Nobel Chemistry 1996).
- Graphene is a one-atom-thick sheet of carbon, isolated in 2004 by Geim and Novoselov (Nobel Physics 2010); it is extremely strong and a very good conductor — used in sensors, batteries and coatings.
- Carbon nanotubes are rolled graphene sheets with very high tensile strength; amorphous carbon (charcoal, coke, lamp black) — activated charcoal adsorbs gases and is used in gas masks and water filters.
- Diamond sparkles because of a high refractive index (about 2.42) and a small critical angle (about 24 degrees), causing repeated total internal reflection; lab-grown diamonds are made by HPHT or CVD methods.
- Coal ranks by carbon content: anthracite (highest) > bituminous > lignite > peat (lowest); petroleum is separated by fractional distillation; octane number rates petrol, cetane number rates diesel.
- India reached its 20% ethanol-blending (E20) goal in 2025, ahead of the original 2030 target; compressed biogas (CBG) under the SATAT scheme (2018) uses farm and municipal waste.
- Polymers: polythene (from ethene), PVC, Teflon (PTFE, non-stick), nylon (first fully synthetic fibre), bakelite and melamine (thermosetting); India banned identified single-use plastic items from 1 July 2022.
- Atmospheric CO2 has risen from about 280 ppm before industrialisation to above 420 ppm today — the core of the greenhouse-gas question.

### Elements and periodic table — quick facts PPSC likes

- Modern periodic table: 118 elements, 7 periods, 18 groups; elements 113, 115, 117, 118 were named in 2016 — nihonium, moscovium, tennessine, oganesson.
- Most abundant in Earth's crust: oxygen, then silicon; aluminium is the most abundant metal, iron next. Hydrogen is most abundant in the universe; nitrogen (about 78%) in air; oxygen by mass in the human body.
- Mercury is the only metal liquid at room temperature; bromine the only liquid non-metal; gallium melts in the hand (about 30 C).
- Lightest element hydrogen, lightest metal lithium, densest natural element osmium, best conductor silver, hardest natural substance diamond.
- Noble gases: helium (balloons, mixed with oxygen for deep-sea diving), neon (advertising signs), argon (bulbs, welding shield), radon (radioactive, indoor-air hazard).
- Hydrogen isotopes: protium, deuterium (in heavy water) and radioactive tritium (fusion fuel).
- Alloys: brass = copper + zinc; bronze = copper + tin; solder = lead + tin; duralumin (aluminium-based, aircraft); German silver = copper + zinc + nickel (no silver); amalgam = any mercury alloy.

**1.** Which of the following substances sublimes on heating?

(a) Sugar  
(b) Camphor  
(c) Copper sulphate  
(d) Sodium chloride

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Camphor** — Camphor changes directly from solid to vapour; the others melt or decompose.

**2.** Who discovered the neutron?

(a) Ernest Rutherford  
(b) Niels Bohr  
(c) James Chadwick  
(d) J.J. Thomson

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) James Chadwick** — Chadwick discovered the neutron in 1932; Thomson found the electron and Rutherford the nucleus.

</details>

**3.** Which isotope is used to estimate the age of fossils and archaeological wood?

(a) Iodine-131  
(b) Cobalt-60  
(c) Uranium-235  
(d) Carbon-14

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) Carbon-14** — C-14 dating works on organic remains up to roughly 50,000 years using a half-life of about 5,730 years.

</details>

**4.** Consider: 1. Graphite conducts electricity because each carbon is bonded to only three others, leaving free electrons. 2. Diamond is a good conductor of heat but a poor conductor of electricity. Which is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Both 1 and 2** — Both are correct: graphite has delocalised electrons; diamond has no free electrons but conducts heat very well.

</details>

**5.** Members of a homologous series differ from the next member by:

(a) One carbon atom only  
(b) A CH2 unit (14 u)  
(c) A CH4 unit (16 u)  
(d) Two hydrogen atoms only

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) A CH2 unit (14 u)** — Successive members differ by -CH2-, i.e., 14 u in molecular mass.

</details>

**6.** Steam at 100 C causes more severe burns than boiling water at 100 C because steam:

(a) Is hotter than boiling water  
(b) Releases extra latent heat as it condenses  
(c) Has a much higher specific heat  
(d) Is denser than boiling water

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Releases extra latent heat as it condenses** — On condensing, steam releases its latent heat in addition to the heat of water at 100 C.

</details>

**7.** Consider the following statements:  
1. Isobars have the same atomic number but different mass numbers.  
2. Isotopes of an element have nearly identical chemical properties.  
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) 2 only** — Isotopes share the same electron arrangement, so chemistry is alike; isobars share mass number, not atomic number.

</details>

**8.** Consider the following statements:  
1. LPG is mainly a mixture of propane and butane.  
2. CNG is mainly methane.  
3. A strong-smelling sulphur compound is added to LPG to detect leaks.  
Which of the statements given above are correct?

(a) 1 and 2 only  
(b) 2 and 3 only  
(c) 1 and 3 only  
(d) 1, 2 and 3

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) 1, 2 and 3** — All three are correct; LPG itself is odourless, so ethyl mercaptan is added.

</details>

**9.** Graphene, whose isolation won the 2010 Nobel Prize in Physics, is best described as:

(a) A hollow cage-shaped C60 carbon molecule  
(b) A rolled carbon tube filled with a metal core  
(c) A disordered amorphous carbon like charcoal  
(d) A one-atom-thick hexagonal sheet of carbon

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) A one-atom-thick hexagonal sheet of carbon** — Graphene is a one-atom-thick hexagonal sheet; C60 is fullerene.

</details>

**10.** Food cooks faster in a pressure cooker mainly because:

(a) Steam has a lower temperature than boiling water  
(b) Pressure lowers the boiling point of water  
(c) The higher pressure raises the boiling point of water  
(d) The lid reflects heat back into the food

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) The higher pressure raises the boiling point of water** — Higher pressure lets water reach about 120 C before boiling.

</details>

**11.** Which type of radiation is the most penetrating?

(a) Beta particles  
(b) Alpha particles  
(c) Cathode rays  
(d) Gamma rays

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) Gamma rays** — Gamma rays are high-energy electromagnetic waves stopped only by thick lead or concrete.

</details>

**12.** Which variety of coal has the highest carbon content?

(a) Peat  
(b) Anthracite  
(c) Bituminous  
(d) Lignite

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Anthracite** — Anthracite is the highest-rank coal; peat the lowest.

</details>
