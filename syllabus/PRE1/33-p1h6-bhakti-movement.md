## Short notes

- Bhakti means personal, loving devotion to a god; it began in Tamil Nadu between the 6th and 9th centuries CE and spread north from about the 13th–15th centuries.
- Alvars (12 saints, devotees of Vishnu) and Nayanars (63 saints, devotees of Shiva) sang in Tamil; Andal was the only woman Alvar, Karaikkal Ammaiyar a woman Nayanar.
- Alvar hymns were compiled as the Nalayira Divya Prabandham; Nayanar hymns as the Tevaram, with lives told in the Periya Puranam.
- Shankaracharya (8th century, born at Kalady, Kerala) taught Advaita (non-dualism) and set up mathas at Sringeri, Dwarka, Puri and Jyotirmath.
- Vaishnava acharyas: Ramanuja — Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dualism); Madhvacharya — Dvaita (dualism); Nimbarka — Dvaitadvaita; Vallabhacharya — Shuddhadvaita and the Pushti Marg.
- Basavanna (12th century, Karnataka) founded the Lingayat/Virashaiva movement, wrote vachanas, rejected caste and ritual, and set up the Anubhava Mantapa.
- Maharashtra's Varkari tradition centred on Vitthala of Pandharpur: Jnaneshwar (Jnaneshwari, a Marathi commentary on the Gita), Namdev, Eknath and Tukaram; Ramdas wrote the Dasbodh.
- Ramananda carried Bhakti to the north, preached in Hindi and accepted disciples of all castes — by tradition Kabir, Ravidas, Dhanna, Sena and Pipa.
- Nirguna Bhakti worships a formless god (Kabir, Guru Nanak, Ravidas, Dadu Dayal); Saguna Bhakti worships god with form, e.g., Rama or Krishna (Tulsidas, Surdas, Mirabai, Chaitanya).
- Kabir (15th century, a weaver of Banaras) attacked idol worship, caste and ritual in both Hinduism and Islam; his verses (dohas) are collected in the Bijak and other compilations.
- Tulsidas wrote the Ramcharitmanas in Awadhi; Surdas (disciple of Vallabhacharya) wrote the Sursagar in Braj on Krishna; Mirabai, a Rajput princess, sang of Krishna.
- Chaitanya Mahaprabhu (Bengal, 16th century) popularised Krishna devotion through sankirtan (Gaudiya Vaishnavism); Sankaradeva in Assam founded Ekasarana dharma with namghars and satras.
- Other saints: Narsinh Mehta (Gujarat), Purandara Dasa (Karnataka, called the father of Carnatic music), Lalla/Lal Ded (Kashmir).
- Common features: devotion over ritual, guru's importance, rejection of caste and priestly control, use of regional languages, equality of men and women before god.
- Impact: growth of regional literature (Hindi, Marathi, Punjabi, Bengali, Kannada), social reform, emergence of Sikhism, and mutual influence with Sufism.
- Guru Nanak (1469–1539), born at Talwandi (Nankana Sahib), preached nirguna devotion to one God, honest labour and sharing; set up sangat and langar and settled at Kartarpur.

## Punjab angle

- Guru Nanak's teaching grew into Sikhism; the Adi Granth compiled by Guru Arjan Dev (1604) includes compositions of Bhakti saints such as Kabir, Namdev, Ravidas and of the Sufi Baba Farid.
- Bhagat Namdev of Maharashtra is associated with Ghuman (Gurdaspur), where a shrine in his memory stands.
- Guru Ravidas has a large following in Doaba; Dera Sachkhand Ballan (Jalandhar) is a major Ravidassia centre.

## Deep dive

### Saint-by-saint timeline (period — saint — region — key contribution)

- 6th–9th c. — Alvars and Nayanars — Tamil country — hymns (pasurams; Tevaram); Nathamuni compiled the Alvar hymns (10th c.); Nambi Andar Nambi the Shaiva canon (Tirumurai).
- 8th c. — Adi Shankaracharya — Kerala to all India — Advaita; commentaries on Brahma Sutra, Upanishads, Gita; four mathas (Sringeri, Dwarka, Puri, Jyotirmath).
- 11th–12th c. — Ramanuja — Srirangam — Vishishtadvaita; Sri Bhashya; reconciled Alvar devotion with Vedanta.
- 12th c. — Basavanna, Akka Mahadevi, Allama Prabhu — Karnataka — Lingayat vachanas; Anubhava Mantapa at Kalyana under the Kalachuri king Bijjala's minister Basava.
- 13th c. — Madhvacharya (Udupi) — Dvaita; Jnaneshwar (Jnaneshwari, 1290, Marathi Gita commentary); Namdev (c. 1270–1350) — Varkari saint who travelled to Punjab.
- 14th c. — Ramananda (Varanasi) — Rama bhakti in Hindi for all castes; Lal Ded/Lalla (Kashmir) — Shaiva mystic poetess (vakhs).
- 15th c. — Kabir (weaver, Varanasi; died at Maghar) — Bijak, sakhis, ramainis; Ravidas (leather-worker); Sankardeva (Assam, 1449–1568) — Ekasarana dharma, namghars, sattras, Ankiya Naat, Borgeet.
- 1469–1539 — Guru Nanak — Punjab — nirguna bhakti, langar, sangat, 'nam japna, kirat karni, vand chhakna'; four udasis; settled at Kartarpur (on the Ravi).
- 1479–1531 — Vallabhacharya — Shuddhadvaita, Pushti Marg; son Vitthalnath formed the Ashtachhap (eight poets, including Surdas).
- 1486–1534 — Chaitanya Mahaprabhu — Bengal/Odisha — Gaudiya Vaishnavism, sankirtan, Achintya Bhedabheda (codified by the Gosvamis of Vrindavan).
- 16th c. — Mirabai (Rajasthan), Surdas (Sursagar, Brajbhasha), Tulsidas (Ramcharitmanas, Awadhi; Vinay Patrika), Dadu Dayal (Rajasthan; Dadu Panth), Purandara Dasa and Kanaka Dasa (Karnataka Haridasas).
- 17th c. — Tukaram (abhangas; Dehu), Ramdas (Dasbodh), Bahinabai — Maharashtra; Narsinh Mehta (15th c.) of Gujarat composed 'Vaishnava jana to'.

### Vedanta schools behind Bhakti (philosophy — acharya — key idea)

- Advaita — Shankara — Brahman alone is real; world is maya; liberation through knowledge (jnana).
- Vishishtadvaita — Ramanuja — Brahman is qualified by souls and matter; devotion (prapatti, surrender) leads to moksha.
- Dvaitadvaita (Bhedabheda) — Nimbarka — difference and non-difference; Radha–Krishna worship; associated with Vrindavan.
- Dvaita — Madhva — eternal difference between God, souls and matter; Vishnu supreme.
- Shuddhadvaita — Vallabha — 'pure non-dualism'; Krishna as Shrinathji (Nathdwara); grace (pushti) over ritual.
- Achintya Bhedabheda — Chaitanya tradition — inconceivable oneness and difference.
- Nirguna sants (Kabir, Nanak, Ravidas, Dadu) stand outside these schools — they drew on Nath yoga, Vaishnava devotion and Sufi ideas.

### Punjab focus — bhagat bani and the Sant tradition

- The Adi Granth (1604, compiled by Guru Arjan Dev at Amritsar; Bhai Gurdas as scribe) includes 15 bhagats besides the Gurus — Kabir, Farid, Namdev, Ravidas, Trilochan, Beni, Dhanna, Jaidev, Sain, Pipa, Sadhna, Ramanand, Parmanand, Surdas, Bhikhan.
- Kabir's compositions form the largest bhagat share; Sheikh Farid's shlokas are among the earliest Punjabi poetry.
- Jaidev (author of the Sanskrit Gita Govinda) has two hymns in the Granth — a link between eastern Vaishnavism and Punjab.
- Bhagat Namdev's association with Ghuman (Gurdaspur) and Bhagat Sadhna's with Sirhind give Punjab its own bhagat shrines.
- Siddh Gosht in the Granth records Guru Nanak's dialogue with Nath yogis — Nath (Gorakhnath) tradition was strong in Punjab (Tilla Jogian).
- Guru Ravidas — large following in Doaba; Ravidassia identity (Dera Sachkhand Ballan, Jalandhar) and Ravidas Jayanti are Punjab current-affairs items.
- Punjabi Sufi poets: Baba Farid (Pakpattan), Shah Hussain (Lahore; Mela Chiraghan), Sultan Bahu, Bulleh Shah (Kasur), Waris Shah (Heer, 1766).
- Mian Mir, Qadiri Sufi of Lahore, is traditionally said to have laid the foundation stone of Harmandir Sahib (1588) — a symbol of Sikh–Sufi amity.

### Sufism — orders, terms and Indian centres

- Chishti — Khwaja Muinuddin (Ajmer, from c. 1192), Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki (Delhi), Fariduddin Ganj-i-Shakar (Pakpattan), Nizamuddin Auliya (Delhi), Nasiruddin Chiragh-i-Delhi, Gesudaraz (Gulbarga), Salim Chishti (Fatehpur Sikri). Kept away from courts, used sama (music).
- Suhrawardi — Bahauddin Zakariya (Multan) — accepted state patronage and grants, unlike the Chishtis.
- Firdausi — Sharafuddin Yahya Maneri (Bihar); Qadiri — Mian Mir and Mulla Shah (Dara Shikoh's teachers); Shattari — Muhammad Ghaus (Gwalior; Tansen's teacher by tradition).
- Naqshbandi — Khwaja Baqi Billah; Sheikh Ahmad Sirhindi ('Mujaddid-i-Alf-i-Sani', Sirhind) opposed Akbar's eclecticism and taught wahdat-ush-shuhud (unity of witness).
- Wahdat-ul-wujud (unity of being), from Ibn Arabi, was the dominant Sufi idea that Sirhindi challenged.
- Terms: khanqah (hospice), pir/shaikh and murid, silsila (chain), zikr (remembrance), sama (audition/music), urs (death anniversary), dargah (shrine), wilayat (spiritual territory).
- Ba-shara (following sharia) vs be-shara (qalandars, malangs) — two styles of Sufis in India.

### Significance, limits and historiography

- Language: Bhakti made regional languages literary — Tamil, Kannada, Marathi, Hindi dialects (Awadhi, Braj), Punjabi, Bengali, Assamese.
- Society: challenged ritual and caste pride; many saints came from 'low' occupations — Kabir (weaver), Ravidas (leather-worker), Sain (barber), Namdev (tailor/calico printer), Dhanna (Jat peasant).
- Women: Andal, Karaikkal Ammaiyar, Akka Mahadevi, Lal Ded, Janabai, Mirabai, Bahinabai — voices against patriarchal norms.
- Limits: Saguna bhakti often stayed within Brahmanical frames; caste was critiqued spiritually more than dismantled socially.
- Interaction with Sufism produced shared shrines, music (qawwali, kirtan) and ideas of a formless God.
- Historiography: older nationalist view of a single 'Bhakti movement'; Marxist readings stress artisan/peasant protest; J.S. Hawley ('A Storm of Songs', 2015) shows the 'movement' idea itself was shaped in the 20th century.
- For Punjab, scholars (W.H. McLeod, J.S. Grewal) debate how far Guru Nanak belongs to the Sant tradition versus founding a distinct faith — present both views.

### PPSC-style asks and traps

- Work–language matching: Ramcharitmanas (Awadhi), Sursagar (Braj), Jnaneshwari (Marathi), Bijak (Hindi/Sadhukkadi), Gita Govinda (Sanskrit), Periya Puranam (Tamil).
- Kabir is called a disciple of Ramananda by tradition only; both Hindus and Muslims claimed his body (Maghar legend).
- Chaitanya never wrote major texts himself (eight verses, the Shikshashtakam); the Gosvamis did.
- Sankardeva's Sattriya dance was recognised as a classical dance by Sangeet Natak Akademi in 2000.
- Do not confuse Ramanuja (Vishishtadvaita) with Ramananda (north Indian Rama bhakti).
- Mirabai is not in the Guru Granth Sahib; Jaidev, Namdev, Ravidas and Kabir are.
- Sheikh Ahmad Sirhindi is Naqshbandi, not Chishti; Bahauddin Zakariya is Suhrawardi, not Chishti.
