## Short notes

- 1947: Partition, refugee rehabilitation (land allotment on 'claims'), East Punjab with its capital first at Shimla; Gopi Chand Bhargava was the first Chief Minister.
- PEPSU (Patiala and East Punjab States Union) was formed on 15 July 1948 from 8 princely states, with Maharaja Yadavindra Singh as Rajpramukh; it merged with Punjab on 1 Nov 1956.
- Chandigarh, designed by Le Corbusier, was inaugurated in 1953 as the capital; the Rock Garden and the Capitol Complex (a UNESCO World Heritage site, 2016) are landmarks.
- Language politics: Sachar Formula (1949) and Regional Formula (1956) on Punjabi/Hindi zones; Punjabi Suba movement led by Master Tara Singh and Sant Fateh Singh.
- Punjab Reorganisation Act 1966: Haryana created on 1 Nov 1966; hill areas went to Himachal Pradesh; Chandigarh became a UT and the shared capital (based on the Hukam Singh committee and Shah Commission).
- Punjab Official Language Act 1967 made Punjabi in Gurmukhi the official language; Gurnam Singh led the first non-Congress (Akali-led) government in 1967.
- Partap Singh Kairon (CM 1956–64) laid the base of the Green Revolution: PAU Ludhiana (1962), rural electrification, consolidation of holdings; high-yielding wheat from 1966–67.
- Anandpur Sahib Resolution (1973; endorsed 1978) demanded greater state autonomy; Dharam Yudh Morcha began in 1982.
- Operation Blue Star (June 1984) at Harmandir Sahib; Indira Gandhi was assassinated (31 Oct 1984), followed by anti-Sikh violence.
- Rajiv–Longowal Accord (24 July 1985): promised Chandigarh to Punjab, a tribunal on river waters, and a commission on territorial claims; Sant Longowal was assassinated (20 Aug 1985).
- Militancy period (1980s–early 1990s): Operation Black Thunder (1986, 1988); President's Rule 1987–92; elections in 1992; CM Beant Singh was assassinated on 31 Aug 1995.
- Chief Ministers to know: Giani Zail Singh (1972–77), Parkash Singh Badal (five terms), Rajinder Kaur Bhattal (first woman CM, 1996–97), Amarinder Singh (2002–07, 2017–21), Charanjit Singh Channi (first Dalit CM, 2021–22).
- Bhagwant Mann (AAP) has been CM since 16 March 2022 (sworn in at Khatkar Kalan); AAP won 92 of 117 seats.
- Punjab Termination of Agreements Act 2004 on river waters; the Supreme Court held it invalid in 2016.
- Districts: Pathankot and Fazilka (2011); Malerkotla (2021) made 23 districts. Punjab has 117 assembly, 13 Lok Sabha and 7 Rajya Sabha seats.
- Kartarpur Corridor to Gurdwara Darbar Sahib (Pakistan) opened in Nov 2019 for Guru Nanak's 550th Prakash Purb.
- Farmers' protests (2020–21) against the three central farm laws, led largely by Punjab unions; repeal announced on 19 Nov 2021.
- Recent challenges: debt, groundwater depletion, drugs (the 'Yudh Nashian Virudh' campaign from 2025), youth emigration; the 2025 floods led to all districts being declared flood-affected.

## Punjab angle

- 1 November is Punjab Day (reorganisation of 1966); questions often contrast 1956 (PEPSU merger) and 1966 (trifurcation).

## Deep dive

### 1947–56: rehabilitation and rebuilding East Punjab

- East Punjab began with 13 districts (Ambala, Simla, Kangra, Hoshiarpur, Jalandhar, Ludhiana, Ferozepur, Amritsar, Gurdaspur, Karnal, Hisar, Rohtak and Gurgaon), with its capital at Shimla until Chandigarh was ready.
- Refugee land resettlement: evacuee Muslim land in East Punjab was smaller and poorer than what Hindus and Sikhs had left in the West. The government used a 'standard acre' valuation and a graded cut (bigger claims cut more) for quasi-permanent allotment (1948–50), under Sardar Tarlok Singh as Director-General of Rehabilitation.
- Consolidation of holdings under the East Punjab Holdings (Consolidation and Prevention of Fragmentation) Act 1948 was largely completed by the 1960s, earlier than elsewhere in India. It was a precondition for tubewells and the Green Revolution.
- Institutions re-founded: Panjab University (camp offices at Solan and elsewhere, moved to Chandigarh in 1956); Khalsa College and other displaced colleges reopened; new model towns and refugee colonies were built.
- Chandigarh: the site was chosen in 1948 (chief engineer P.L. Varma). Albert Mayer drew the first plan, then Le Corbusier (from 1951) with Pierre Jeanneret, Maxwell Fry and Jane Drew. It was inaugurated on 7 Oct 1953 by President Rajendra Prasad. The Capitol Complex is part of the UNESCO Le Corbusier serial site (2016).
- Bhakra-Nangal: Nehru called it a temple of resurgent India when opening the Nangal canal (1954). The dam was completed and dedicated in 1963; the Bhakra Beas Management Board (BBMB) was set up under the 1966 Act.
- President's Rule was first used anywhere in India in Punjab (20 June 1951–April 1952), after Gopi Chand Bhargava's Congress ministry fell to factionalism.

### PEPSU (1948–56)

- The covenant was signed in May 1948 and PEPSU was inaugurated on 15 July 1948 from eight states: Patiala, Nabha, Jind, Faridkot, Kapurthala, Malerkotla, Kalsia and Nalagarh. The capital was Patiala.
- Rajpramukh: Maharaja Yadavindra Singh of Patiala; Uprajpramukh: Maharaja Jagatjit Singh of Kapurthala.
- 1952 elections produced an unstable Akali-led United Front government under Gian Singh Rarewala, followed by President's Rule (March 1953) and a Congress ministry.
- Agrarian conflict: the muzara (tenant) movement against biswedars (landlords), led by the Lal Communist Party and the Praja Mandal veterans (Kishangarh clash, 1949), led to the PEPSU Tenancy and Agricultural Lands Act (1955).
- Following the States Reorganisation Commission, PEPSU merged into Punjab on 1 Nov 1956 (States Reorganisation Act 1956). The Regional Formula was adopted at the same time.
- Traps: PEPSU (1948) is not the same as the Punjab Riyasti Praja Mandal (1928). The 1956 merger is not the 1966 reorganisation.

### The Punjabi Suba movement: phases

- Sachar Formula (Oct 1949): Punjabi (Gurmukhi) and Hindi zones for the medium of school instruction, with the other language compulsory from a fixed class.
- Census campaigns (1951, 1961): Arya Samaj and Hindu organisations urged Punjabi Hindus to return Hindi as their mother tongue, which undermined the linguistic case for a Punjabi-speaking state.
- The States Reorganisation Commission (1955) rejected a Punjabi Suba, citing the absence of a clear linguistic divide and the lack of general support.
- 1955 morcha: the government banned the Punjabi Suba slogan, police entered the Darbar Sahib complex (4 July 1955) and thousands courted arrest. The ban was lifted.
- Regional Formula (1956): regional committees of the legislature for the Punjabi and Hindi regions. The Akalis briefly joined the Congress.
- 1960–61: Sant Fateh Singh fasted (Dec 1960–Jan 1961), then Master Tara Singh (Aug–Oct 1961, broken without result, for which a Panj Pyare court punished him). The Das Commission (1962) found no discrimination against Sikhs.
- Sant Fateh Singh took over the Akali Dal (1962). In 1965 he threatened a fast and self-immolation, then postponed it during the Indo-Pak war, and Sikh troops' role strengthened the case.
- 1966: the Parliamentary Consultative Committee under Speaker Hukam Singh reported in March 1966 for a Punjabi-speaking state. The Shah Boundary Commission (J.C. Shah, Subimal Dutt, M.M. Philip) reported on 31 May 1966; its majority put Kharar tehsil, including Chandigarh, in Haryana, but the Centre made Chandigarh a Union Territory.
- Punjab Reorganisation Act 1966: appointed day 1 Nov 1966. Haryana was created (Bhagwat Dayal Sharma first CM); hill areas (Kangra, Kullu, Lahaul-Spiti, Shimla, Nalagarh, Una) went to Himachal Pradesh; Chandigarh became a UT and joint capital; there is a common High Court; BBMB (s.79) and Panjab University (an inter-state body corporate) were shared.
- Unfinished issues: Chandigarh, Punjabi-speaking villages left outside Punjab, and river waters. Darshan Singh Pheruman died fasting for these demands (27 Oct 1969). Indira Gandhi's award (Jan 1970) gave Chandigarh to Punjab within five years and the Abohar–Fazilka belt to Haryana; it was never implemented.

### Politics 1967–1980 and the Anandpur Sahib Resolution

- 1967: a non-Congress United Front (Akali Dal, Jana Sangh, communists and others) under Gurnam Singh. Lachhman Singh Gill defected (Nov 1967) and formed a short-lived ministry with Congress support; the Punjab Official Language Act 1967 was passed in this period.
- Parkash Singh Badal first became CM in 1970, the youngest CM of the time. Giani Zail Singh (Congress, 1972–77) promoted Sikh heritage projects such as the Guru Gobind Singh Marg (1973).
- During the Emergency (1975–77) the Akali Dal ran a sustained 'Save Democracy' morcha with mass arrests. Badal returned as CM in 1977 with Janata support.
- Anandpur Sahib Resolution: after its 1972 poll defeat, the Akali Dal set up a 12-member sub-committee (11 Dec 1972) including Surjit Singh Barnala and Gurcharan Singh Tohra, advised by Sirdar Kapur Singh. The working committee adopted the resolution at Anandpur Sahib on 16–17 Oct 1973 (Encyclopaedia of Sikhism).
- It was endorsed as a series of resolutions at the 18th All-India Akali Conference, Ludhiana (28–29 Oct 1978, presided over by Jagdev Singh Talwandi).
- Main demands: restructured Centre–state relations, with the Centre limited to defence, foreign affairs, currency, communications and railways and residual powers to the states; Chandigarh and Punjabi-speaking areas to Punjab; control of river headworks; an all-India Gurdwara Act; protection of Punjabi in neighbouring states; and more Sikh recruitment to the army.
- Controversy: rival drafts and interpretations; critics read it as secessionist, while Akalis presented it as federalism. The 1985 Accord sent it to the Sarkaria Commission (set up 1983).
- 13 April 1978: the Akali–Nirankari clash at Amritsar (13 killed) began the rise of Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale of the Damdami Taksal. The Nirankari chief Gurbachan Singh was killed in 1980.

### Crisis decade 1980–1995: chronology

- 1981–83: the Dharam Yudh Morcha (from 4 Aug 1982, under Longowal) absorbed the earlier 'Nehar Roko' (SYL) agitation. Indira Gandhi laid the SYL foundation at Kapoori (8 April 1982). Lala Jagat Narain was killed (1981) and DIG A.S. Atwal at the Golden Temple (April 1983). President's Rule came in Oct 1983 after the Dhilwan bus killings.
- Operation Blue Star (1–8 June 1984; main assault 5–6 June) was led by Maj-Gen K.S. Brar under Gen A.S. Vaidya, with Lt-Gen K. Sundarji commanding the operation. Bhindranwale, Gen. Shabeg Singh (retd) and Amrik Singh were killed; the Akal Takht was damaged and the Sikh Reference Library burned. Operation Woodrose followed in the countryside.
- Reactions: mutinies by some Sikh soldiers; Khushwant Singh returned his Padma Bhushan; Amarinder Singh resigned from the Lok Sabha and the Congress. Indira Gandhi was assassinated on 31 Oct 1984, followed by anti-Sikh violence in Delhi and elsewhere (later the Nanavati Commission, 2000–05).
- Rajiv–Longowal Accord (24 July 1985): Chandigarh to go to Punjab (by 26 Jan 1986) in exchange for Hindi-speaking areas to Haryana, to be identified by a commission; a tribunal (Eradi) on the Ravi–Beas waters; the SYL canal to be completed by 15 Aug 1986; the Anandpur Sahib Resolution referred to the Sarkaria Commission; an all-India Gurdwara Bill; compensation, rehabilitation of army deserters and army recruitment by merit.
- Longowal was assassinated at Sherpur on 20 Aug 1985. The Akali Dal won the Sept 1985 elections and Surjit Singh Barnala became CM. The Mathew, Venkataramiah and Desai commissions failed to settle the territorial exchange, and the Chandigarh transfer lapsed.
- Operation Black Thunder I (30 April 1986, under Barnala) and II (May 1988, DGP K.P.S. Gill). President's Rule ran from 11 May 1987 to Feb 1992, extended by constitutional amendments; the 59th Amendment (1988) allowed an emergency in Punjab on 'internal disturbance' and was undone by the 63rd (1989).
- Elections in Feb 1992 were boycotted by the main Akali factions (turnout about 24%) and Beant Singh became CM. Militancy was largely ended by 1993. Beant Singh was assassinated on 31 Aug 1995.

### River waters, SYL and Chandigarh: figures to remember

- Indus Waters Treaty (1960): the Ravi, Beas and Sutlej went to India. Disputes since then concern sharing the Ravi–Beas surplus among the riparian and non-riparian states.
- 1955 inter-state agreement: of 15.85 MAF of Ravi–Beas surplus, Rajasthan got 8.0, Punjab 7.2 (including PEPSU's 1.3) and J&K 0.65.
- 1976 central notification (after reorganisation): Punjab 3.5 MAF, Haryana 3.5 MAF and Delhi 0.2 out of undivided Punjab's 7.2.
- 31 Dec 1981 agreement (surplus re-estimated at 17.17 MAF): Punjab 4.22, Haryana 3.5, Rajasthan 8.6, J&K 0.65 and Delhi 0.2. The SYL was to carry Haryana's share.
- SYL canal: 214 km in total (122 km in Punjab, 92 km in Haryana). Haryana completed its part; Punjab's part was abandoned during militancy (engineers were killed in 1990).
- Eradi Tribunal (set up 1986, report 1987): Punjab 5.00 MAF and Haryana 3.83 MAF. The final report is still pending.
- Punjab Termination of Agreements Act (12 July 2004) annulled all water agreements. On a Presidential reference, the Supreme Court held it unconstitutional (Nov 2016). Punjab also passed a 2016 law returning acquired SYL land to owners.
- Chandigarh: a UT since 1966; since 1984 the Governor of Punjab has also been its Administrator. Both states claim it as capital.

### Green Revolution in Punjab: drivers, data and costs

- Foundations: consolidation of holdings, rural electrification and link roads under Partap Singh Kairon, Bhakra irrigation, and the Intensive Agricultural District Programme (1960–61, with Ludhiana among the first seven districts).
- Institutions: Punjab Agricultural University, Ludhiana (1962, on the US land-grant model; M.S. Randhawa later its Vice-Chancellor); the Agricultural Prices Commission and FCI (both 1965) for MSP and procurement; regulated mandis under the Punjab Agricultural Produce Markets Act 1961.
- Technology: dwarf Mexican wheats tested from 1965–66 (Lerma Rojo, Sonora 64), Indian selections Kalyan Sona and PV-18, then high-yield rice (IR-8 and later PR varieties), with fertiliser, tubewells, tractors and credit.
- Results: Punjab, with about 1.5% of India's area, long supplied the largest share of central-pool wheat and a large share of rice. Wheat output roughly trebled between 1960–61 and 1970–71.
- Social effects: the strengthening of Jat peasant proprietors, migrant labour from UP and Bihar, and the rise of farmer unions (Bharatiya Kisan Union in Punjab from 1980).
- Costs: a wheat–paddy monoculture; falling water tables (most blocks over-exploited); the Punjab Preservation of Subsoil Water Act 2009, which delays paddy transplanting; stubble burning; pesticide-linked health concerns in the Malwa 'cancer belt'; rural debt and farmer suicides.
- Policy responses: the Johl Committee reports on diversification (1986, 2002), crop-diversification and direct-seeded rice incentives, and the MSP and procurement debates leading to the 2020–21 farm-law protests.
