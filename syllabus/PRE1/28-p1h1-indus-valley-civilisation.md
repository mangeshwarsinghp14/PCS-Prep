## Short notes

- Called the Harappan Civilisation after Harappa (on the Ravi, now in Pakistani Punjab), the first site excavated, by Daya Ram Sahni in 1921.
- Mohenjo-daro (on the Indus, Sindh) was excavated by R.D. Banerji in 1922; John Marshall was then Director-General of the ASI.
- A Bronze Age culture; mature phase c. 2600–1900 BCE, with early and late phases stretching the wider span to c. 3300–1300 BCE.
- Extent: Sutkagendor (Baluchistan) in the west to Alamgirpur (Meerut, UP) in the east; Manda (J&K) in the north to Daimabad (Maharashtra) in the south.
- Town planning: grid-pattern streets, a raised citadel (usually west) and a lower town, standardised burnt bricks (ratio 1:2:4) and covered drains.
- Mohenjo-daro: Great Bath, great granary, assembly hall, bronze 'dancing girl', steatite 'priest-king' bust and the Pashupati seal.
- Lothal (Gujarat): dockyard and bead-making; Kalibangan (Rajasthan, on the Ghaggar): ploughed field and fire altars; Chanhudaro: the only city without a citadel.
- Dholavira (Kutch): elaborate water reservoirs, a large 'signboard' inscription, three-part town; declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2021.
- Rakhigarhi (Haryana) is the largest known Harappan site in India; Banawali (Haryana) yielded a clay plough model.
- Crops: wheat, barley, peas, sesame and cotton (Harappans were among the earliest cotton growers); rice traces at Lothal and Rangpur.
- Animals: cattle, buffalo, sheep, goats domesticated; the horse is not clearly attested; the humped bull is a common seal motif.
- Metals: copper, bronze, gold, silver, lead were known; iron was unknown.
- Trade with Mesopotamia: Indus seals found there; Mesopotamian texts mention 'Meluhha' (believed to be the Indus region), with Dilmun and Magan as intermediate points.
- Script: pictographic, about 400 signs, written mostly right to left, still undeciphered; seals mostly square steatite, the 'unicorn' being the commonest motif.
- Standardised cubical chert weights followed a binary system (1, 2, 4, 8 …) and multiples of 16.
- Religion (inferred): Mother Goddess figurines, 'proto-Shiva' Pashupati seal, linga-yoni and tree (pipal) worship, fire altars; no temples found.
- Decline theories: Aryan invasion (Wheeler, now rejected), floods, shifting rivers and drying of the Ghaggar-Hakra, climate aridity, collapse of trade — likely a combination.

## Punjab angle

- Ropar (Rupnagar) on the Sutlej was excavated by Y.D. Sharma in the 1950s, the first Harappan site excavated after Independence; a man buried with his dog was found here.
- Sanghol (Fatehgarh Sahib) has late Harappan levels below its later Buddhist/Kushana remains; Kotla Nihang Khan and Bara near Ropar are other Punjab sites.
- Harappa itself lies in Sahiwal district of West (Pakistani) Punjab, on an old course of the Ravi.

## Deep dive

### Discovery and excavation timeline (year — event)

- 1826 — Charles Masson notes the mounds of Harappa (published 1842), the first written report of the site.
- 1853–56 — Alexander Cunningham visits Harappa; railway contractors (Lahore–Multan line, 1856) carry away its bricks as track ballast.
- 1872–73 — Cunningham digs at Harappa and publishes the first Indus seal (1875), but takes it to be foreign.
- 1921 — Daya Ram Sahni excavates Harappa; 1922 — R.D. Banerji excavates Mohenjo-daro.
- 20 September 1924 — John Marshall, Director-General of ASI, announces a new civilisation in the Illustrated London News.
- 1931 — Marshall's three-volume report 'Mohenjo-daro and the Indus Civilization' appears; Ernest Mackay and M.S. Vats continue work at Mohenjo-daro and Harappa.
- 1946 — Mortimer Wheeler excavates the Harappa citadel defences and builds his 'Aryan invasion' explanation of the decline.
- 1953–56 — Y.D. Sharma excavates Ropar (Rupnagar) on the Sutlej — the first Harappan site dug in independent India.
- 1955–60 — S.R. Rao excavates Lothal; 1960–69 — B.B. Lal and B.K. Thapar excavate Kalibangan (site identified by A. Ghosh in 1953).
- 1967–68 — J.P. Joshi locates Dholavira; R.S. Bisht excavates it from 1990; 1974–77 — Bisht excavates Banawali (Haryana).
- 1997–2000 — Amarendra Nath (ASI) excavates Rakhigarhi; 2013–16 — Vasant Shinde (Deccan College) resumes work; 2019 — first ancient-DNA study of a Rakhigarhi burial published.
- 2020 — Union Budget names Rakhigarhi among five 'iconic' archaeological sites to be developed with on-site museums.
- July 2021 — Dholavira inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site (India's 40th), the first Harappan site in India on the list.

### Phases and chronology — beyond 'mature Harappa'

- Neolithic roots: Mehrgarh (Kachhi plain, Baluchistan; excavated by J.-F. Jarrige from 1974) shows farming and herding from c. 7000 BCE — the earliest in South Asia.
- Early Harappan (c. 3300–2600 BCE) regional cultures: Hakra–Ravi, Kot Diji (Sindh), Amri, Sothi–Siswal (Haryana–Rajasthan) — fortified villages, wheel-made pottery, copper.
- Mature Harappan (c. 2600–1900 BCE): standard bricks, seals, script, weights, planned towns across about 1.3 million sq km — larger than the Egyptian and Mesopotamian civilisations.
- Late Harappan / Posturban (c. 1900–1300 BCE): Cemetery-H culture (Harappa), Jhukar (Sindh), Rangpur–Lustrous Red Ware (Gujarat), Bara ware (Ropar–Sutlej area).
- Ochre Coloured Pottery (OCP) and Copper Hoard sites of the Ganga–Yamuna doab overlap in time with the late Harappan phase.
- Late Harappan and Painted Grey Ware overlap is seen at Bhagwanpura (Kurukshetra, Haryana) and at Dadheri, Nagar and Katpalon in Punjab (J.P. Joshi, 1970s).
- Gregory Possehl called the whole span the 'Indus Age'; J.M. Kenoyer prefers 'Indus Tradition' with eras: Regionalisation, Integration, Localisation.
- Radiocarbon dates from Bhirrana (Fatehabad, Haryana) have been claimed to push the earliest (Hakra) levels well before 5000 BCE — treat this as a debated claim.
- Harappan sites are named the 'Harappan culture' after the type-site convention: a culture is named after the first site where it was identified.

### Site file (site — river/location — excavator — key finds)

- Harappa — old course of the Ravi, Sahiwal (Pakistani Punjab) — D.R. Sahni 1921, M.S. Vats — granary blocks (2 rows of 6), workmen's quarters, Cemetery R-37 and Cemetery H.
- Mohenjo-daro ('mound of the dead') — Indus, Larkana (Sindh) — R.D. Banerji 1922 — Great Bath, 'granary'/great hall, 'college', bronze 'dancing girl', 'priest-king', Pashupati seal, woven cotton fragment.
- Chanhudaro — Indus, Sindh — N.G. Majumdar 1931, Mackay 1935–36 — bead factory, no citadel, footprints of a dog chasing a cat on a brick.
- Kalibangan ('black bangles') — Ghaggar, Hanumangarh (Rajasthan) — Lal and Thapar — furrowed field of the early phase, fire altars, possible earthquake damage, tiled floor.
- Lothal — Bhogava near the Gulf of Khambhat (Gujarat) — S.R. Rao — brick 'dockyard', bead factory, rice husk, Persian Gulf seal, paired burials, ivory scale.
- Dholavira — Khadir island, Rann of Kutch — R.S. Bisht — citadel, middle town, lower town; stone architecture; a chain of reservoirs; a ten-sign 'signboard'.
- Surkotada (Kutch) — J.P. Joshi 1964 — claimed horse bones (disputed); Desalpur and Bagasra (Gujarat) — shell and bead work.
- Banawali — dry Sarasvati/Ghaggar bed, Fatehabad (Haryana) — R.S. Bisht 1974–77 — terracotta plough model, radial street plan, barley.
- Rakhigarhi — Hisar (Haryana) — Amarendra Nath; Vasant Shinde — multiple mounds; largest Harappan site in India; jewellery workshop; burials used for the 2019 DNA study.
- Alamgirpur — Hindon, Meerut (UP) — Y.D. Sharma 1958 — easternmost; Manda (Chenab, Akhnoor, J&K) — northernmost in India; Daimabad (Pravara, Maharashtra) — southernmost, bronze chariot hoard.
- Shortughai (Amu Darya, Afghanistan) — trading outpost for lapis lazuli; Sutkagen-dor (Dasht river, Makran coast) — westernmost.

### Punjab and Haryana — the Harappan heartland on the Sutlej–Ghaggar plains

- Ropar (Rupnagar) — excavated by Y.D. Sharma 1953–56; a continuous sequence from Harappan through Painted Grey Ware, Northern Black Polished Ware, Saka–Kushana, Gupta to medieval levels.
- Ropar finds: a steatite seal with Indus script, copper and bronze tools, and an unusual burial of a man with a dog; the ASI site museum at Ropar opened in 1998 (Gupta-period 'Vina-vadini' plaque, Chandragupta gold coins).
- Kotla Nihang Khan (near Ropar) — Harappan and Bara-ware levels with a Bronze Age furnace; Bara (near Ropar) gave its name to 'Bara ware', a regional late/post-Harappan style.
- Sanghol (Ucha Pind, Fatehgarh Sahib) — late Harappan levels with pottery, faience bangles, terracotta cakes and 92 tiny gold beads; later famous for its Kushana stupa and sculptures.
- Dadheri (Ludhiana district), Nagar and Katpalon (Jalandhar district) — Late Harappan and Painted Grey Ware overlap sites, important for the Harappan-to-Vedic transition debate.
- Haryana: Rakhigarhi (Hisar) and Banawali, Bhirrana and Kunal (all Fatehabad) lie on the Ghaggar–Chautang system; Kunal yielded early silver ornaments.
- Mitathal (Bhiwani) and Siswal (Hisar) were excavated by Suraj Bhan (Kurukshetra University) — 'Siswal' lends its name to the Sothi–Siswal early Harappan culture.
- Farmana (Rohtak) has a large Harappan cemetery excavated by an Indo-Japanese team (2000s); Balu (Kaithal) and Bhagwanpura (Kurukshetra) are other key sites.
- Pattern to remember: most Indian Harappan sites cluster on the now-dry Ghaggar–Hakra, which many scholars identify with the Vedic Sarasvati — linking IVC geography to Punjab–Haryana.

### Economy, technology and long-distance trade

- Weights: cubical chert weights, binary in smaller units (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32…) and decimal in larger ones; the basic unit is about 13.6–14 g.
- Measures: a shell scale from Mohenjo-daro and an ivory scale from Lothal; standard brick ratio 1:2:4 used across the whole zone.
- Mesopotamian texts name Meluhha (Indus region), Dilmun (Bahrain) and Magan (Oman); Sargon of Akkad (c. 2300s BCE) boasts of Meluhhan ships docking at Akkad.
- Raw-material sources: copper from Khetri (Rajasthan) and Oman, tin from Afghanistan/Central Asia, lapis lazuli from Badakhshan, turquoise from Iran, carnelian and agate from Gujarat, gold possibly from Kolar or Afghanistan.
- Crafts: lost-wax bronze casting, faience, steatite micro-beads, long carnelian beads (traded to Ur), shell bangles; cotton spinning (spindle whorls everywhere).
- Agriculture: wheat and barley in the north, millets and rice in Gujarat (Rangpur, Lothal), double cropping suggested at Kalibangan; cotton (called 'sindon' by later Greeks).
- No hoe-and-plough evidence of iron; the Banawali clay plough and the Kalibangan furrows show ploughing with wooden ploughshares.
- Lothal 'dockyard' debate: S.R. Rao called it a tidal dock; L.S. Leshnik and others argued it was a water reservoir — useful for a 'historiography' line.

### Script, religion and society — exam-grade details

- The script has about 400 signs (I. Mahadevan's 1977 concordance lists 417), written mostly right to left; it remains undeciphered — no bilingual text exists.
- Most seals are square steatite with a one-horned 'unicorn' motif; others show bull, elephant, tiger, rhinoceros; cylinder seals are rare (Mesopotamian type).
- Pashupati seal: a seated horned figure ringed by elephant, tiger, rhinoceros and buffalo, with deer below — no lion and no cow; Marshall called it 'proto-Shiva'.
- Worship of a mother goddess (terracotta female figurines), trees (pipal), animals and phallic/yoni stones; fire altars at Kalibangan, Lothal and Banawali.
- No temple or palace has been identified — a major contrast with Egypt and Mesopotamia; rule by merchants or priest-elite is inferred, not proven.
- Burial practices: extended burials north-south (Harappa R-37), coffin burial (Harappa), paired burials (Lothal), pot burials (Surkotada), man-with-dog (Ropar).
- The Rakhigarhi DNA (2019) showed ancestry mixing Iranian-related farmers and South Asian hunter-gatherers with no Steppe component — used in the 'Aryan migration' debate.

### Decline — theories and historiography

- Aryan invasion (Mortimer Wheeler, 'Indra stands accused'): based on unburied skeletons at Mohenjo-daro; rejected after G.F. Dales' 'The Mythical Massacre at Mohenjo-daro' (1964).
- Floods (M.R. Sahni, R.L. Raikes): silt layers at Mohenjo-daro; criticised because floods were seasonal and did not affect every site.
- Tectonic uplift and river shifts: change of course of the Sutlej and Yamuna starving the Ghaggar–Hakra (Sarasvati) — the strongest explanation for the Punjab–Haryana belt.
- Climate change: the '4.2 ka event' (c. 2200 BCE) weakened monsoons; supported by lake and cave records.
- Ecological stress: deforestation for brick-firing fuel, salinity and over-grazing (W.A. Fairservis).
- Trade collapse: fall of Akkad and Ur III reduced demand from Mesopotamia; Meluhha disappears from texts after about 1800 BCE.
- Modern view: 'deurbanisation' or 'localisation', not sudden collapse — people moved east and south into Haryana, western UP and Gujarat as smaller rural cultures.

### PPSC-style asks and common traps

- River–site matching is a favourite: Harappa–Ravi, Mohenjo-daro–Indus, Ropar–Sutlej, Kalibangan–Ghaggar, Lothal–Bhogava, Alamgirpur–Hindon, Daimabad–Pravara, Banawali–dry Sarasvati.
- 'Largest site in India' = Rakhigarhi; 'UNESCO site' = Dholavira (2021); 'first site excavated after 1947' = Ropar; 'no citadel' = Chanhudaro; 'three divisions' = Dholavira.
- Iron is absent; silver, copper, bronze, gold and lead are present — any statement that Harappans used iron is false.
- The horse is not clearly attested (Surkotada bones disputed); the cow is not shown on seals; the bull is common.
- Do not confuse Kalibangan (fire altars, ploughed field) with Banawali (clay plough model) — both have 'plough' evidence of different kinds.
- Kot Diji, Amri, Mehrgarh are pre/early Harappan — not mature-phase cities.
- For Punjab-specific papers, pair Ropar with Sanghol and Kotla Nihang Khan, and remember that Harappa itself is in West Punjab.
