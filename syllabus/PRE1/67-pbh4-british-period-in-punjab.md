# British period in Punjab

## Short notes

- Annexation on 29 March 1849 (Dalhousie). Board of Administration (1849–53): Henry Lawrence (President), John Lawrence and Charles Mansel (later Robert Montgomery).
- John Lawrence became Chief Commissioner (1853) and the first Lieutenant-Governor (1859); Punjab became a Governor's province in 1921 under the Montford reforms.
- Paternalist 'Punjab school' of administration: non-regulation province, district officers with wide powers, low taxes initially, roads and canals, disarmament of the Khalsa army.
- 1857: Punjab was largely held by the British (Sikh princes and new recruits helped); mutinies at Ferozepur, Jalandhar and Sialkot; Ahmad Khan Kharal rose in the Gogera/Neeli Bar area.
- Ajnala massacre (1857): about 280 sepoys of the 26th Native Infantry were killed by Frederic Cooper and thrown into a well (Kalianwala Khooh), excavated in 2014.
- Delhi was attached to Punjab in 1858; the North-West Frontier Province was separated in 1901 (Curzon); Delhi was separated in 1911–12 when it became the capital.
- Canal colonies (from the 1880s): nine colonies in the West Punjab bars — Sidhnai, Lower Chenab (Lyallpur), Lower Jhelum, Lower Bari Doab, etc. — settled with farmers from central Punjab; Lyallpur was named after Lt Gov James Lyall.
- Canals: Upper Bari Doab Canal (1859), Sirhind Canal (1882), the Triple Canal Project (early 1900s); Punjab became the 'granary' and military recruiting ground of the Raj.
- Punjab Alienation of Land Act 1900: restricted transfer of land from 'agriculturist' to 'non-agriculturist' (moneylender) castes to curb rural indebtedness.
- Punjab Colonisation Bill (1906) and water-rate hike sparked the 1907 agitation ('Pagri Sambhal Jatta' song by Banke Dayal); Lajpat Rai and Ajit Singh were deported to Mandalay; Lord Minto vetoed the Bill.
- Education: Government College Lahore (1864), Punjab University Lahore (1882), DAV College Lahore (1886), Khalsa College Amritsar (1892).
- Press and finance: The Tribune (1881, Dyal Singh Majithia), Punjab National Bank (founded 1894, Lahore), Akhbar-i-Am; growth of a vernacular press in Urdu, Punjabi and Hindi.
- Martial races policy: heavy recruitment of Sikhs, Punjabi Muslims and Dogras; Punjab supplied a very large share of Indian troops in World War I.
- Constitutional bodies: Punjab Legislative Council (1897); dyarchy (1921); the Unionist Party (1923, Fazl-i-Husain and Chhotu Ram) represented rural landed interests.
- Under the 1935 Act, the 1937 elections were won by the Unionists: Sikander Hayat Khan became Premier (Sikander–Jinnah Pact 1937), followed by Khizr Hayat Tiwana (1942–47).
- Chhotu Ram's agrarian laws (late 1930s) gave debt relief and protected peasant land; he was called 'Deenbandhu'.
- Partition (1947): Punjab Boundary Commission under Cyril Radcliffe, members Mehr Chand Mahajan, Teja Singh, Din Muhammad, Muhammad Munir; award announced 17 Aug 1947; mass migration and violence.
- East Punjab's capital moved from Shimla to Chandigarh, planned by Le Corbusier and inaugurated in 1953.

## Punjab angle

- The canal colonies and the Land Alienation Act shaped Punjab's peasant proprietorship and later Green Revolution base.

## Deep dive

### Administrative chronology, 1849–1947

- 1849–53: Board of Administration (Henry Lawrence, John Lawrence, Charles Mansel; Robert Montgomery replaced Mansel in 1851). 1853–59: Chief Commissioner John Lawrence. 1 Jan 1859: Punjab became a Lieutenant-Governorship with John Lawrence as the first Lt-Governor.
- Lt-Governors to know: Robert Montgomery (1859–65), Donald McLeod (1865–70), Charles Aitchison (1882–87), James Lyall (1887–92; Lyallpur named after him), Denzil Ibbetson (1907–08), Michael O'Dwyer (1913–19) and Edward Maclagan (1919–21, who became the first Governor in 1921).
- Governors: Maclagan (1921–24), Malcolm Hailey (1924–28; Sikh Gurdwaras Act 1925), Herbert Emerson, Henry Craik, Bertrand Glancy and Evan Jenkins (1946–47, the last).
- Punjab was a non-regulation province: district officers (Deputy Commissioners) combined revenue, magisterial and judicial powers. It was organised in divisions, districts and tehsils, with the paternalist 'Punjab school' of the Lawrences.
- Law and courts: Punjab Civil Code (1854), Chief Court at Lahore (1866), Punjab Laws Act (1872, giving custom the force of law) and the Lahore High Court (1919).
- Boundary changes: Delhi territory added (1858); NWFP carved out (9 Nov 1901, Curzon); Delhi separated when it became the capital (1911–12).
- Legislature: Punjab Legislative Council (1897); Morley–Minto (1909) expansion; dyarchy (1921), with Fazl-i-Husain and Lala Harkishan Lal among the first ministers; provincial autonomy under the 1935 Act (assembly of 175 seats, 1937).

### Land settlement and agrarian law

- Summary settlements (1847–49) were followed by regular settlements village by village: a mahalwari or 'bhaichara' pattern with cash revenue assessed on the village and recorded in jamabandis, shajras and khasra girdawaris (crop inspections).
- The 1850s revenue demand was heavy and paid in cash, and falling grain prices caused distress. Later settlements lowered the share, aiming at about half of the net assets, then less.
- Punjab Tenancy Acts (1868, 1887) protected occupancy tenants. The Punjab Land Revenue Act (1887) consolidated the revenue law.
- Rising indebtedness and land passing to moneylenders (S.S. Thorburn's 'Musalmans and Money-Lenders in the Punjab', 1886) led to the Punjab Alienation of Land Act 1900. Land could be sold or mortgaged only within notified 'agricultural tribes'; the Act came into force in 1901.
- Punjab Pre-emption Acts (1905, 1913) gave kin and neighbours first claim on land sales.
- Co-operative credit societies (from the 1904 Act) grew fastest in Punjab; Malcolm Darling, later Registrar of Co-operative Societies, studied rural debt in 'The Punjab Peasant in Prosperity and Debt' (1925).
- Chhotu Ram's 'golden laws' (1934–39): Punjab Relief of Indebtedness Act (1934), Restitution of Mortgaged Lands Act (1938), Registration of Money-lenders Act (1938), Alienation of Land (Second Amendment) Act (1938, against benami transfers) and the Agricultural Produce Markets Act (1939).
- Effect: a legally protected peasant proprietary class, the political base of the Unionist Party. Critics say the Acts entrenched the tribal and caste categories of the Raj.

### Canals and canal colonies

- Perennial canals: Western Yamuna Canal (restored 1820s), Upper Bari Doab Canal (opened 1859, Madhopur headworks on the Ravi), Sirhind Canal (1882, from Ropar on the Sutlej) and the Triple Canal Project (Upper Jhelum, Upper Chenab and Lower Bari Doab, c.1905–17).
- The nine canal colonies in the bar (upland scrub) tracts of west Punjab: Sidhnai (1886–88), Sohag Para (1886–88), Chunian (1896–98), Chenab (1892–1905; Lyallpur), Jhelum (1902–06; Sargodha), Lower Bari Doab (1914–24; Montgomery), Upper Chenab (1915–19), Upper Jhelum (1916–21) and Nili Bar (1916–40).
- Lyallpur (now Faisalabad) was laid out with eight bazaars radiating from a clock tower like the Union Jack. Montgomery is now Sahiwal.
- Grant categories: peasant (abadkar) grants, yeoman and capitalist grants, military and horse-breeding grants, and auctions. Preference went to 'agricultural castes', so Jat Sikhs and others from overcrowded central districts (Jalandhar, Hoshiarpur, Amritsar, Ludhiana) moved west.
- Excluded or disadvantaged groups: Dalit labourers (non-agriculturist castes could not hold colony land), local pastoral Janglis, and artisans.
- Economic outcome: the canal-irrigated area grew several-fold, Punjab produced a wheat and cotton surplus for export through Karachi, and mandi towns grew. Imran Ali calls this agrarian imperialism, serving revenue, the army and loyal elites.
- Railways tied the colonies to the ports: Amritsar–Lahore (1862), Lahore–Multan (1865), Delhi–Amritsar (1870); the North Western Railway was formed in 1886.
- 1907 colony agitation: the Punjab Colonisation (Amendment) Bill (1906) tightened inheritance and fines, and Bari Doab water rates were raised. The 'Pagri Sambhal Jatta' protests followed (see pbh5), and the Viceroy Minto vetoed the Bill (1907).

### 1857, the army and the 'martial races'

- 1857 in Punjab: John Lawrence disarmed suspect sepoy regiments (Lahore, Mian Mir), raised new Punjabi and Sikh levies, and sent the Movable Column under John Nicholson to Delhi. The Phulkian chiefs and Kapurthala helped the British.
- Resistance in Punjab: mutinies at Jalandhar, Ferozepur and Sialkot; Rai Ahmad Khan Kharal's revolt in the Gogera/Ravi bar (killed Sept 1857); the Ajnala killings of sepoys of the 26th Native Infantry (Frederic Cooper).
- After 1857 the Peel Commission (1859) favoured mixing communities in regiments. Under Lord Roberts (C-in-C 1885–93) the 'martial races' doctrine made Punjab the main recruiting ground.
- Sikh regiments raised after 1846 (the 14th Ferozepur Sikhs and 15th Ludhiana Sikhs) required recruits to be Amritdhari Sikhs, which reinforced Khalsa identity.
- World War I: Punjab supplied roughly half of India's combatant recruits (O'Dwyer's recruiting drive used village quotas and pressure). This fed the post-war anger of 1919.
- Soldiers' remittances, pensions, and colony grants for military service made the army a pillar of the rural economy and of loyalism.
- Emigration: Sikhs went to Malaya, Hong Kong and Shanghai (police), then Canada and the USA (from about 1903). This background produced the Ghadar movement and the Komagata Maru episode.

### Socio-religious reform movements

- Christian missions: the American Presbyterian mission at Ludhiana (1834; printing press 1836; a Punjabi grammar and dictionary in the 1850s); Forman Christian College, Lahore (1864); conversions such as Maharaja Duleep Singh (1853) alarmed Sikh leaders.
- Sikh reform: the Nirankaris (Baba Dayal, Rawalpindi, d. 1855) opposed idol worship and promoted simple anand marriage; the Namdharis (Kukas, from 1857) are covered in pbh5.
- Singh Sabha: Amritsar (1 Oct 1873, Thakur Singh Sandhawalia and Giani Gian Singh), prompted partly by the planned conversion of Sikh students at the Amritsar mission school. Lahore Singh Sabha (2 Nov 1879; Diwan Buta Singh president, Professor Gurmukh Singh secretary), the radical Tat Khalsa wing.
- Singh Sabha institutions: Khalsa Diwan Amritsar (1883) and Lahore (1886); Khalsa College, Amritsar (foundation 1892); Khalsa Tract Society (Bhai Vir Singh, 1894) and Khalsa Samachar (1899); Chief Khalsa Diwan (1902, Sunder Singh Majithia); Sikh Educational Conference (1908); Anand Marriage Act (1909); Bhai Kahn Singh Nabha's tract 'Ham Hindu Nahin' (1898).
- Arya Samaj: Dayanand toured Punjab in 1877 and the Lahore Arya Samaj was founded (24 June 1877). DAV School/College, Lahore (1886; Hans Raj) followed. In 1893 it split into the 'College' party (Hans Raj, Lajpat Rai) and the 'Gurukul' party (Munshi Ram, later Swami Shraddhanand; Gurukul Kangri 1902). Its shuddhi (reconversion) campaigns and Lekh Ram's polemics sharpened communal boundaries.
- Other Hindu bodies: Brahmo Samaj at Lahore (1863); Dev Samaj (Shiv Narayan Agnihotri, 1887); Sanatan Dharm Sabhas; Shardha Ram Phillauri (author of 'Sikhan de Raj di Vithia', 1866).
- Muslim bodies: Anjuman-i-Himayat-i-Islam (Lahore, 1884) and Islamia College, Lahore (1892); the Ahmadiyya movement (Mirza Ghulam Ahmad, Qadian, 1889); Iqbal's Allahabad address (1930) as the political turn.
- Ad-Dharm: Mangu Ram Mugowalia, a former Ghadarite, launched a Dalit religious movement in 1925–26 (first conference at Mugowal, Hoshiarpur, June 1926) claiming that Dalits were the original inhabitants. It was recorded as a separate religion in the 1931 census, supported Ambedkar at the Round Table Conferences, and contested the 1937 elections through the Ad Dharm Mandal.
- Mains angle: these movements modernised and standardised religious identities (Harjot Oberoi's 'construction of religious boundaries'). They promoted print, schools and women's education, but also competitive communalism in Punjab.

### Education, press and institutions (founding years)

- Lahore Medical School (1860, later King Edward Medical College); Government College, Lahore (1864); Oriental College (1876); Mayo School of Arts (1875, under Lockwood Kipling); Aitchison College (Chiefs' College, 1886).
- Punjab University, Lahore (1882) was the fourth university of British India, after Calcutta, Bombay and Madras (1857). It grew out of G.W. Leitner's Anjuman-i-Punjab movement for vernacular learning.
- Community colleges: DAV College (1886), Khalsa College Amritsar (1892), Islamia College Lahore (1892) and Forman Christian College (1864).
- Press: Koh-i-Noor (Lahore, 1850, Urdu, Harsukh Rai; among the earliest Punjab papers); Civil and Military Gazette (Lahore, 1870s, where Rudyard Kipling worked); The Tribune (Lahore, 2 Feb 1881, founded by Dyal Singh Majithia); the Panjabee; the Khalsa Akhbar (Lahore, 1886, Giani Ditt Singh).
- Finance and enterprise: Punjab National Bank (registered 1894 at Lahore, opened 1895; Lajpat Rai among its founders); Dyal Singh Majithia also founded Dyal Singh College and Library.
- Language politics: Urdu was the court language, while Hindi (Arya Samaj) and Punjabi in Gurmukhi (Singh Sabha) movements competed. These were the roots of the 1950s Punjabi Suba debate.

### Provincial politics and Partition, 1920–47

- National Unionist Party (1923): Fazl-i-Husain, Chhotu Ram and later Sikandar Hayat Khan. It was a cross-communal party of the landed 'agriculturist' interest.
- 1937 elections: the Unionists won a clear majority (about 95 of 175 seats) and Sikandar Hayat became Premier. The Sikandar–Jinnah Pact (Lucknow, Oct 1937) let Unionist Muslims join the League in all-India matters.
- Khizr Hayat Tiwana became Premier (1942) after Sikandar's death. He refused Jinnah's demand to run the ministry as a League government (1944).
- 1946 elections: the Muslim League won nearly all Muslim seats (about 73–75). A Unionist–Congress–Akali coalition under Khizr governed until he resigned on 2 March 1947, followed by the Rawalpindi riots (March 1947) and Governor's rule under Evan Jenkins.
- Sikh position: Master Tara Singh opposed Pakistan, the Akali 'Azad Punjab' scheme (1943) proposed redrawn boundaries, and the Akalis rejected the Cabinet Mission plan (1946) for its grouping scheme.
- Partition procedure: under the 3 June Plan, the Punjab Assembly's West and East members voted separately (23 June 1947). The Boundary Commission under Radcliffe (members Mehr Chand Mahajan, Teja Singh, Din Muhammad and Muhammad Munir) was told to consider 'contiguous majority areas' and 'other factors'.
- Disputed awards (announced 17 Aug 1947): three tehsils of Gurdaspur (Gurdaspur, Batala, Pathankot) went to India and Shakargarh to Pakistan; the Ferozepur and Zira tehsils stayed in India; Lahore went to Pakistan.
- Human cost: the Punjab Boundary Force (Maj-Gen T.W. Rees) failed and was wound up on 1 Sept 1947. About 10 million people crossed the Punjab border and several hundred thousand died. The Partition Museum (Town Hall, Amritsar, 2017) records this.
