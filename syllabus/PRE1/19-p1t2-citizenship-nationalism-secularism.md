## Short notes

- Citizenship = full and equal membership of a political community with rights and obligations; T.H. Marshall ('Citizenship and Social Class', 1950) described civil, political and social rights of citizenship.
- Constitution Part II (Arts 5-11) dealt with citizenship at commencement (26 Jan 1950); Art 11 lets Parliament regulate acquisition and termination - exercised through the Citizenship Act, 1955.
- Citizenship Act 1955 modes of acquisition: birth, descent, registration, naturalisation and incorporation of territory; loss by renunciation, termination and deprivation.
- Jus soli (by birth on soil) vs jus sanguinis (by blood); India has moved towards jus sanguinis - born after 1 July 1987 needs one citizen parent; after 3 Dec 2004, one citizen parent and the other not an illegal migrant.
- India has single citizenship (from Britain) unlike the dual federal-state citizenship of the USA; Overseas Citizenship of India (OCI) is not dual citizenship and gives no voting rights.
- Rights only for citizens: Arts 15, 16, 19, 29 and 30; voting and contesting elections; holding offices such as President, Vice-President, judges of SC/HC, Governor, Attorney General.
- Citizenship (Amendment) Act 2019: Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi and Christian migrants from Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan who entered on or before 31 Dec 2014 made eligible; naturalisation residence cut from 11 to 5 years; Rules notified March 2024.
- Nationalism: a shared sense of belonging to a nation built on common history, territory, shared political ideals and identity; Benedict Anderson called nations 'imagined communities'.
- National self-determination claims a state for each nation (Woodrow Wilson after WWI); since nations and states rarely coincide, protecting minorities becomes essential.
- Indian nationalism grew as an anti-colonial, civic and inclusive force; Rabindranath Tagore warned against aggressive nationalism and placed humanity above it.
- Secularism: separation of religion and state, freedom of religion, and equality of all religions; Western model (strict mutual exclusion, e.g. US 'wall of separation') vs Indian model.
- Indian secularism follows 'principled distance' (Rajeev Bhargava) - equal respect for all faiths, while the state may intervene to reform practices (e.g. abolishing untouchability, temple entry).
- Words 'Socialist' and 'Secular' were added to the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment (1976); S.R. Bommai (1994) held secularism part of the basic structure; in Nov 2024 the Supreme Court dismissed petitions challenging these words.
- Arts 25-28 freedom of religion: Art 25(2) allows the state to regulate secular activities linked to religion and open Hindu religious institutions to all classes; Art 28 bars religious instruction in fully state-funded institutions.
- Arts 29-30 protect cultural and educational rights of minorities; Art 44 (DPSP) calls for a Uniform Civil Code - Goa has a Portuguese-era common civil code and Uttarakhand enacted a UCC in 2024.

## Punjab angle

- Punjab's large diaspora (Canada, UK, USA, Australia) makes OCI, dual-citizenship demands and NRI issues politically important in the state.
- Sikhs from Afghanistan and Pakistan are among the communities covered by the CAA 2019; many such migrants live in Punjab and Delhi.
- The Punjabi Suba movement (leading to the 1966 reorganisation) is an example of linguistic-cultural identity working within Indian nationalism.

## Deep dive

### Citizenship law — timeline (year — change)

- 1950 — Arts 5–8 decided citizenship at commencement; Art 6 covered migrants from Pakistan (before or after 19 July 1948); Art 7 excluded those who migrated to Pakistan after 1 March 1947 unless they returned on a resettlement permit.
- 1985 — Sec 6A added after the Assam Accord: entrants before 1 Jan 1966 deemed citizens; those entering from 1 Jan 1966 to before 25 March 1971 could become citizens after 10 years of registration as foreigners.
- 2003 — "illegal migrant" defined; Sec 14A allowed compulsory registration (NRC); Overseas Citizenship introduced (operational 2005); PIO cards merged into OCI in 2015.
- 2005 — Sarbananda Sonowal case struck down the IMDT Act 1983 for making detection of foreigners in Assam nearly impossible.
- 2019 — Assam NRC final list (31 Aug) left out about 19 lakh applicants; CAA 2019 passed (Sec 6B).
- 2024 — CAA Rules notified (11 March); on 17 Oct 2024 a 5-judge bench upheld Sec 6A by 4:1.
- 2025 — Immigration and Foreigners Act 2025 (in force 1 Sept 2025) replaced the Passport (Entry into India) Act 1920, Registration of Foreigners Act 1939, Foreigners Act 1946 and the Immigration (Carriers' Liability) Act 2000.
- Sept 2025 — an exemption order let persecuted minorities from Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan who entered up to 31 Dec 2024 stay without passport/visa penalties; the CAA citizenship cut-off still stays 31 Dec 2014.
- Sec 9 — voluntary acquisition of another country's citizenship ends Indian citizenship; India has no dual citizenship.
- OCI cardholders get a lifelong multi-entry visa but cannot vote, hold constitutional office or buy agricultural land; the 2021 notification requires permission for research, journalism and missionary work.

### Nationalism — theories and Indian debates

- Ernest Renan, "What is a Nation?" (1882): a nation is a "daily plebiscite" — shared memory and the will to live together.
- Ernest Gellner (1983): nationalism is a product of industrial society and mass education; Eric Hobsbawm: "invented traditions"; Anthony D. Smith: ethno-symbolism, nations grow from older ethnic communities.
- Hans Kohn (1944): civic (Western, voluntary) vs ethnic (Eastern, organic) nationalism.
- J.S. Mill (Representative Government, 1861, ch. 16): boundaries of government should broadly coincide with those of nationality.
- Jurgen Habermas: "constitutional patriotism" — loyalty to constitutional values rather than ethnicity; close to Ambedkar's idea of constitutional morality.
- Gandhi's Hind Swaraj (1909): swaraj as self-rule and moral regeneration, not just transfer of power.
- V.D. Savarkar's Hindutva (1923): cultural nationalism; contrasted with the composite nationalism of Maulana Azad and Nehru's "unity in diversity" (Discovery of India, 1946).
- Two-nation theory: Iqbal's Allahabad address (1930), Lahore Resolution (1940); Ambedkar analysed it in Thoughts on Pakistan (1940).
- Symbols and law: Bijoe Emmanuel (1986) — no one can be compelled to sing the anthem; the 2016 order making the anthem compulsory in cinemas was made optional in Jan 2018.
- Vande Mataram (Bankim Chandra, written 1875) completed 150 years in 2025; the government marked the anniversary from Nov 2025.
- Punjab contributions: Kuka (Namdhari) movement under Ram Singh (1872 Malerkotla executions), Ghadar Party (1913), Jallianwala Bagh (1919), Bhagat Singh and Udham Singh.

### Secularism in court (case — year — principle)

- Shirur Mutt (1954) — "essential religious practices" test; religion includes rituals, not only belief.
- Sri Venkataramana Devaru (1958) — temple-entry law under Art 25(2)(b) prevails over a denomination's claim, with limited exceptions.
- Rev. Stainislaus v State of MP (1977) — right to propagate does not include a right to convert; anti-conversion laws upheld.
- Ismail Faruqui (1994) — a mosque is not essential to Islam for offering namaz; acquisition allowed.
- Aruna Roy (2002) — teaching about religions is not "religious instruction" barred by Art 28.
- M. Siddiq (Ayodhya, 9 Nov 2019) — praised the Places of Worship Act 1991 as protecting secularism; in Dec 2024 the SC stopped new suits and survey orders while the Act's challenge is heard.
- Indian Young Lawyers (Sabarimala, 2018, 4:1) — women of all ages may enter; review petitions sent to a 9-judge bench (2019) on essential practices — still pending.
- Aishat Shifa (Hijab, Oct 2022) — split verdict; referred to a larger bench.
- Art 290A — annual payments to Devaswom funds in Kerala and Tamil Nadu: an example of the Indian state's engagement, not separation.
- Art 27: no tax for promotion of any religion (a fee for services is allowed); Art 28(2): institutions under a trust may impart religious instruction even if state-run; Art 28(3): consent needed in aided institutions.

### Comparative models and critics

- France — laïcité (1905 law): strict separation and a religion-free public sphere (e.g. headscarf ban in schools, 2004).
- USA — First Amendment's establishment clause; Jefferson's "wall of separation" (1802 letter); Everson (1947) applied it to states.
- UK — an established Church of England with liberal toleration; shows establishment and freedom can co-exist.
- India — Gandhi's sarva dharma sambhava (equal respect), Bhargava's "principled distance" (contextual secularism).
- Critics: T.N. Madan ("Secularism in its place", 1987) — secularism is alien to South Asian faith traditions; Ashis Nandy — anti-secularist manifesto; Partha Chatterjee — politics of toleration.
- Citizenship theorists beyond Marshall: Will Kymlicka (Multicultural Citizenship, 1995) — group-differentiated rights; Iris Marion Young — differentiated citizenship.
- India is not party to the 1951 Refugee Convention or its 1967 Protocol, nor the 1954 and 1961 statelessness conventions.
- Uniform Civil Code: Goa's Portuguese Civil Code (1867); Uttarakhand UCC in force 27 Jan 2025; Gujarat passed its UCC Bill on 24 March 2026, becoming the second state; the 21st Law Commission (2018) called a UCC "neither necessary nor desirable" at that stage.

### Punjab specifics

- Kartarpur Corridor (opened 9 Nov 2019, Guru Nanak's 550th birth anniversary) — visa-free pilgrimage to Gurdwara Darbar Sahib, Pakistan; suspended by India in May 2025 amid hostilities.
- Malerkotla (district since 2021) honours Nawab Sher Mohammad Khan's "haa da naara" (1705) protest against the execution of the younger Sahibzadas — a living symbol of composite heritage.
- Anand Marriage Act 1909 (amended 2012 to allow registration of Sikh marriages) — a community-specific personal-law statute.
- Sikh Gurdwaras Act 1925 created the SGPC — an elected religious body under state law; general house elections were last held in 2011.
- Constitution (Scheduled Castes) Order 1950 first limited SC status to Hindus; Sikh SCs were included in 1956 (Buddhists in 1990) — vital for Punjab's Mazhabi and Ramdasia Sikhs.
- Punjab's diaspora (Canada, UK, USA) makes OCI rules, NRI marriages and the India–Canada diplomatic strains of 2023–24 politically important.
- Partition 1947 divided Punjab along the Radcliffe Line; refugee rehabilitation shaped East Punjab's identity and later the Punjabi Suba demand.

### Traps

- Citizenship by incorporation of territory: Goa (1961–62), Puducherry (1962), Sikkim (1975).
- Termination (automatic, on taking foreign citizenship) differs from deprivation (government order for fraud, disloyalty, etc.).
- Part II has Arts 5–11 but no Article defines "citizen"; the Act does the work under Art 11.
- "Secular" is not defined anywhere in the Constitution; it was added by the 42nd Amendment, but secular features (Arts 25–30) existed from 1950.
- CAA does not apply to tribal areas under the Sixth Schedule or to areas under the Inner Line Permit system.
- NRC (Assam) was supervised by the Supreme Court; NPR is under the Citizenship Rules 2003 — do not confuse the two.
