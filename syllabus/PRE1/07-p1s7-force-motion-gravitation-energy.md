## Short notes

- Newton's first law (inertia): a body stays at rest or in uniform motion unless an external force acts; examples - passengers jerk backward when a bus starts, dust leaves a beaten carpet.
- Newton's second law: force = rate of change of momentum = mass x acceleration (F = ma); SI unit newton (kg m/s squared); 1 kgf = 9.8 N.
- Newton's third law: action and reaction are equal and opposite and act on different bodies - rocket propulsion, recoil of a gun, swimming, walking.
- Momentum p = mv; total momentum is conserved when no external force acts (gun-bullet recoil, rocket).
- Impulse = force x time; seat belts, airbags, sand pits and a cricketer pulling hands back increase impact time and so reduce force.
- Universal law of gravitation: F = G m1 m2 / r squared; G = 6.67 x 10^-11 N m^2/kg^2 is a universal constant (first measured by Cavendish).
- Acceleration due to gravity g is about 9.8 m/s^2 at Earth's surface; it is greater at the poles than at the equator and decreases with height and with depth, becoming zero at the centre.
- Mass is constant everywhere; weight (mg) changes - an object weighs about one-sixth on the Moon.
- In vacuum all bodies fall with the same acceleration (Galileo); astronauts in orbit feel weightless because they and their craft are in continuous free fall.
- Escape velocity from Earth is about 11.2 km/s; orbital velocity near the surface about 7.9 km/s; geostationary satellites orbit about 35,786 km above the equator with a 24-hour period.
- Kepler's laws: planets move in ellipses with the Sun at one focus; equal areas in equal times; square of period proportional to cube of mean distance.
- Pressure = force/area (pascal); sharp knives and pins concentrate force; liquid pressure increases with depth, hence dams are thicker at the base.
- Archimedes' principle: a body immersed in a fluid experiences an upward buoyant force equal to the weight of fluid displaced.
- A body floats if its density is less than the fluid's; ships float because their shape displaces water equal to their weight; floating is easier in sea water (denser), easiest in the Dead Sea; ice floats on water.
- Applications of buoyancy: hydrometer, lactometer (milk purity), submarine ballast tanks, hot-air balloons.
- Work = force x displacement (joule); power = work/time (watt); 1 horsepower = 746 W; commercial energy unit 1 kWh = 3.6 x 10^6 J.
- Kinetic energy = 1/2 mv^2 (doubling speed quadruples KE); potential energy = mgh (water stored in a dam, raised hammer, stretched bow has elastic PE).
- Law of conservation of energy: energy changes form but total stays constant; in free fall PE converts to KE with total mechanical energy constant (ignoring air resistance).

## Deep dive

### Motion and laws — everyday applications asked

- Uniform circular motion has constant speed but changing velocity, so it needs a centripetal force — friction for a turning car, tension for a whirled stone; roads and rail curves are banked so vehicles do not skid.
- 'Centrifugal force' is a pseudo-force felt in a rotating frame; centrifuges use it in cream separators, washing-machine spin dryers and uranium enrichment.
- Friction: static > sliding > rolling; ball bearings and lubricants reduce it, tyre treads increase grip, and anti-lock brakes (ABS) stop wheels locking so the car can still be steered.
- Inertia of direction: sparks from a grinding wheel fly off along the tangent; inertia of motion: long-jumpers run up before jumping.
- Rockets and jet engines work by Newton's third law; a rocket does not need air to push against, so it works in space.
- Conservation of angular momentum: a spinning skater or dancer speeds up by pulling the arms in; planets move faster when nearer the Sun (Kepler's second law).
- Projectiles: without air resistance the range is greatest at a 45-degree launch angle; a bullet fired horizontally and one dropped from the same height reach the ground together.
- Apparent weight in a lift increases while accelerating upward, decreases while accelerating downward and becomes zero in free fall.
- Levers: class I (see-saw, scissors), class II (nutcracker, wheelbarrow), class III (tongs, human forearm); a longer spanner gives more torque.
- Stability needs a low centre of gravity and a vertical line through it falling within the base — racing cars are low; the Leaning Tower of Pisa still stands for this reason.

### Gravitation and space applications

- Newton's Principia (1687) set out the law of gravitation; Cavendish measured G with a torsion balance (1798).
- Surface gravity: Moon about 1.62 m/s2 (one-sixth of Earth's), Jupiter about 25 m/s2; on Earth g is about 9.83 at the poles and 9.78 at the equator because of rotation and the equatorial bulge.
- Orbits: low Earth orbit (ISS at about 400 km), sun-synchronous polar orbits of 600–800 km for imaging (PSLV's speciality), GPS at about 20,200 km, geostationary orbit at 35,786 km for communication and weather satellites.
- The Moon's escape velocity is only about 2.4 km/s, so it cannot hold an atmosphere.
- Aditya-L1 orbits the Sun–Earth L1 Lagrange point about 1.5 million km from Earth (halo orbit reached on 6 January 2024), where the pulls of Sun and Earth balance the orbit.
- Mangalyaan (2013) used repeated orbit-raising burns and a transfer orbit to reach Mars cheaply; spacecraft also use 'gravity assist' from planets.
- Tides are caused mainly by the Moon's differential pull; spring tides occur at new and full moon, neap tides at the quarter phases.
- Astronauts feel weightless because they are in continuous free fall; gravity at 400 km is still about 90% of its surface value.
- Gravitational waves were first detected by LIGO in 2015 (Nobel 2017); LIGO-India at Aundha in Hingoli, Maharashtra, was approved in 2023.
- Chandrayaan-3's lander touched down on 23 August 2023 near 69 degrees south; the Pragyan rover confirmed sulphur on the lunar surface.

### Fluids, pressure and buoyancy — applications

- Sea-level air pressure is about 101.3 kPa (76 cm of mercury), first measured with Torricelli's barometer (1643); it falls with height, so aircraft cabins are pressurised.
- Pascal's law: hydraulic lifts, hydraulic brakes, hydraulic presses and earth-movers multiply force.
- Bernoulli's principle: aircraft wings get lift, roofs are blown off in storms, sprayers and carburettors work, and two boats moving side by side are drawn together.
- Viscosity and terminal velocity explain why raindrops and parachutists fall at a steady speed (Stokes' law).
- Surface tension lets insects walk on water and makes drops round; soap lowers it. Capillary action draws oil up a lamp wick and water through soil and blotting paper.
- About 90% of an iceberg is under water because ice (density about 0.92 g/cm3) is only slightly lighter than sea water; the Plimsoll line on ships marks safe loading in different waters.
- Submarines dive or surface by filling or emptying ballast tanks; hot-air balloons rise because hot air is less dense.
- Hydrometers measure relative density; a lactometer detects water added to milk.
- Water is densest at 4 C, so lakes freeze from the top and fish survive in the water below.

### Work, energy and power — numbers and India's energy angle

- Energy conversions: dynamo (mechanical to electrical), solar cell (light to electrical), battery (chemical to electrical), loudspeaker (electrical to sound), photosynthesis (light to chemical).
- Units: 1 kWh = 3.6 MJ; 1 calorie = 4.186 J; 1 electron-volt = 1.6 x 10^-19 J; 1 horsepower = 746 W.
- Einstein's E = mc2 (1905) explains the energy released when nuclei split or fuse (mass defect).
- Braking distance rises with the square of speed, so doubling speed roughly quadruples braking distance — the physics behind speed limits.
- Regenerative braking in EVs, metros and Vande Bharat trains turns kinetic energy back into electricity.
- Wind power varies with the cube of wind speed, which is why turbine siting matters; pumped-storage hydro stores energy as water's potential energy.
- India's non-fossil sources crossed 50% of installed electricity capacity in 2025, five years ahead of its 2030 Paris pledge; the target is 500 GW non-fossil capacity by 2030 and net zero by 2070.
- No machine is 100% efficient; perpetual-motion machines are impossible under the laws of thermodynamics.

### Units, instruments and scientists

- Seven SI base units: metre, kilogram, second, ampere, kelvin, mole, candela. Since 20 May 2019 the kilogram is defined using Planck's constant, not a metal cylinder.
- CSIR-National Physical Laboratory, New Delhi, keeps Indian Standard Time and the national measurement standards.
- Astronomical distances: light year (about 9.46 x 10^15 m), astronomical unit (about 150 million km), parsec (about 3.26 light years).
- Instruments: anemometer (wind speed), manometer (gas pressure), sphygmomanometer (blood pressure), seismograph, altimeter, hygrometer (humidity), pyrometer (very high temperatures).
- Pairs: Galileo (inertia, free fall), Archimedes (buoyancy), Pascal (fluid pressure), Torricelli (barometer), Bernoulli (fluid flow), Hooke (elasticity), Kepler (planetary laws), James Watt (steam engine).
- Aryabhata (499 CE) held that the Earth rotates on its axis; Bhaskara II (12th century) wrote of the Earth's attractive power.

### Punjab links

- The Bhakra Dam on the Sutlej (managed by BBMB) turns the potential energy of the Gobind Sagar reservoir into hydroelectricity.
- Ranjit Sagar (Thein) Dam on the Ravi generates hydropower for Punjab; the Shahpurkandi project downstream was completed in 2024 to use Ravi water that earlier flowed into Pakistan.
- Punjab's Sadak Suraksha Force (2024) patrols highways; momentum and kinetic energy explain why speeding and not wearing seat belts or helmets turn crashes fatal.
- Pushpa Gujral Science City, Kapurthala, has a space and aviation gallery with a PSLV display and a digital planetarium for revision of motion and gravitation.
- Canal irrigation in Punjab depends on gravity flow from headworks such as Harike and Ropar.
- Tractor and combine-harvester safety (overturning on slopes, centre of gravity) is a common rural-accident theme in Punjab.

## Practice MCQs (12)

**1.** The recoil of a gun when a bullet is fired is explained by:

(a) Newton's first law of motion  
(b) Archimedes' principle  
(c) Law of gravitation  
(d) Newton's third law of motion

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) Newton's third law of motion** — The gun experiences an equal and opposite reaction; momentum is conserved.

</details>

**2.** The value of g on Earth's surface is maximum at the:

(a) Tropic of Cancer  
(b) Equator  
(c) Poles  
(d) Top of Mount Everest

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Poles** — Earth is flattened at the poles, so the poles are closer to the centre and g is highest there.

</details>

**3.** If the speed of a moving body is doubled, its kinetic energy becomes:

(a) Two times  
(b) Eight times  
(c) Half  
(d) Four times

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) Four times** — KE = 1/2 mv^2, so KE varies with the square of speed.

</details>

**4.** Consider: 1. It is easier to float in fresh water than in sea water. 2. The weight of a body is the same on the Moon as on Earth. Which is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) Neither 1 nor 2** — Sea water is denser and gives more buoyancy; weight on the Moon is about one-sixth because g is lower, though mass stays the same.

</details>

**5.** Seat belts and airbags reduce injury in accidents mainly by:

(a) Reducing gravitational force  
(b) Increasing the passenger's momentum  
(c) Increasing the time over which momentum changes  
(d) Reducing the mass of the passenger

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Increasing the time over which momentum changes** — Force = change in momentum / time; a longer stopping time means smaller force.

</details>

**6.** A geostationary satellite has an orbital period of about:

(a) 90 minutes  
(b) 27 days  
(c) 12 hours  
(d) 24 hours

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) 24 hours** — It matches Earth's rotation, so it appears fixed over one point on the equator at about 35,786 km altitude.

</details>

**7.** Consider the following statements:  
1. Astronauts on the ISS feel weightless because gravity is zero at that height.  
2. Gravity at about 400 km altitude is still about 90% of its value at Earth's surface.  
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) 2 only** — Weightlessness is due to continuous free fall, not absence of gravity.

</details>

**8.** Consider the following statements:  
1. Hydraulic brakes work on Pascal's law.  
2. Lift on an aircraft wing is explained by Bernoulli's principle.  
3. Lakes freeze from the top because water is densest at 4 C.  
Which of the statements given above are correct?

(a) 1 and 2 only  
(b) 2 and 3 only  
(c) 1 and 3 only  
(d) 1, 2 and 3

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) 1, 2 and 3** — All three are correct: hydraulic brakes use Pascal's law, wing lift is explained by Bernoulli's principle, and lakes freeze from the top because water is densest at 4 C. Dropping statement 3 is the tempting error.

</details>

**9.** A skater spins faster on pulling her arms in because of conservation of:

(a) Mass  
(b) Angular momentum  
(c) Electric charge  
(d) Linear momentum

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Angular momentum** — Smaller moment of inertia means greater angular speed.

</details>

**10.** Aditya-L1 operates in a halo orbit around:

(a) The Sun–Earth Lagrange point L1  
(b) The Earth–Moon point L2  
(c) A low Earth polar orbit  
(d) Geostationary orbit

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) The Sun–Earth Lagrange point L1** — L1 lies about 1.5 million km from Earth toward the Sun.

</details>

**11.** If a car's speed is doubled, its braking distance under the same braking force becomes about:

(a) Twice  
(b) Four times  
(c) Unchanged  
(d) Half

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Four times** — Kinetic energy, and so braking distance, varies with speed squared.

</details>

**12.** Since 2019 the SI kilogram has been defined using:

(a) A platinum-iridium cylinder kept near Paris  
(b) The mass of one litre of water at 4 C  
(c) The mass of one mole of oxygen  
(d) Planck's constant

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) Planck's constant** — The 2019 redefinition tied all base units to fixed constants.

</details>
