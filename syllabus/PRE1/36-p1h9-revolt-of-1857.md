# Revolt of 1857

## Short notes

- Mangal Pandey of the 34th Bengal Native Infantry attacked officers at Barrackpore on 29 March 1857 and was hanged in April.
- The revolt began at Meerut on 10 May 1857; sepoys marched to Delhi on 11 May and proclaimed Bahadur Shah Zafar as emperor.
- Immediate cause: cartridges of the new Enfield rifle, believed to be greased with cow and pig fat, offended both Hindu and Muslim sepoys.
- Political causes: Doctrine of Lapse, annexation of Awadh (1856), plans to end the Mughal title, and disrespect to rulers such as Nana Saheb (pension stopped).
- Economic causes: heavy land revenue, loss of land by taluqdars and peasants, ruin of handicrafts by British goods.
- Social-religious causes: fear of conversion, missionary activity, laws such as the abolition of sati (1829) and the Widow Remarriage Act (1856) seen as interference.
- Military causes: low pay and promotion for Indians, withdrawal of foreign-service allowance (batta), and the General Service Enlistment Act (1856) requiring service overseas.
- Leaders: Delhi — Bahadur Shah II and general Bakht Khan; Kanpur — Nana Saheb, Tantia Tope; Lucknow — Begum Hazrat Mahal; Jhansi — Rani Lakshmibai.
- Other leaders: Kunwar Singh (Jagdishpur, Bihar), Maulvi Ahmadullah (Faizabad), Khan Bahadur Khan (Bareilly).
- British commanders: Delhi — John Nicholson (killed) and Hodson; Lucknow — Henry Lawrence (killed), Havelock, Outram, Colin Campbell; Kanpur — Campbell; Jhansi — Hugh Rose.
- Delhi was recaptured in September 1857; Bahadur Shah was exiled to Rangoon and died there in 1862; Rani Lakshmibai died fighting near Gwalior in June 1858; Tantia Tope was executed in 1859.
- Many rulers stayed loyal — Scindia, Holkar, Nizam, and the Sikh states; most of south India, Bengal and Punjab saw little revolt.
- Reasons for failure: limited spread, no common leadership or plan, loyalty of many princes and the educated class, superior British arms, money and telegraph communication.
- Interpretations: V.D. Savarkar called it the War of Independence (1909 book); British writers called it a 'Sepoy Mutiny'; R.C. Majumdar denied it was a national war; S.N. Sen wrote the official centenary history 'Eighteen Fifty-Seven' (1957).
- Government of India Act 1858: Company rule ended; the Crown took over; a Secretary of State for India with a 15-member Council of India; the Governor-General became Viceroy (Canning first).
- Queen Victoria's Proclamation (read at Allahabad, 1 November 1858) promised no further annexations, religious non-interference and respect for princes' rights; the Doctrine of Lapse was dropped.
- Army reorganisation: higher ratio of European soldiers, artillery kept with Europeans, recruitment favouring 'martial races' and mixing communities to prevent unity.

## Punjab angle

- Punjab under Chief Commissioner John Lawrence stayed largely calm; Punjabi troops and the Sikh states (Patiala, Nabha, Jind, Kapurthala) helped the British retake Delhi.
- There were uprisings among sepoys at Ferozepur, Jalandhar and Sialkot, and Ahmad Khan Kharal led a rising in the Gogera area (now in Pakistan).
- At Ajnala (Amritsar), sepoys of the 26th Native Infantry were killed in 1857; their remains were excavated from a well in 2014.
- The British reward for Punjabi loyalty led to heavy army recruitment from Punjab in later decades.

## Deep dive

### Detailed timeline, 1857–59 (date — event)

- January 1857 — cartridge rumours at Dum Dum; 26 February — 19th Native Infantry at Berhampore refuses cartridges (later disbanded).
- 29 March — Mangal Pandey (34th NI) at Barrackpore; hanged 8 April; 24 April — 85 troopers of the 3rd Light Cavalry at Meerut refuse cartridges and are sentenced on 9 May.
- 10 May — Meerut rising; 11 May — Delhi seized, Bahadur Shah Zafar proclaimed; 30 May — Lucknow; 4–5 June — Kanpur and Jhansi rise.
- 27 June — Satichaura Ghat killings at Kanpur; 15 July — Bibighar massacre; Havelock retakes Kanpur (17 July); British reprisals under Neill.
- July–August — Kunwar Singh's siege of Arrah; Lucknow Residency besieged from 30 June (Henry Lawrence killed 4 July).
- 14–20 September — Delhi recaptured (Nicholson mortally wounded); 21 September — Bahadur Shah surrenders to Hodson at Humayun's tomb; princes shot near Khooni Darwaza.
- September and November 1857 — Lucknow relieved by Havelock–Outram, then by Colin Campbell; Lucknow finally retaken in March 1858.
- 3–4 April 1858 — Hugh Rose storms Jhansi; 17–18 June — Rani Lakshmibai killed at Kotah-ki-Serai near Gwalior; 20 June — Gwalior retaken.
- 26 April 1858 — Kunwar Singh dies after crossing the Ganga, wounded; 1 November 1858 — Queen's Proclamation read by Canning at Allahabad.
- April 1859 — Tantia Tope, betrayed by Man Singh of Narwar, hanged at Shivpuri (18 April); Bahadur Shah, tried in early 1858, exiled to Rangoon (died 1862).

### Region-wise leaders — beyond the textbook list

- Delhi — Bahadur Shah II, Bakht Khan (commander from Bareilly); Mirza Mughal led the court army.
- Kanpur — Nana Saheb (adopted son of Baji Rao II), Tantia Tope, Azimullah Khan (adviser, had visited London and Crimea).
- Awadh — Begum Hazrat Mahal with son Birjis Qadr; Maulvi Ahmadullah Shah of Faizabad (killed 1858); Raja Beni Madho, taluqdars.
- Rohilkhand — Khan Bahadur Khan (Bareilly); Allahabad — Liaqat Ali; Bihar — Kunwar Singh (Jagdishpur) and his brother Amar Singh.
- Haryana region — Rao Tula Ram (Rewari; fought the Battle of Nasibpur near Narnaul, 16 November 1857), Lala Hukam Chand Jain (Hansi, hanged 1858), Raja Nahar Singh (Ballabhgarh) and Nawab Abdur Rahman Khan (Jhajjar), both hanged.
- Central India and Rajasthan — Rani of Ramgarh (Avantibai Lodhi), Kota (Jai Dayal and Mehrab Khan), Feroz Shah (a Mughal prince) in Malwa.
- East and Northeast — Surendra Sai (Sambalpur), Thakur Vishwanath Shahdeo and Pandey Ganpat Rai (Chhota Nagpur), Maniram Dewan (Assam, hanged 1858).
- Loyalists: Scindia (minister Dinkar Rao), Holkar, Nizam (minister Salar Jung), Rajput chiefs, Sikh states of Patiala, Nabha, Jind, Kapurthala; Gorkha help from Jang Bahadur of Nepal.

### Punjab and Haryana in 1857 — detail for PPSC

- John Lawrence (Chief Commissioner) disarmed suspect regiments at Mian Mir, Lahore, on 13 May 1857 and formed a Movable Column under Neville Chamberlain, later John Nicholson.
- Uprisings: Ferozepur (13 May), Jalandhar and Phillaur (7–8 June — mutineers crossed the Sutlej and reached Delhi), Sialkot (9 July; Nicholson defeated them at Trimmu Ghat), Jhelum, Rawalpindi.
- Ajnala (Amritsar): sepoys of the 26th NI who had fled Mian Mir were killed on the orders of Deputy Commissioner Frederick Cooper (August 1857); remains were recovered from a well in 2014.
- Ahmad Khan Kharal led a rising of Kharal and other tribes in the Gogera/Neeli Bar area; he was killed in September 1857.
- The Phulkian states (Patiala, Nabha, Jind) and Kapurthala guarded the Grand Trunk Road and supplied troops for the Delhi siege; they were rewarded with land and titles.
- Punjabi and Pathan levies, the Guides and the Punjab Irregular Force formed a large part of the Delhi Field Force.
- Punishment: after 1858 the Delhi territory, including the present Haryana districts, was taken from the North-Western Provinces and attached to Punjab — the start of 'Haryana in Punjab' till 1966.
- Long-term effect: Punjab became the main recruiting ground of the Indian Army under the 'martial races' policy.

### Nature of the revolt — historiography table (writer — view)

- British officials (J. Seeley, J.W. Kaye in parts) — a 'sepoy mutiny' without popular support; James Outram — a Muslim conspiracy using Hindu grievances.
- Benjamin Disraeli (in Parliament) and Karl Marx (New York Daily Tribune, 1857) — a national revolt, not merely military.
- Syed Ahmad Khan, 'Asbab-e-Baghawat-e-Hind' (1859) — blamed absence of Indians in the legislative council and misunderstanding between rulers and ruled.
- V.D. Savarkar, 'The Indian War of Independence of 1857' (1909) — the 'First War of Independence', planned and national.
- R.C. Majumdar — 'neither first, nor national, nor a war of independence'; S.N. Sen ('Eighteen Fifty-Seven', 1957, official centenary volume) — began as a mutiny, became a revolt in places.
- S.B. Chaudhuri — 'civil rebellions' joined the military mutiny; Eric Stokes ('The Peasant Armed') — local agrarian roots; Rudrangshu Mukherjee — Awadh as a people's war; Ranajit Guha — subaltern insurgency.
- Exam framing: mutiny in origin, popular revolt in north and central India, limited in the south and east, without a unified programme.

### Why it failed and what changed (cause — consequence)

- Limited spread: Bengal proper, Punjab, Rajputana and the south largely quiet; the educated middle class stayed aloof.
- No unified command or programme — restoration of old rulers was the main aim; leaders acted locally.
- British advantages: telegraph ('the telegraph saved India'), Enfield rifles, control of sea routes, resources of the empire and loyal princes.
- Government of India Act 1858 — Crown rule, Secretary of State, Viceroy; Queen's Proclamation promised no further annexation, religious non-interference and equal opportunity.
- Army reorganisation (Peel Commission, 1859): higher European share (about 1:2 in the Bengal army), artillery kept in British hands, regiments mixed by caste and region, recruitment shifted to 'martial races'.
- Princes became allies: adoption sanads (1860) ended the Doctrine of Lapse; paramountcy replaced annexation.
- Legal-administrative overhaul: Indian Penal Code (1860), Police Act (1861), Indian Councils Act (1861), High Courts Act (1861).
- Political memory: 1857 became an inspiration for later nationalists — Savarkar, Ghadarites and INA units used its symbols (Rani of Jhansi Regiment).

### PPSC-style asks and traps

- Mangal Pandey's act (29 March) preceded the Meerut outbreak (10 May) — the revolt 'began' at Meerut, not Barrackpore.
- Lord Canning was Governor-General and became the first Viceroy; Dalhousie had left India in 1856.
- Bahadur Shah surrendered at Humayun's tomb, not at the Red Fort; Hodson shot the princes.
- Lucknow: Henry Lawrence died in the Residency; Havelock died (November 1857) after the first relief; Campbell's second relief evacuated it.
- Rani Lakshmibai died near Gwalior, not at Jhansi; Hugh Rose called her the bravest of the rebel leaders.
- Tantia Tope was captured through betrayal and hanged at Shivpuri (1859) — he was the longest-fighting leader.
- Rao Tula Ram (Rewari) and the Nasibpur battle are Haryana-specific items often missed.
