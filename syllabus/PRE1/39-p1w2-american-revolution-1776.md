## Short notes

- Thirteen British colonies on the Atlantic coast of North America were governed under mercantilist policy; Navigation Acts restricted their trade to benefit Britain.
- Britain's debt after the Seven Years' War (1756–63) led to new taxes on the colonies; the Proclamation of 1763 also barred settlement west of the Appalachians.
- Taxes and acts: Sugar Act (1764), Stamp Act (1765, repealed 1766), Declaratory Act (1766), Townshend Acts (1767).
- Colonists argued 'No taxation without representation' since they had no members in the British Parliament.
- Boston Massacre (1770); Tea Act (1773) led to the Boston Tea Party (16 December 1773); Britain responded with the Coercive ('Intolerable') Acts (1774).
- First Continental Congress met at Philadelphia (1774); fighting began at Lexington and Concord (April 1775); the Second Continental Congress made George Washington commander-in-chief.
- Thomas Paine's pamphlet 'Common Sense' (January 1776) argued for independence and won wide support.
- Declaration of Independence adopted on 4 July 1776, drafted mainly by Thomas Jefferson; it asserted natural rights to life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness (influenced by John Locke).
- Battle of Saratoga (1777) was the turning point; it brought France into the war as an ally (1778), later joined by Spain and the Dutch.
- At Yorktown (1781) British general Cornwallis surrendered — the same Cornwallis later became Governor-General in India (Permanent Settlement, 1793).
- Treaty of Paris (1783): Britain recognised the independence of the United States.
- The Articles of Confederation (1781) proved weak; the Philadelphia Convention (1787) drafted the US Constitution, in force from 1789, with George Washington as first President.
- The US Constitution was the first written national constitution: federalism, separation of powers, checks and balances, independent judiciary; the Bill of Rights (first ten amendments) was added in 1791.
- Limits: slavery continued (abolished by the 13th Amendment, 1865); women and Native Americans were excluded from rights.
- Significance: first successful colonial revolt against a European power; inspired the French Revolution and later anti-colonial movements; spread ideas of republic and popular sovereignty.
- India's Constitution borrowed from the US: Fundamental Rights, judicial review, independence of judiciary, impeachment of the President, removal of judges, and the Vice-President as ex-officio Chairman of the Upper House.

## How PPSC asks it

Expect sequence of acts and events, the author of the Declaration or Common Sense, turning-point battles, and the Treaty of Paris. The Cornwallis link and US features in the Indian Constitution are common twists.

## Deep dive

### Detailed timeline, 1754–1791 (year — event)

- 1754–63 — French and Indian War (American theatre of the Seven Years' War); Britain wins Canada but doubles its national debt, so London decides the colonies must pay for their own defence.
- 1763 — Royal Proclamation bars settlement west of the Appalachians; Pontiac's War on the frontier; end of the long era of 'salutary neglect'.
- 1764 — Sugar Act (duty on molasses, stricter customs) and Currency Act (ban on colonial paper money).
- 1765 — Stamp Act (first direct internal tax on newspapers, legal papers, playing cards) and Quartering Act; Stamp Act Congress meets in New York (October, delegates of 9 colonies); Sons of Liberty organise boycotts.
- 1766 — Stamp Act repealed, but the Declaratory Act asserts Parliament's power to legislate for the colonies 'in all cases whatsoever'.
- 1767 — Townshend Acts tax imports of glass, lead, paint, paper and tea; John Dickinson's 'Letters from a Farmer in Pennsylvania' argue Parliament may regulate trade but not tax for revenue.
- 1770 — Boston Massacre (5 March, five colonists killed); Townshend duties repealed except the tax on tea; Lord North becomes Prime Minister (serves to 1782).
- 1772–73 — Committees of Correspondence (Samuel Adams, Boston) link the colonies; Tea Act (1773) gives the East India Company a monopoly on tea sales, leading to the Boston Tea Party (16 December 1773).
- 1774 — Coercive Acts: Boston Port Act, Massachusetts Government Act, Administration of Justice Act, new Quartering Act; the Quebec Act extends Quebec's borders; First Continental Congress (12 colonies, Georgia absent) adopts the Continental Association boycott.
- 1775 — Patrick Henry's 'liberty or death' speech (March); Lexington and Concord (19 April); Second Continental Congress (May); Bunker (Breed's) Hill (June); Olive Branch Petition rejected; King declares the colonies in rebellion (August).
- 1776 — 'Common Sense' (January); Lee Resolution for independence voted 2 July; Declaration adopted 4 July (most signatures added on 2 August); Washington's surprise victory at Trenton (26 December).
- 1777–78 — Saratoga (Burgoyne surrenders, 17 October 1777); winter at Valley Forge; Franco-American Treaty of Alliance (February 1778); Spain joins against Britain (1779), the Dutch Republic (1780).
- 1781 — Articles of Confederation in force (1 March); Cornwallis surrenders at Yorktown (19 October) to Washington and the French general Rochambeau, with de Grasse's French fleet blocking the sea.
- 1783–91 — Treaty of Paris (3 September 1783); Shays' Rebellion (1786–87) exposes a weak centre; Philadelphia Convention (May–September 1787) signs the Constitution on 17 September 1787; New Hampshire becomes the ninth state to ratify (June 1788); Washington inaugurated 30 April 1789; Bill of Rights ratified 15 December 1791.

### Key personalities (name — role — why it matters)

- George Washington — commander of the Continental Army (1775), presided over the 1787 Convention, first President (1789–97); set the two-term tradition.
- Thomas Jefferson — main drafter of the Declaration (committee of five: Jefferson, Adams, Franklin, Sherman, Livingston); later third President (Louisiana Purchase, 1803).
- Benjamin Franklin — scientist-diplomat; won the French alliance in Paris (1778) and helped negotiate the 1783 peace.
- John Adams — leading voice for independence in Congress; negotiator at Paris; second President.
- Samuel Adams and the Sons of Liberty — organisers of protest in Boston; Committees of Correspondence.
- Thomas Paine — 'Common Sense' (1776) and 'The American Crisis' pamphlets; later defended the French Revolution in 'Rights of Man' (1791).
- James Madison — 'Father of the Constitution' (Virginia Plan) and main author of the Bill of Rights.
- Alexander Hamilton, Madison and John Jay — wrote the 85 'Federalist' essays (1787–88) defending the new Constitution.
- Marquis de Lafayette — French volunteer general; later drafted France's Declaration of the Rights of Man with Jefferson's advice (1789).
- King George III and Lord North — British side; Lord Cornwallis surrendered at Yorktown and was later Governor-General of India (1786–93).
- John Locke (natural rights, consent of the governed) and Montesquieu (separation of powers) — the two thinkers most visible in the Declaration and Constitution.

### Causes analysed — economic, political, ideological

- Mercantilism: Navigation Acts (from 1651) made the colonies supply raw materials and buy British goods; smuggling was common and tolerated until 1763.
- Fiscal pressure after 1763: Britain kept about 10,000 troops in America and wanted colonial revenue; colonists saw new taxes as a break with tradition.
- Constitutional argument: colonists claimed the 'rights of Englishmen' — only their own elected assemblies could tax them; Britain replied with 'virtual representation' in Parliament.
- Self-government habit: each colony had an elected assembly (Virginia's House of Burgesses dated from 1619), so local elites were used to ruling themselves.
- Ideology: Enlightenment natural rights plus English 'Country' (Whig) suspicion of standing armies and corrupt ministers; many religious dissenters distrusted the Anglican establishment.
- Frontier grievance: the 1763 Proclamation and the 1774 Quebec Act blocked westward expansion that land speculators (including Washington) wanted.
- Immediate triggers: British repression after the Tea Party (Coercive Acts) united the colonies; fighting at Lexington and Concord made compromise almost impossible.
- Not a single class revolt: merchants, planters, artisans and farmers joined for different reasons; perhaps a fifth of colonists stayed Loyalist and tens of thousands later left, many for Canada.

### The Constitution of 1787 — features to know (and India links)

- Federal structure: powers divided between the Union and states; Tenth Amendment reserves residuary powers to states (India gives residuary powers to the Union — a contrast).
- Separation of powers in three Articles: Congress (Art. I), President (Art. II), courts (Art. III); checks and balances such as presidential veto, Senate confirmation and impeachment.
- Connecticut (Great) Compromise: House of Representatives by population, Senate with two members per state.
- Three-fifths Compromise counted enslaved people as three-fifths of a person for representation; the slave trade could not be banned before 1808.
- President chosen by an Electoral College; amendment procedure in Article V needs two-thirds of Congress and three-fourths of the states — a rigid constitution.
- Judicial review is not written in the text; the Supreme Court claimed it in Marbury v. Madison (1803).
- Bill of Rights (1791): First Amendment (religion, speech, press, assembly), Fifth (due process, no self-incrimination), Sixth (fair trial).
- India borrowed: Preamble idea ('We the people'), Fundamental Rights, judicial review, independent judiciary, President's impeachment, removal of SC/HC judges, Vice-President as chair of the Upper House.
- India deliberately chose 'procedure established by law' (Art. 21) over American 'due process'; the Supreme Court read fairness into it in Maneka Gandhi (1978).

### Consequences and global impact

- First successful colonial revolt; created the first large modern republic and the first written national constitution still in force.
- France's war spending in America worsened its debt crisis — a direct link to the French Revolution of 1789.
- Inspired later revolts: Haiti (independence 1804), Spanish American wars of independence (Bolívar, San Martín, 1810s–20s), and Irish radicals of the 1790s.
- Britain, having lost the thirteen colonies, turned more to India and the East — historians call this the shift to a 'second British Empire'.
- Loyalists who moved north shaped English-speaking Canada (Upper Canada, New Brunswick).
- Limits: slavery survived (ended only by the Civil War and the 13th Amendment, 1865); women could not vote (19th Amendment, 1920); Native Americans lost land as settlers moved west.
- Northwest Ordinance (1787) set the pattern for admitting new states as equals and banned slavery in the Northwest Territory.

### Historiography — how historians read it

- Whig/patriotic school (George Bancroft, 19th century): a struggle for liberty against tyranny.
- Imperial school (C.M. Andrews): view from London — an administrative crisis of empire.
- Progressive school: Charles Beard, 'An Economic Interpretation of the Constitution' (1913) — the founders protected property and creditors' interests.
- Ideological school: Bernard Bailyn, 'The Ideological Origins of the American Revolution' (1967) — colonists genuinely feared a conspiracy against liberty.
- Gordon Wood, 'The Radicalism of the American Revolution' (1992): it transformed a hierarchical society into a democratic one.
- Exam angle: 'War of Independence or Revolution?' — answer that it was both: separation from Britain plus a new political order (republic, written constitution, rights).

### PPSC-style asks and common traps

- Trap: the Declaration was adopted on 4 July 1776, but the formal vote for independence was on 2 July and most signing was on 2 August.
- Trap: the Constitution was drafted in 1787 but came into force in 1789; the Bill of Rights was added in 1791.
- Trap: 'No taxation without representation' is a colonial slogan of the 1760s (often linked to James Otis), not a line of the Declaration.
- Trap: Saratoga (1777) is the turning point; Yorktown (1781) is the last major battle; the Treaty of Paris (1783) ended the war.
- Trap: Lafayette was French, Paine was English-born; Franklin was never President.
- Match-the-pair favourites: Common Sense—Paine; Federalist Papers—Hamilton/Madison/Jay; Declaration—Jefferson; 'Father of the Constitution'—Madison.
- Indian link questions: Cornwallis (Yorktown, then Permanent Settlement 1793 in India); US features borrowed in the Indian Constitution.
- Mains-style (10 marks): 'Explain how mercantilism and the end of salutary neglect led to the American Revolution' — use the timeline and causes sections above.

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) 1 only** — Cornwallis surrendered at Yorktown and later became Governor-General; the Constitution came into force in 1789, after 1776.

**6.** The Boston Tea Party (1773) was a protest against the:

(a) Quartering Act  
(b) Tea Act  
(c) Stamp Act  
(d) Sugar Act

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Tea Act** — Colonists dumped East India Company tea into Boston harbour in protest against the Tea Act.

</details>

**7.** Which Act, passed in 1766 along with the repeal of the Stamp Act, asserted the British Parliament's right to make laws for the American colonies 'in all cases whatsoever'?

(a) Townshend Acts  
(b) Declaratory Act  
(c) Quartering Act  
(d) Sugar Act

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Declaratory Act** — Parliament repealed the Stamp Act in 1766 but at once passed the Declaratory Act to insist on its full authority. The Townshend Acts (1767) came next, while the Sugar Act (1764) preceded the Stamp Act.

</details>

**8.** Who was appointed commander-in-chief of the Continental Army by the Second Continental Congress in 1775?

(a) John Adams  
(b) Benjamin Franklin  
(c) Alexander Hamilton  
(d) George Washington

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) George Washington** — The Second Continental Congress chose George Washington in June 1775, after fighting had begun at Lexington and Concord. Franklin served as a diplomat in France, and Hamilton was Washington's aide.

</details>

**9.** Consider the following statements:  
1. The First Continental Congress met at Boston in 1774.  
2. The Articles of Confederation created a strong central government with the power to levy taxes.  
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) Neither 1 nor 2** — Neither is correct: the First Continental Congress met at Philadelphia, and the Articles of Confederation (in force 1781) left Congress unable to tax. That weakness led to the Philadelphia Convention of 1787 and the US Constitution.

</details>

**10.** The first ten amendments to the US Constitution, known as the Bill of Rights, came into force in:

(a) 1791  
(b) 1789  
(c) 1776  
(d) 1787

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) 1791** — The Bill of Rights was ratified in December 1791. 1776 is the Declaration of Independence, 1787 the drafting of the Constitution, and 1789 the year it came into force with Washington as President.

</details>

**11.** Consider the following statements:  
1. The Proclamation of 1763 barred colonial settlement west of the Appalachian Mountains.  
2. The Townshend Acts of 1767 placed duties on imports such as glass, paper and tea.  
3. The Coercive (Intolerable) Acts were Britain's response to the Boston Massacre.  
Which of the statements given above are correct?

(a) 1 and 2 only  
(b) 2 and 3 only  
(c) 1 and 3 only  
(d) 1, 2 and 3

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) 1 and 2 only** — The 1763 Proclamation line and the Townshend duties are correctly described. The Coercive Acts of 1774 punished Massachusetts for the Boston Tea Party (December 1773), not for the Boston Massacre of 1770.

</details>

**12.** The 'Federalist Papers' (1787–88), written to win support for the new US Constitution, were authored by Alexander Hamilton, James Madison and:

(a) Thomas Paine  
(b) Thomas Jefferson  
(c) Samuel Adams  
(d) John Jay

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) John Jay** — Hamilton, Madison and John Jay wrote the 85 essays under the pen name 'Publius'. Jefferson was in France as minister at the time, and Paine is known for 'Common Sense' (1776).

</details>
