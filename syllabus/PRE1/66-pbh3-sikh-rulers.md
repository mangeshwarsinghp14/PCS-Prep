# Sikh rulers

## Short notes

- Banda Singh Bahadur (1710–15) set up the first Sikh rule: Lohgarh capital, coins and seal in the Gurus' names, abolition of zamindari in the areas he held.
- Misl period: 12 misls — Bhangi, Ahluwalia, Ramgarhia, Kanhaiya, Sukerchakia, Nakai, Dallewalia, Nishanwalia, Singhpuria (Faizullapuria), Karorasinghia, Shaheedan, Phulkian.
- Misl institutions: Sarbat Khalsa and Gurmata (collective decisions at Amritsar), rakhi system (protection levy); Dal Khalsa formed in 1748 under Jassa Singh Ahluwalia.
- Phulkian states (Patiala, Nabha, Jind) lay south of the Sutlej; Baba Ala Singh founded Patiala (Qila Mubarak, 1763). Kapurthala was the Ahluwalia state; Faridkot, Malerkotla and Kalsia were other princely states.
- Maharaja Ranjit Singh: born 13 Nov 1780 at Gujranwala, son of Maha Singh of the Sukerchakia misl; took Lahore on 7 July 1799; proclaimed Maharaja on Baisakhi 1801.
- Conquests: Amritsar (1805), Kangra (1809), Attock (1813), Multan (1818), Kashmir (1819), Peshawar (annexed 1834); Ladakh taken by the Dogra general Zorawar Singh (1834).
- Treaty of Amritsar (25 April 1809) with Charles Metcalfe fixed the Sutlej as the boundary — the cis-Sutlej states came under British protection.
- Ranjit Singh met Lord William Bentinck at Ropar (1831); the Tripartite Treaty (1838) was between the British, Ranjit Singh and Shah Shuja.
- Ranjit Singh obtained the Koh-i-noor from Shah Shuja (1813); gold-plated Harmandir Sahib; struck Nanakshahi coins; capital Lahore.
- Army: Fauj-i-Ain (regular) and Fauj-i-Khas trained by French and Italian generals (Ventura, Allard, Court, Avitabile); Hari Singh Nalwa and Diwan Mohkam Chand were key commanders.
- Administration: Fakir Azizuddin (foreign affairs), Dhian Singh Dogra (prime minister), Dina Nath (finance); secular court with Hindu, Muslim and European officers. Ranjit Singh died on 27 June 1839.
- Successors: Kharak Singh (1839–40), Nau Nihal Singh (killed by a falling gateway, 1840), Sher Singh (1841–43), Duleep Singh (1843–49) under regent Maharani Jind Kaur; court intrigues weakened the state.
- First Anglo-Sikh War (1845–46): Mudki, Ferozeshah, Buddowal, Aliwal, Sobraon (10 Feb 1846, Sham Singh Attariwala killed); treachery of Lal Singh and Tej Singh.
- Treaty of Lahore (9 March 1846): Jalandhar Doab ceded, indemnity imposed, army cut; Treaty of Amritsar (16 March 1846): Kashmir sold to Gulab Singh for ₹75 lakh; Treaty of Bhairowal (Dec 1846): British Resident (Henry Lawrence) controlled the regency.
- Second Anglo-Sikh War (1848–49): began with Mulraj's revolt at Multan (1848); battles of Ramnagar, Chillianwala (13 Jan 1849) and Gujrat (21 Feb 1849, the 'battle of guns').
- Annexation of Punjab on 29 March 1849 by Lord Dalhousie; Duleep Singh was sent to England and the Koh-i-noor went to Queen Victoria.
- Princely states after 1849 (Patiala, Nabha, Jind, Faridkot, Kapurthala, Malerkotla, Kalsia, Nalagarh) merged into PEPSU (1948), which joined Punjab on 1 Nov 1956.

## Punjab angle

- Ranjit Singh's summer palace at Ram Bagh, Amritsar, and Gobindgarh Fort are now heritage sites; Sobraon, Ferozeshah and Mudki lie in today's Ferozepur–Tarn Taran region.

## How PPSC asks it

Dates and sequence are key: 1799, 1801, 1809, the conquest years, the battle order of both wars and the treaties of 1846. Also misl names and the founders of the princely states.

## Deep dive

### Banda Singh Bahadur (1708–1716): detailed chronology

- 1670: born Lachhman Dev at Rajouri (Poonch). He became the bairagi Madho Das and kept a math at Nanded.
- Sept 1708: met Guru Gobind Singh at Nanded and was named Banda Singh. He received five arrows, a nagara (drum) and nishan (banner), hukamnamas to the sangats, and a council of five Sikhs including Binod Singh and Baj Singh.
- Nov 1709: sacked Samana (home of the executioners of Guru Tegh Bahadur and the Sahibzadas), then Ghuram, Thaska, Shahabad, Mustafabad, Kapuri, Sadhaura (where Usman Khan had persecuted Pir Budhu Shah) and Banur.
- 12 May 1710: battle of Chappar Chiri, where Wazir Khan was killed. Sirhind was occupied on 14 May 1710 and Baj Singh was made governor.
- Lohgarh (Mukhlisgarh, in the Sadhaura hills) became the capital. He struck a coin and used a seal in the names of Guru Nanak and Guru Gobind Singh, and dated orders in a new era from the conquest of Sirhind.
- Agrarian change: zamindari dues were ended and tillers were treated as owners in the territory he held. Scholars debate how systematic this was.
- Dec 1710: Bahadur Shah personally besieged Lohgarh and Banda escaped to the hills. The emperor's edict ordered Sikhs killed; Bahadur Shah died at Lahore in Feb 1712.
- 1711–15: raids in the Jalandhar Doab, Batala and Kalanaur. Farrukhsiyar ordered Abdus Samad Khan to finish the campaign.
- 1715: an eight-month siege at Gurdas Nangal (the haveli of Duni Chand); Banda surrendered starving in Dec 1715.
- 1716: paraded through Delhi with hundreds of captives, who were executed in batches. Banda was executed on 9 June 1716 after his son Ajai Singh was killed before him.
- Aftermath: a split between the Tat Khalsa and the 'Bandai' Khalsa, settled at Amritsar in 1721 by Bhai Mani Singh, according to tradition.
- Significance (Mains): first Sikh political authority; broke the aura of Mughal invincibility; gave a precedent for Khalsa sovereignty and peasant ownership; the 'first Sikh state', though short-lived.

### Misl organisation (c.1748–1799)

- The word misl is variously derived from the Arabic for 'alike/equal' or from 'file/record' (a register of jathas).
- Dal Khalsa: at the Sarbat Khalsa of Baisakhi, 29 March 1748, at Amritsar, about 65 jathas were grouped into 11 misls under the supreme command of Jassa Singh Ahluwalia, with Nawab Kapur Singh still revered as leader. The first six misls came under the Budha Dal and the other five under the Taruna Dal (Encyclopaedia of Sikhism).
- The Phulkian (Patiala) house was counted as the 12th misl but was never part of the Dal Khalsa command.
- Sarbat Khalsa met at Amritsar at Baisakhi and Diwali. Decisions taken before the Guru Granth were called gurmatas and bound all misls on common matters such as war, defence of Amritsar and choice of commander.
- Rakhi (protection) system: villages paid about one-fifth of produce, usually at the two harvests, for protection against Afghans and others. It gave the misls revenue and peasant loyalty.
- Land within a misl was shared out as pattis (shares) to sardars and their horsemen. Arrangements included pattidari, misldari, tabedari (dependants) and jagirdari.
- Ideals versus practice: an early equality of horsemen gave way to hereditary chiefships by the 1780s. Misls fought each other (Bhangi against Sukerchakia, Ramgarhia against Kanhaiya and Ahluwalia).
- Historiography: Cunningham and Latif called it a 'theocratic confederate feudalism'; others saw it as democratic or republican; Grewal and Indu Banga stress its emerging chiefships and agrarian base.
- Ranjit Singh stopped convening the Sarbat Khalsa after the 1805 gathering (held on the Holkar issue). Gurmata politics revived only in the 20th century.

### Misls: founder or leading chief and headquarters (as commonly given in PSEB texts)

- Bhangi: Chhajja Singh and Bhuma Singh, then Hari Singh; headquarters Amritsar, later Lahore and Gujrat. The strongest misl in the 1760s; Lehna Singh and Sobha Singh held Lahore from 1765 to 1797. They captured the gun Zamzama ('Bhangian wali top').
- Ahluwalia: Jassa Singh Ahluwalia (1718–83), titled Sultan-ul-Qaum and commander of the Dal Khalsa; headquarters Kapurthala (later a princely state).
- Ramgarhia: Jassa Singh Ramgarhia (1723–1803); headquarters Sri Hargobindpur. Named after the Ram Rauni fort (Ramgarh) at Amritsar; expelled to Hansi–Hisar in 1776 and returned in 1783.
- Kanhaiya: Jai Singh Kanhaiya; headquarters Sohian, later Batala and Mukerian. Sada Kaur, widow of Gurbakhsh Singh (killed at Achal, 1785), was Ranjit Singh's mother-in-law and ally.
- Sukerchakia: Charat Singh (Ranjit Singh's grandfather), then Mahan Singh; headquarters Gujranwala.
- Nakai: Hira Singh Nakai; headquarters Chunian (the Nakka tract south-west of Lahore). Ranjit Singh's wife Raj Kaur (Datar Kaur) came from this misl.
- Dallewalia: Gulab Singh, later Tara Singh Ghaiba; headquarters Rahon (Doaba).
- Nishanwalia: Dasaundha Singh, standard-bearers of the Dal Khalsa; headquarters Ambala and Shahabad.
- Singhpuria or Faizullapuria: Nawab Kapur Singh (d. 1753); headquarters Jalandhar, with the original seat at Faizullapur near Amritsar.
- Karorasinghia (Panjgarhia): Karora Singh, later Baghel Singh; headquarters Chhalaudi (Karnal area). Baghel Singh entered Delhi in 1783. The Kalsia state descends from this misl.
- Shaheedan: Baba Deep Singh and Sudh Singh; headquarters Talwandi Sabo (Damdama Sahib) and later Shahzadpur.
- Phulkian: Phul's descendants under Ala Singh; headquarters Patiala (Qila Mubarak, 1763), with the collateral states of Nabha and Jind. Outside the Dal Khalsa.

### Sikh expansion milestones, 1764–1799

- Jan 1764: the combined Dal Khalsa took Sirhind and killed the Afghan governor Zain Khan. The Sirhind sarkar was divided among Phulkian and other chiefs.
- 1765: Lahore was occupied by Lehna Singh and Sobha Singh (Bhangi) and Gujjar Singh. Sikh coins were struck in the name of the Gurus, a statement of sovereignty.
- 1767–1780s: Sikh raids into the Ganga–Yamuna Doab and Rohilkhand; local chiefs paid rakhi; the Mughal court sought treaties.
- 1783: Baghel Singh (Karorasinghia) camped in Delhi with a large force. By tradition he was allowed to build gurdwaras at Sikh sites (Sis Ganj, Rakab Ganj, Bangla Sahib, Majnu ka Tilla and others) and to take a share of Delhi's octroi.
- 1785: Mahadji Scindia tried to make a treaty with the cis-Sutlej Sikhs. The Marathas, Sikhs and Rohillas competed in the Delhi region.
- 1785: battle of Achal (near Batala): the Sukerchakias and Ramgarhias defeated the Kanhaiyas. It led to the Sukerchakia–Kanhaiya marriage alliance (Ranjit Singh and Mehtab Kaur).
- 1797–99: Zaman Shah's invasions; he briefly held Lahore in Jan 1799. Ranjit Singh took Lahore on 7 July 1799.
- Ranjit Singh absorbed the trans-Sutlej misls one by one (Bhangis, Kanhaiyas, Ramgarhias, Nakais, Dallewalias). The 1809 treaty left the cis-Sutlej chiefs under British protection.

### After Ranjit Singh: the Lahore Darbar, 1839–45

- Kharak Singh (June 1839–Nov 1840): his favourite Chet Singh Bajwa was murdered in Oct 1839, and real power passed to his son Nau Nihal Singh and the Dogra wazir Dhian Singh.
- Nau Nihal Singh died on 5 Nov 1840 when a gateway fell on him as he returned from his father's cremation; many suspected a plot.
- Rani Chand Kaur (Kharak Singh's widow) was regent from Dec 1840 to Jan 1841. Sher Singh, backed by the army and the Dogras, took Lahore in Jan 1841.
- Sher Singh (1841–15 Sept 1843) was killed by Ajit Singh and Lehna Singh Sandhawalia, who also killed Dhian Singh the same day. Dhian Singh's son Hira Singh avenged them and became wazir.
- Hira Singh (Sept 1843–Dec 1844), guided by Pandit Jalla, was killed by the army. Jawahar Singh, Maharani Jind Kaur's brother, became wazir (May 1845) and was executed by the army (Sept 1845).
- Lal Singh (wazir) and Tej Singh (commander-in-chief) took charge as the regimental panchayats of the Khalsa army became kingmakers. Both were in contact with the British in 1845–46.
- Gulab Singh of Jammu (Dhian Singh's brother) stayed aloof, negotiated with both sides and gained Kashmir in 1846.

### Cis-Sutlej and princely Sikh states

- Phulkian origin: Phul (d. 1652), a Sidhu–Brar Jat chaudhri, was blessed by Guru Har Rai according to tradition. His descendants founded Patiala, Nabha and Jind.
- Patiala: Ala Singh (1691–1765) built Qila Mubarak (1763). Abdali recognised him as Raja; his grandson Amar Singh received the title Raja-i-Rajgan (1767).
- Nabha (Hamir Singh) and Jind (Gajpat Singh, whose capital moved to Sangrur in 1827) both became states around 1763–64. Faridkot came under the Brar chief Hamir Singh (c.1763); Kapurthala under the Ahluwalias; Kalsia under the Karorasinghia line.
- Malerkotla was ruled by Afghan nawabs; its Nawab Sher Mohammad Khan protested the execution of the Sahibzadas (1705), and Sikhs long remembered this.
- Ranjit Singh's three cis-Sutlej expeditions (1806–08), sparked by the Patiala–Nabha dispute over Doladhi, led the chiefs to seek British protection.
- 1809: the Treaty of Amritsar (25 April) and the British proclamation of 3 May 1809 took the cis-Sutlej states under protection, with no tribute. A second proclamation (1811) protected them against each other.
- 1857: Patiala, Nabha, Jind and Kapurthala aided the British and were rewarded with Narnaul (Patiala), Dadri (Jind) and estates in Awadh (Kapurthala), plus sanads of adoption (1860).
- 20th century: Maharaja Ripudaman Singh of Nabha was forced to abdicate (1923), which triggered the Jaito morcha. Bhupinder Singh of Patiala was Chancellor of the Chamber of Princes. The eight states merged into PEPSU (1948).

### PPSC traps

- Jassa Singh Ahluwalia (Dal Khalsa commander, Kapurthala) is a different person from Jassa Singh Ramgarhia (Ram Rauni, Sri Hargobindpur).
- The Sukerchakia founder was Charat Singh, Ranjit Singh's grandfather; his father was Mahan Singh. Ranjit Singh was not the founder of the misl.
- The Phulkian misl was outside the Dal Khalsa. Its states came under British protection in 1809, not after 1849.
- The Dal Khalsa dates from 1748, the Budha Dal and Taruna Dal from 1734, and the Sikh occupation of Lahore from 1765. Do not mix them up.
- Chappar Chiri (1710) is not the same as Gurdas Nangal (1715). Lohgarh (Banda's capital) is not the same as Gobindgarh fort (Amritsar, a Bhangi fort later strengthened by Ranjit Singh).
- The Bhangis held Lahore from 1765 to 1799, so Ranjit Singh took Lahore from the Bhangi sardars, not from the Afghans.

**9.** In the battle of Chappar Chiri (May 1710), Banda Singh Bahadur's forces defeated and killed:

(a) Zakariya Khan, governor of Lahore

**11.** Arrange the following conquests of Maharaja Ranjit Singh in chronological order:

**12.** The battle that ended the Second Anglo-Sikh War, fought on 21 February 1849, was:

(a) Gujrat  
(b) Sobraon

**(a) Gujrat** — Lord Gough's victory at Gujrat (the 'battle of the guns') ended the war, and Punjab was annexed on 29 March 1849. Ramnagar (Nov 1848) and Chillianwala (Jan 1849) came earlier in the same war; Sobraon (1846) ended the first war.

</details>
