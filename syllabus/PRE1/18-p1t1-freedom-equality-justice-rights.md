# Freedom, equality, justice, rights

## Short notes

- Negative liberty = absence of external restraint; positive liberty = the capacity and conditions to realise one's potential (Isaiah Berlin, 'Two Concepts of Liberty', 1958).
- J.S. Mill, 'On Liberty' (1859): harm principle - the state may restrict a person's action only to prevent harm to others; he separated self-regarding from other-regarding actions.
- Preamble secures liberty of thought, expression, belief, faith and worship; Art 19 gives six freedoms to citizens; Art 21 protects life and personal liberty.
- Maneka Gandhi v Union of India (1978): 'procedure established by law' under Art 21 must be just, fair and reasonable - Art 14, 19 and 21 read together.
- Freedoms are not absolute: Art 19(2)-(6) allow reasonable restrictions for sovereignty and integrity, security of state, public order, decency or morality, contempt, defamation, incitement.
- Equality: formal equality (same treatment by law) vs substantive equality (removing real disadvantages, e.g. through reservations); natural vs socially produced inequality (Rousseau).
- Dimensions of equality: political (equal vote, equal citizenship), social (no caste or gender discrimination), economic (reducing gaps in wealth and opportunity).
- Art 14: 'equality before law' (British concept) and 'equal protection of laws' (American concept); reasonable classification allowed if based on intelligible differentia with a rational nexus to the object.
- Art 15 (no discrimination), Art 16 (equal opportunity in public employment), Art 17 (untouchability abolished), Art 18 (titles abolished) complete the right to equality.
- Justice: Plato saw it as each class doing its own function; John Rawls, 'A Theory of Justice' (1971), proposed the 'veil of ignorance' and the difference principle - inequalities are fair only if they benefit the least advantaged.
- Robert Nozick (libertarian) argued for an entitlement theory and a minimal state - contrast with Rawls's redistributive liberalism.
- Social justice in India: Preamble promises social, economic and political justice; DPSP Arts 38, 39 and 46 direct the state to reduce inequality and protect weaker sections.
- Indra Sawhney (1992) upheld OBC reservation with a 50% ceiling and the creamy-layer exclusion; Janhit Abhiyan (2022) upheld the 103rd Amendment's 10% EWS quota.
- Rights: claims recognised by society and enforced by the state; natural rights theory (John Locke - life, liberty, property), legal rights, moral rights and human rights (UDHR adopted 10 Dec 1948).
- Part III (Arts 12-35) Fundamental Rights are justiciable; Ambedkar called Art 32 the 'heart and soul' of the Constitution; right to property removed from Part III by the 44th Amendment (1978) - now a legal right under Art 300A.
- Rights and duties are correlated: one person's right is another's duty and the state's obligation; Harold Laski linked rights to the conditions needed for a person to be at their best.
- Fundamental Duties (Part IVA, Art 51A) added by the 42nd Amendment (1976) on the Swaran Singh Committee's advice, inspired by the USSR constitution; 11th duty (education of child aged 6-14) added by the 86th Amendment (2002); duties are non-justiciable.

## Punjab angle

- Sikh Gurus' institutions of langar, pangat and sangat rejected caste hierarchy - a ready example of social equality from Punjab's tradition.
- Punjab has the highest proportion of Scheduled Castes among states (31.9%, Census 2011), making social-justice policy (reservation, scholarships, land rights) central to state politics.

## Deep dive

### Thinkers and key texts (thinker — work — idea)

- Thomas Hobbes — Leviathan (1651) — liberty is the absence of external impediments; people surrender natural rights to an absolute sovereign in return for security.
- John Locke — Two Treatises of Government (1689) — natural rights to life, liberty and estate; government rests on consent; people may resist a government that breaks its trust.
- J.J. Rousseau — The Social Contract (1762) — opens with "man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains"; the general will; a citizen may be "forced to be free" (a line later critics used against positive liberty).
- J.S. Mill — On Liberty (1859) — besides the harm principle, warns against the "tyranny of the majority" and defends free speech because silenced opinions may be true, partly true, or keep truth alive.
- T.H. Green — Lectures on the Principles of Political Obligation (1882) — positive freedom as the real capacity to do something worth doing; the state should remove hindrances (ignorance, ill-health, drink).
- R.H. Tawney — Equality (1931) — mocked equal opportunity without equal conditions as a "tadpole philosophy"; argued for equal consideration and social provision.
- F.A. Hayek — The Road to Serfdom (1944) and The Constitution of Liberty (1960) — liberty as absence of coercion; social justice as a mirage in a market order.
- John Rawls — A Theory of Justice (1971) — two principles: (1) equal basic liberties with lexical priority; (2) fair equality of opportunity plus the difference principle (maximin).
- Robert Nozick — Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974) — entitlement theory with three parts: justice in acquisition, in transfer, and rectification; taxation for redistribution likened to forced labour.
- Ronald Dworkin — Taking Rights Seriously (1977) — rights as "trumps" over collective goals; equality of resources.
- Michael Walzer — Spheres of Justice (1983) — complex equality: each social good (money, office, education) has its own distributive principle.
- Amartya Sen — Development as Freedom (1999), The Idea of Justice (2009) — capability approach; contrasts niti (rules, institutions) with nyaya (realised justice); matsyanyaya as the injustice to avoid.
- Philip Pettit — Republicanism (1997) — freedom as non-domination, a third concept beyond Berlin's two.
- B.R. Ambedkar — Annihilation of Caste (1936) and the 25 Nov 1949 speech — liberty, equality and fraternity as an inseparable trinity; political democracy must rest on social democracy.
- Karl Marx — Critique of the Gotha Programme (1875) — "from each according to his ability, to each according to his needs"; bourgeois rights seen as formal.

### Concepts and distinctions the examiner tests

- Liberty vs licence: liberty is freedom within rules that protect others; licence is unrestrained freedom.
- Aristotle (Nicomachean Ethics): distributive justice gives to each in proportion to merit; corrective (rectificatory) justice restores balance after a wrong; "treat equals equally and unequals unequally".
- Procedural justice (fair rules, e.g. Nozick) vs substantive/distributive justice (fair outcomes, e.g. Rawls); retributive vs restorative justice in criminal law.
- Equality of opportunity vs equality of outcome; luck egalitarianism (Dworkin, G.A. Cohen) compensates for unchosen bad luck but not for choices.
- Positive discrimination (affirmative action) aims at substantive equality; critics call it reverse discrimination.
- Hohfeld (1913/1919) analysed legal rights into four incidents: claim, liberty (privilege), power and immunity, each with a correlative.
- Will (choice) theory of rights (H.L.A. Hart) vs interest theory (Joseph Raz).
- Karel Vasak (1977): three generations of human rights — civil-political (liberty), socio-economic (equality), solidarity rights such as development and environment (fraternity).
- Jeremy Bentham called natural rights "nonsense upon stilts" (Anarchical Fallacies); legal positivists see rights as creations of law.
- Gandhi: "the true source of rights is duty" — duty-centred view behind Part IVA.
- Rawls's original position: parties behind the veil of ignorance would choose maximin because they do not know their place in society.
- Communitarian critique (Sandel, MacIntyre, Taylor): Rawls's "unencumbered self" ignores community, tradition and shared goods.

### Constitutional translation — cases beyond the basics (case — year — principle)

- A.K. Gopalan (1950) — read Arts 19 and 21 as separate silos; this view was rejected by R.C. Cooper (1970) and Maneka Gandhi (1978).
- State of West Bengal v Anwar Ali Sarkar (1952) — reasonable classification test: intelligible differentia plus rational nexus.
- E.P. Royappa (1974) — arbitrariness is the antithesis of equality; Art 14 also forbids arbitrary state action.
- State of Madras v Champakam Dorairajan (1951) — struck down communal G.O.; led to Art 15(4) by the 1st Amendment.
- State of Kerala v N.M. Thomas (1976) — Art 16(4) is a facet of 16(1), i.e. reservation is part of equality, not an exception.
- State of Punjab v Davinder Singh (1 Aug 2024, 7 judges, 6:1) — states may sub-classify Scheduled Castes for reservation; overruled E.V. Chinnaiah (2004). Origin: Punjab's 2006 law giving first preference to Balmikis and Mazhabi Sikhs within the SC quota.
- Olga Tellis (1985) — right to livelihood is part of Art 21; eviction of pavement dwellers needs fair procedure.
- K.S. Puttaswamy (2017, 9 judges) — privacy is a fundamental right; tests of legality, legitimate aim and proportionality.
- Navtej Singh Johar (2018) — consensual same-sex relations decriminalised; Joseph Shine (2018) — adultery (Sec 497 IPC) struck down.
- Shreya Singhal (2015) — Sec 66A IT Act struck down as vague; Anuradha Bhasin (2020) — speech and trade over the internet protected; indefinite shutdowns not allowed.
- Kaushal Kishor (2023, 5 judges) — Arts 19 and 21 can be enforced against some non-state actors; no extra grounds of restriction beyond Art 19(2).
- Vishaka (1997) — workplace sexual harassment guidelines → POSH Act 2013; NALSA (2014) — third-gender recognition → Transgender Persons Act 2019.
- Common Cause (2018) — right to die with dignity includes living wills (passive euthanasia); Supriyo Chakraborty (2023) — no fundamental right to marry; same-sex marriage left to Parliament.

### Rights in statute — laws that give effect to ideals (Act — year — what it secures)

- Protection of Civil Rights Act 1955 (renamed 1976) — punishes untouchability, enforcing Art 17.
- SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act 1989 — special courts; 2018 amendment restored no anticipatory bail after Subhash Kashinath Mahajan (2018) diluted it.
- Protection of Human Rights Act 1993 — NHRC and State Human Rights Commissions (Punjab SHRC set up 1997).
- Legal Services Authorities Act 1987 — NALSA, Lok Adalats; gives effect to Art 39A.
- RTI Act 2005 — right to information drawn from Art 19(1)(a); the DPDP Act 2023 amended Sec 8(1)(j) on personal information.
- MGNREGA 2005 (right to work, Art 41), RTE Act 2009 (Art 21A), National Food Security Act 2013 (right to food).
- Rights of Persons with Disabilities Act 2016 — 21 disabilities; 4% reservation in government jobs.
- Code on Wages 2019 subsumed the Equal Remuneration Act 1976 (Art 39(d) equal pay).
- Punjab Right to Service Act 2011 — time-bound delivery of notified services with a Right to Service Commission; Punjab was among the early states with such a law.
- Punjab Scheduled Castes and Backward Classes (Reservation in Services) Act 2006 — 50% of SC vacancies offered first to Balmikis and Mazhabi Sikhs (validated by the Davinder Singh ruling, 2024).

### Punjab lens — equality and justice in practice

- Guru Nanak taught the equality of all humans regardless of caste or sex; Guru Amar Das made langar compulsory before an audience ("pehle pangat, phir sangat") and opposed sati and purdah.
- Ad Dharm movement (1926, Mangu Ram Mugowalia, Doaba) — Dalit assertion for a separate religious identity and dignity.
- Gurdwara Reform Movement (1920–25) led to the Sikh Gurdwaras Act 1925 — community control of shrines through an elected SGPC.
- Bhagat Singh and the HSRA (1928) linked freedom to a socialist republic; his essay "Why I Am an Atheist" (1930) is a classic of rational liberty.
- Land reforms: PEPSU Tenancy and Agricultural Lands Act 1955 and Punjab Land Reforms Act 1972 (ceiling 17.5 standard acres for a family).
- Punjab Village Common Lands (Regulation) Act 1961 and its rules reserve one-third of panchayat land leased for cultivation for Scheduled Caste bidders — the basis of Dalit land-rights struggles in Sangrur and Malwa.
- Punjab State Commission for Scheduled Castes (statutory) and the national Commission handle caste-discrimination complaints in the state with the highest SC share.
- Free legal aid through the Punjab State Legal Services Authority and regular Lok Adalats — an applied example of social justice.

### PPSC/UPSC patterns and traps

- Thinker–quote matching: "nonsense upon stilts" (Bentham), "tadpole philosophy" (Tawney), "rights as trumps" (Dworkin), "complex equality" (Walzer), "non-domination" (Pettit).
- Rights available to all persons (Arts 14, 20, 21, 25) vs only citizens (Arts 15, 16, 19, 29, 30) — a favourite statement question.
- Arts 15(2), 17, 23 and 24 can be enforced against private persons too; Art 14 binds only the State.
- Fundamental Duties carry no penalty in the Constitution; they are enforced through laws such as the Prevention of Insults to National Honour Act 1971. The Verma Committee (1999) listed such laws.
- Art 51A(k) (added 2002) places a duty on parents/guardians, not on the child.
- Right to property is now a constitutional right under Art 300A; the SC has also called it a human right (Vidya Devi, 2020).
- Davinder Singh (2024): some judges suggested a creamy layer for SC/STs, but that was not part of the binding order — do not mark it as settled law.
- "Justice — social, economic and political" in the Preamble is inspired by the Russian Revolution; liberty, equality, fraternity by the French Revolution.
