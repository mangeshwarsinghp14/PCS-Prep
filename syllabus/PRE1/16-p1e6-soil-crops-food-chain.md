## Short notes

- Soil forms by weathering of parent rock plus organic matter; soil-forming factors are parent material, climate (most important active factor), relief, organisms and time.
- Soil profile horizons: O (organic litter), A (topsoil, humus-rich), B (subsoil, accumulation), C (weathered parent material), then bedrock.
- ICAR groups Indian soils into eight types: alluvial, black, red and yellow, laterite, arid, saline, peaty and forest soils.
- Alluvial soils are the most widespread (about 40% of India), in the northern plains and deltas; khadar is new, fertile floodplain soil, bhangar is older with kankar nodules; generally rich in potash, poor in phosphorus.
- Black (regur) soils of the Deccan basalt region (Maharashtra, Gujarat, MP) are clayey, retain moisture, crack when dry ('self-ploughing'), rich in lime, iron, magnesia and potash - ideal for cotton.
- Red soils owe their colour to iron oxide (yellow when hydrated); laterite soils form under high heat and heavy rain by intense leaching, are rich in iron and aluminium oxides, and suit cashew and tea.
- Arid soils are sandy, saline and low in humus; saline/alkaline soils (reh, kallar, usar) form where evaporation and poor drainage concentrate salts, often after over-irrigation, and are reclaimed with gypsum.
- Peaty soils (Kerala, Sundarbans) are rich in organic matter; forest soils vary with altitude and are acidic at higher levels.
- Erosion types: sheet, rill, gully (ravines/badlands of the Chambal) and wind erosion; conservation by contour ploughing, terracing, strip cropping, shelter belts, mulching and check dams.
- Soil Health Card Scheme (2015) gives farmers nutrient status and fertiliser advice; World Soil Day is 5 December.
- Kharif crops (sown with monsoon, June-July; harvested September-October): rice, maize, cotton, jowar, bajra, soybean, groundnut, arhar.
- Rabi crops (sown October-December; harvested March-April): wheat, barley, gram, mustard, peas; zaid crops (short summer season): watermelon, muskmelon, cucumber, moong, fodder.
- Green Revolution (mid-1960s): high-yielding dwarf wheat varieties (from Norman Borlaug's Mexican work), led in India by M.S. Swaminathan, with irrigation, fertilisers and assured procurement - first in Punjab, Haryana and western UP.
- Biological nitrogen fixation: Rhizobium in root nodules of legumes, free-living Azotobacter, blue-green algae (Anabaena, Nostoc) and Azolla in paddy; crop rotation with pulses restores soil nitrogen.
- Food chain: producers (green plants) to primary consumers (herbivores) to secondary and tertiary consumers; decomposers (bacteria, fungi) recycle nutrients; interlinked chains form a food web.
- Grazing food chains start with green plants; detritus food chains start with dead organic matter.
- Ten percent law (Lindeman): only about 10% of energy passes to the next trophic level, so food chains rarely exceed 4-5 levels.
- Ecological pyramids: the energy pyramid is always upright; biomass pyramid can be inverted in oceans (phytoplankton); number pyramid can be inverted (a single tree supporting many insects); toxins like DDT biomagnify up the chain.

## Punjab angle

- Punjab's soils are mostly alluvial; saline-sodic soils and waterlogging affect parts of the south-west (Muktsar, Fazilka, Faridkot), while the Kandi belt along the Shivaliks suffers soil erosion.
- Punjab follows an intensive wheat-paddy rotation and contributes a large share of wheat and rice to the central pool despite having about 1.5% of India's area.
- Crop diversification efforts promote maize, cotton, basmati, pulses and horticulture (kinnow in Abohar-Fazilka); direct-seeded rice is incentivised to save water.
- Punjab Agricultural University, Ludhiana (1962) played a central role in spreading Green Revolution technology.

## How PPSC asks it

Questions match soils with features and regions, crops with seasons, nitrogen fixers with organisms, and test pyramid/10% law concepts. Memorise one distinctive property and one crop for each soil type.

## Deep dive

### Soil science beyond NCERT

- Texture: sand, silt and clay proportions — loam (balanced mix) is ideal for most crops; clayey black soils hold water, sandy arid soils drain fast.
- Soil reaction: acidic soils (pH below about 6.5; North-East, Kerala laterites, hill forests) are corrected with lime; sodic soils (pH above about 8.5, high exchangeable sodium) are corrected with gypsum.
- USDA taxonomy shares for India (NCERT table): Inceptisols about 40% and Entisols about 28% (mostly alluvium), Alfisols about 14% (red soils), Vertisols about 8.5% (black soils), Aridisols about 4%, Ultisols about 2.5%, Mollisols under 1%.
- Nutrients: primary N, P, K; secondary calcium, magnesium, sulphur; micronutrients zinc, iron, manganese, copper, boron, molybdenum, chlorine, nickel — zinc deficiency is the most widespread micronutrient problem in Indian soils, including Punjab.
- Recommended NPK use ratio is about 4:2:1; Punjab's use is heavily tilted to nitrogen (urea), which lowers nutrient-use efficiency and raises nitrate leaching.
- Soil organic carbon is low (often under 0.5%) in intensively cropped Punjab soils, partly because residue is burnt rather than returned.
- Nano urea (IFFCO, 2021) and nano DAP (2023) are liquid fertilisers promoted to cut bulk fertiliser use.
- World Soil Day is 5 December (FAO); 2015 was the International Year of Soils.

### Cropping systems and agro-climatic planning

- The rice–wheat system covers about 10 million ha of the Indo-Gangetic plains; cotton–wheat dominates south-west Punjab and Haryana; maize–wheat in the hills and Kandi belt.
- Planning Commission (1988) divided India into 15 agro-climatic regions; Punjab, Haryana, Delhi and Ganganagar (Rajasthan) form the Trans-Gangetic Plains Region; ICAR's NARP uses 127 smaller agro-climatic zones.
- Crop seasons are linked to day length and temperature: kharif crops are mostly short-day and heat-loving; rabi crops need cool growth and warm, dry ripening.
- Millets: 2023 was the International Year of Millets at India's proposal; millets are branded 'Shree Anna' and need far less water than paddy.
- Cropping intensity = gross cropped area ÷ net sown area × 100; Punjab's is close to 190% because almost all its net sown area is irrigated.
- Multiple cropping, intercropping, relay cropping and crop rotation are different — rotation means changing crops on the same field over seasons.

### Ecology concepts beyond the NCERT food chain

- Raymond Lindeman (1942, Cedar Bog Lake) gave the trophic-dynamic concept behind the 10% rule; actual ecological efficiency ranges roughly 5–20%.
- Gross primary productivity is total photosynthesis; net primary productivity = GPP minus plant respiration. Per unit area, estuaries, swamps, coral reefs and tropical rainforests are most productive; deserts and the open ocean least.
- Biogeochemical cycles: gaseous (carbon, nitrogen, oxygen) versus sedimentary (phosphorus, sulphur); phosphorus has no significant atmospheric phase.
- Nitrogen cycle steps: fixation (Rhizobium, Azotobacter, cyanobacteria, lightning, the industrial Haber process), ammonification, nitrification (Nitrosomonas: ammonia to nitrite; Nitrobacter: nitrite to nitrate) and denitrification (Pseudomonas, Thiobacillus).
- Ecological succession: pioneer species (lichens on bare rock) give way to mosses, grasses, shrubs and a climax community; hydrosere starts in water, xerosere on dry rock; secondary succession follows disturbance of an existing community.
- Interactions: mutualism (lichens, mycorrhiza), commensalism (orchid on a mango branch, barnacles on whales), parasitism, predation, competition (Gause's competitive exclusion), amensalism (Penicillium inhibiting bacteria).
- Niche (Grinnell, Elton) is a species' role; Liebig's law of the minimum — growth is limited by the scarcest resource; Shelford's law of tolerance — both too little and too much limit growth.
- Biomes: tundra, taiga, temperate grasslands (prairies, pampas, steppes, veld, downs), savanna, Mediterranean scrub, tropical rainforest, deserts.

### Soil and land degradation — programmes and global commitments

- UNCCD (1994; India party since 1996): India hosted COP14 in New Delhi (2019) and committed to restoring 26 million ha of degraded land by 2030 (also its Bonn Challenge pledge).
- UNCCD COP17 at Ulaanbaatar, Mongolia, closed on 28 August 2026; it launched a Rangelands Flagship Initiative worth about US$1.2 billion across 45 projects during the International Year of Rangelands and Pastoralists (2026).
- Aravalli Green Wall Project (2023) aims at a 5 km green buffer along the Aravallis across Gujarat, Rajasthan, Haryana and Delhi.
- Watershed Development Component of PMKSY (successor to the Integrated Watershed Management Programme) treats ridge-to-valley land; National Mission for Sustainable Agriculture (2014-15) includes soil health management.
- Organic and natural farming: Paramparagat Krishi Vikas Yojana (2015, cluster-based organic farming) and the National Mission on Natural Farming (approved November 2024, about ₹2,481 crore).
- Saline and sodic soils: the Central Soil Salinity Research Institute (Karnal, 1969) developed gypsum-based reclamation, sub-surface drainage and salt-tolerant 'CSR' rice and wheat varieties.
- Punjab: waterlogging and salinity in the south-west (Muktsar, Fazilka, Faridkot) are tackled with sub-surface drainage and drainage-line clearing; Kandi belt erosion with check dams and closure under the Punjab Land Preservation Act 1900.

### Punjab farm-soil and crop file

- Punjab's soils are mainly alluvial (Entisols and Inceptisols) — sandy loam in the south-west, loam to clay loam in central districts, with sand dunes once common in Bathinda–Fazilka.
- Nearly all net sown area is irrigated, mostly by tube-wells; wheat and paddy each occupy over 30 lakh ha in their seasons.
- Regional specialisation: kinnow orchards in Abohar–Fazilka, cotton in the Malwa south-west (Bathinda, Mansa, Muktsar), sugarcane in Doaba, disease-free seed potato from Jalandhar–Hoshiarpur, basmati along the Majha belt.
- Pest history to quote: whitefly devastation of cotton (2015), pink bollworm resistance to Bt cotton (2021–22), and the 2020 locust alert.
- Residue management machines developed at PAU Ludhiana — Happy Seeder and the cheaper Surface Seeder (2022) — let farmers sow wheat without burning paddy straw.
- Punjab follows ICAR soil testing with Soil Health Cards; zinc and sulphur deficiencies are common in heavily cropped blocks.

### Traps and PPSC patterns

- Regur = black soil; khadar = new alluvium, bhangar = old alluvium with kankar; 'laterite' comes from the Latin 'later' (brick).
- Acidic soil → lime; alkaline/sodic soil → gypsum (frequently swapped in options).
- Nitrosomonas makes nitrite; Nitrobacter makes nitrate — a classic swap.
- Energy pyramid is always upright; biomass pyramid can be inverted in water; number pyramid can be inverted in a tree ecosystem.
- PPSC 2018 asked which crops grow in equatorial regions and about integrated watershed benefits; PPSC 2025 asked rice requirements — revise crop climate needs.
- Detritus food chains (starting with dead matter) move more energy than grazing chains in forests — do not assume grazing chains dominate everywhere.

**1.** Which soil is known as 'self-ploughing' because it develops deep cracks when dry?

(a) Black (regur) soil  
(b) Alluvial soil  
(c) Red soil  
(d) Laterite soil

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) Black (regur) soil** — Black soil swells when wet and shrinks and cracks when dry, aerating itself.

**2.** Saline and alkaline soils are commonly reclaimed by applying:

(a) Potash  
(b) Urea  
(c) Gypsum  
(d) Lime

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Gypsum** — Gypsum (calcium sulphate) replaces sodium in sodic soils, which is then leached away.

</details>

**3.** Consider:  
1. The pyramid of energy can be inverted in some aquatic ecosystems.  
2. Only about 10% of energy is transferred from one trophic level to the next.  
Which is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) 2 only** — Statement 1 is wrong: energy is lost as heat at every step, so the pyramid of energy is always upright; it is the pyramid of biomass that can be inverted in some aquatic ecosystems. Statement 2 is correct: Lindeman's 10% law says only about 10% of energy passes to the next trophic level.

</details>

**4.** Which of the following is a rabi crop?

(a) Maize  
(b) Groundnut  
(c) Mustard  
(d) Cotton

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Mustard** — Mustard is sown in October-November; the others are kharif crops.

</details>

**5.** Nitrogen-fixing Rhizobium bacteria live in the root nodules of:

(a) Cereals like wheat  
(b) Grasses  
(c) Leguminous plants  
(d) Conifers

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Leguminous plants** — Rhizobium lives symbiotically in nodules of pulses and other legumes.

</details>

**6.** Laterite soil is formed mainly under conditions of:

(a) Deposition of moraine by glaciers in mountains  
(b) Silt deposition by rivers in floodplains  
(c) High temperature and heavy rain, causing leaching  
(d) Low rainfall and high evaporation in arid areas

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) High temperature and heavy rain, causing leaching** — Heavy rain leaches silica and bases, leaving iron and aluminium oxides.

</details>

**7.** Consider the following statements: 1. The phosphorus cycle has no significant atmospheric phase. 2. Nitrosomonas bacteria convert nitrite into nitrate. Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only

**8.** Consider the following statements: 1. Inceptisols and Entisols together cover the largest share of India's soils in USDA taxonomy. 2. Vertisols broadly correspond to black cotton soils. 3. Acidic soils are reclaimed by adding gypsum. Which of the statements given above are correct?

(a) Mutualism
