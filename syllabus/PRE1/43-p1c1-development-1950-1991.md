## Short notes

- At Independence India had a stagnant, agrarian colonial economy with low productivity, weak industry and mass poverty; the chosen path was a mixed economy with state-led planning.
- Pre-1947 blueprints: Bombay Plan (1944, industrialists), Gandhian Plan (S.N. Agarwal), People's Plan (M.N. Roy).
- Planning Commission set up on 15 March 1950 by a Cabinet resolution (non-constitutional), chaired by the PM; National Development Council formed in 1952.
- Industrial Policy Resolution 1948 declared a mixed economy; IPR 1956 (the 'economic constitution') made three schedules - A: 17 industries exclusively for the state, B: 12 progressively state-owned, C: rest for private sector.
- Industries (Development and Regulation) Act 1951 introduced industrial licensing - the base of the later 'licence-permit-quota raj'.
- Mahalanobis model (Second Plan) prioritised heavy and capital-goods industries; the public sector was to hold the 'commanding heights' of the economy.
- Public sector steel plants: Bhilai (Soviet help), Rourkela (West German), Durgapur (British); Bokaro later with Soviet help.
- Inward-looking trade: import substitution, high tariffs, quantitative restrictions; FERA 1973 controlled foreign exchange; MRTP Act 1969 curbed concentration of economic power.
- Reservation of products for small-scale industry (from 1967) to protect employment and village industries.
- Land reforms: abolition of zamindari, tenancy reforms and land ceilings - zamindari abolition largely succeeded, ceilings much less; Vinoba Bhave's Bhoodan movement began in 1951.
- Green Revolution (mid-1960s): high-yielding varieties (Norman Borlaug, M.S. Swaminathan), irrigation and fertiliser; Food Corporation of India and Agricultural Prices Commission set up in 1965; MSP-based procurement.
- RBI nationalised on 1 Jan 1949; Imperial Bank became State Bank of India in 1955; life insurance nationalised (LIC) in 1956; general insurance nationalised in the early 1970s (GIC 1972).
- Bank nationalisation: 14 banks with deposits above Rs 50 crore nationalised on 19 July 1969; 6 more on 15 April 1980; aims - credit to agriculture, small industry and rural areas.
- Banking expansion tools: priority-sector lending, Lead Bank Scheme (1969), Regional Rural Banks (1975), NABARD (1982).
- Growth averaged about 3.5% a year from 1950 to 1980 - the 'Hindu rate of growth' (term coined by economist Raj Krishna).
- Other milestones: rupee devaluation of 1966, 'Garibi Hatao' (1971), Twenty Point Programme (1975), partial liberalisation and faster growth under Rajiv Gandhi in the 1980s.
- 1991 crisis: high fiscal deficits, rising external debt, Gulf War oil shock and remittance loss; forex reserves fell to a few weeks of imports; gold was pledged abroad and India turned to the IMF, triggering the 1991 reforms.
- Achievements of the period: diversified industrial base, food self-sufficiency, institutions (IITs, ISRO, public sector); weaknesses: inefficiency, slow growth, low exports, persistent poverty.

## Punjab angle

- Punjab was the heartland of the Green Revolution; Punjab Agricultural University, Ludhiana (1962) spread HYV wheat and rice.
- Bhakra-Nangal on the Sutlej, dedicated in 1963 and called a 'temple of modern India' by Nehru, transformed Punjab's irrigation and power.
- Punjab National Bank, founded in Lahore in 1894, was among the 14 banks nationalised in 1969.

## Deep dive

### Detailed timeline 1947–1991 (year — event)

- 1948 — Industrial Policy Resolution (6 April) creates four categories: state monopoly (arms, atomic energy, railways); six basic industries where only the state starts new units (coal, iron and steel, aircraft, shipbuilding, telecom equipment, mineral oils); 18 regulated industries; the rest private.
- 1949 — RBI nationalised (1 January); Banking Companies Act (later Banking Regulation Act) gives RBI supervisory powers.
- 1951 — Industries (Development and Regulation) Act: licence needed to start, expand or relocate a unit; First Plan begins.
- 1952–53 — National Development Council formed (August 1952); Air Corporations Act 1953 nationalises airlines into Indian Airlines and Air India.
- 1955–56 — Imperial Bank becomes SBI (1 July 1955); Avadi Congress adopts the 'socialistic pattern of society'; IPR 1956 (30 April); 245 life insurers merged into LIC (1 September 1956).
- 1957–58 — Foreign-exchange crisis during the Second Plan leads to tight import licensing and exchange control.
- 1964–65 — IDBI and UTI set up (1964); Monopolies Inquiry Commission (K.C. Das Gupta) reports (1965); FCI and Agricultural Prices Commission start work (1965).
- 1966 — Rupee devalued by 57.5% (from Rs 4.76 to Rs 7.50 per US dollar) under US/World Bank pressure after war and drought; HYV Programme launched.
- 1969 — 14 banks nationalised (19 July); MRTP Act passed (in force 1970); Lead Bank Scheme starts.
- 1970–73 — R.C. Cooper case strikes down the first bank nationalisation law on compensation grounds, so a new Act follows (1970); general insurance nationalised (1972, effective 1973); coal mines nationalised (1971–73); FERA 1973 forces most foreign firms to cut equity to 40%.
- 1975–78 — Regional Rural Banks (2 October 1975); IBM and Coca-Cola leave India (1977) rather than dilute equity; District Industries Centres set up (1978) under the 1977 Industrial Policy Statement.
- 1979–81 — Second oil shock and drought: GDP shrinks about 5% in 1979-80 (old series); India takes an IMF Extended Fund Facility of about SDR 5 billion (1981).
- 1982–88 — NABARD and EXIM Bank (1982); Long-Term Fiscal Policy (1985) and MODVAT (1986); broad-banding of licences and partial de-licensing under Rajiv Gandhi; SEBI set up as a non-statutory body (1988).
- 1990–91 — Gulf War raises oil import bill and cuts Gulf remittances; political instability; reserves collapse, setting up the July 1991 reforms.

### The 'licence–permit' regime: instruments and committees

- Instruments: industrial licensing (IDRA 1951), MRTP asset thresholds, import licensing and quantitative restrictions, FERA exchange control, price and distribution controls (steel, cement, sugar), SSI reservation, capital-issue control (Controller of Capital Issues).
- Hazari Report (1967) — licensing favoured big business houses and did not steer investment to plan priorities.
- Industrial Licensing Policy Inquiry Committee (Subimal Dutt, 1969) — confirmed concentration of licences; led to the 'joint sector' idea and tighter MRTP control.
- Mahalanobis Committee on Distribution of Income and Levels of Living (1964) — found that planning had increased concentration of wealth.
- Monopolies Inquiry Commission (K.C. Das Gupta, 1965) — basis for the MRTP Act 1969.
- Abid Hussain Committee on trade policy (1984) — early case for export promotion and fewer import controls.
- Sukhamoy Chakravarty Committee (1985) — reviewed the monetary system; advised monetary targeting and flexible interest rates.
- Effect: low competition, under-used capacity, poor quality, rent-seeking and the 'Hindu rate of growth' of about 3.5% to 1980.

### Nationalisation and directed credit — what changed

- 1969 criterion: banks with deposits of Rs 50 crore or more (14 banks); 1980 criterion: deposits of Rs 200 crore or more (6 banks).
- New Bank of India was merged with Punjab National Bank in 1993 — the only merger of two nationalised banks before the 2019–20 consolidation.
- Priority-sector lending target of 40% of net bank credit was fixed in 1980 (agriculture, small industry, weaker sections).
- Branch expansion rule (1977): a bank had to open four branches in unbanked rural areas for each new branch in a banked area; rural branches grew sharply.
- Financial repression: high CRR and SLR (SLR touched 38.5% around 1990), administered interest rates and cross-subsidised lending.
- Nationalisation of oil marketing: Esso (1974), Burmah Shell (1976), Caltex (1977) became the base of HPCL and BPCL.
- Gains: rural savings mobilised, credit to farmers and small units; costs: weak profitability, political lending and rising bad loans by the late 1980s.

### Agriculture and food policy 1950–91 (beyond NCERT)

- 1952 — Community Development Programme; 1953 — National Extension Service.
- 1960–61 — Intensive Agricultural District Programme (IADP) in seven districts including Ludhiana; 1964 — Intensive Agricultural Areas Programme.
- 1965–66 — Food Minister C. Subramaniam backs import of Mexican dwarf wheat seed (Lerma Rojo 64, Sonora 64); HYV Programme from kharif 1966.
- 1965 — Agricultural Prices Commission (renamed CACP in 1985) and FCI set up to run MSP procurement and buffer stocks.
- 1960s–70s — PL-480 food aid from the US ends as output rises; India becomes broadly self-sufficient in cereals by the late 1970s.
- 1970s — Operation Flood (1970) for dairy; Command Area Development Programme (1974) for canal-irrigated areas.
- Green Revolution limits: concentrated in wheat and in irrigated north-west India; bypassed rain-fed areas, pulses and oilseeds; raised inequality between regions.

### Anatomy of the 1991 crisis (with numbers)

- Fiscal: the Centre's fiscal deficit was about 8% of GDP in 1990-91; interest payments ate a rising share of revenue.
- External: current account deficit about 3% of GDP; external debt about US$ 84 billion with heavy short-term borrowing and NRI deposits.
- Reserves fell to about US$ 1 billion by mid-1991 — roughly two weeks of imports.
- Inflation reached double digits (WPI over 13% in 1991-92).
- Triggers: Gulf War oil spike, loss of Gulf remittances and evacuation of workers, rating downgrades, NRI deposit outflow, collapse of rupee trade with the USSR.
- Emergency steps: about 20 tonnes of gold sold/leased via SBI (May 1991) and about 47 tonnes pledged with the Bank of England and Bank of Japan (July 1991).
- Rupee devalued by roughly 18–19% in two steps (1 and 3 July 1991); IMF stand-by credit followed later in 1991.

### Debates and historiography

- B.R. Shenoy's dissent (1955) on the Second Plan warned that deficit-financed heavy industry would cause inflation and BoP stress.
- Vakil–Brahmananda 'wage-goods' model (1956) argued for food and consumer goods first to absorb labour — an alternative to Mahalanobis.
- C. Rajagopalachari and the Swatantra Party (1959) attacked the 'licence-permit raj'.
- Bhagwati and Desai, 'India: Planning for Industrialization' (1970), documented the inefficiency of licensing and import controls.
- Isher Judge Ahluwalia (1985) showed industrial stagnation from the mid-1960s; Raj Krishna coined 'Hindu rate of growth' (1978).
- Rodrik and Subramanian (2004) argued the 1980s growth pickup came from a pro-business shift before 1991, not only the 1991 reforms.
- Defence of planning: built a diversified industrial base, institutions (IITs, ISRO, CSIR) and food security under hostile global conditions.

### Punjab angle 1950–91

- Punjab Agricultural University, Ludhiana (1962) and the IADP district of Ludhiana made Punjab the testing ground for HYV wheat.
- Bhakra–Nangal (1963) and the canal network plus rural electrification by the Punjab State Electricity Board (1959) underpinned tubewell irrigation.
- Markfed (1954) and state agencies built the procurement and input-supply network that FCI later used.
- Punjab reorganisation on 1 November 1966 created present-day Punjab, Haryana and transferred hill areas to Himachal Pradesh.
- Punjab became the largest contributor of wheat to the central pool by the 1970s, with the highest per capita income among major states for much of the 1970s–80s.
- The 1980s militancy hurt industrial investment and contributed to the later relative decline of Punjab's growth.

### PPSC/UPSC traps and patterns

- Schedules A/B/C belong to IPR 1956, not IPR 1948 (which had four categories).
- 1969 nationalisation threshold Rs 50 crore; 1980 threshold Rs 200 crore — do not swap.
- The Planning Commission and NDC were both created by executive resolution, not by the Constitution or statute.
- LIC (1956) came from merging 245 companies; GIC (1972) from 107 general insurers grouped into four subsidiaries.
- FERA 1973 was a criminal law; its replacement FEMA came only in 1999.
- District Industries Centres belong to the 1977 policy (George Fernandes), not 1980.
- 'Hindu rate' refers to growth of about 3.5% in 1950–80, not to religion — coined by Raj Krishna.
