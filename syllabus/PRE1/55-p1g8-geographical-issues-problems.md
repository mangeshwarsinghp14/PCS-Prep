## Short notes

- This unit (NCERT) covers environmental pollution, urbanisation-related problems (slums, migration) and land degradation in India.
- Pollution types: water, air, land and noise; sources are point (factory outfall) or non-point (farm runoff, urban runoff).
- Water pollution: sewage, industrial effluents, agro-chemicals, cremation and religious offerings; the Water (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act 1974 created the Central and State Pollution Control Boards; Namami Gange (2014) for the Ganga.
- Air pollution: vehicles, industry, thermal power, construction dust, crop-residue burning; Air (Prevention and Control of Pollution) Act 1981; National Air Quality Index (2014); National Clean Air Programme (2019) for non-attainment cities.
- Delhi-NCR winter smog worsens due to temperature inversion, calm winds and stubble burning in Punjab and Haryana; tools include the Graded Response Action Plan and the Commission for Air Quality Management (2021).
- Noise pollution: measured in decibels (dB); Noise Pollution (Regulation and Control) Rules 2000 set zone-wise limits; silence zones near hospitals and schools.
- Land/solid-waste pollution: Solid Waste Management Rules 2016, Plastic Waste Management rules (ban on identified single-use plastics from 1 July 2022), E-waste Rules 2022, legacy landfill sites.
- Urbanisation problems: rural-urban migration driven by push factors creates slums (Dharavi in Mumbai is among Asia's largest); Census 2011 counted about 6.5 crore slum dwellers.
- Slum issues: poor housing, sanitation and water, health risks, informal jobs, crime and social exclusion; responses include PMAY-Urban, Swachh Bharat and in-situ slum redevelopment.
- Land degradation: natural (gully and ravine erosion, salinity, desertification) and human-induced (waterlogging from over-irrigation, mining, deforestation, overgrazing, industrial waste).
- About 29.7% of India's land area is under degradation or desertification (ISRO atlas, 2018-19); India aims to restore 26 million hectares of degraded land by 2030.
- Examples: ravines of the Chambal valley; salinity/alkalinity (reh, kallar) in canal-irrigated Punjab, Haryana and western UP; mining degradation in Jharkhand and Odisha.
- NCERT case study - Jhabua district (Madhya Pradesh): watershed management by Bhil communities restored degraded land through pasture development, tree planting and soil conservation.
- Natural hazards also feature here: floods (Assam, Bihar), droughts, cyclones (east coast), earthquakes (Himalaya), landslides; Disaster Management Act 2005 created NDMA; India follows the Sendai Framework (2015-2030).
- Climate-linked problems: heat waves, glacial lake outburst floods, erratic monsoon and cloudbursts in the Himalaya.

## Punjab angle

- Paddy stubble burning in Punjab after the kharif harvest is a major contributor to north Indian winter air pollution; in-situ (Happy Seeder, super-SMS) and ex-situ straw management are promoted.
- Groundwater contamination and heavy pesticide use in Malwa are linked to high cancer incidence (Bathinda region's so-called 'cancer train' to Bikaner).
- Buddha Nullah in Ludhiana carries industrial and domestic waste into the Sutlej, affecting downstream areas and the Harike wetland.
- The 2025 floods hit Punjab's agriculture hard (agri GSDP fell 2.5% in 2025-26), showing flood-risk vulnerability of riverine districts.

## How PPSC asks it

Questions cover pollution laws and their years, sources and effects of pollution types, the Jhabua case study and land degradation causes. Memorise Act years (1974, 1981, 1986, 2005) and Punjab-specific environmental problems.

## Deep dive

### Mapping list — mountain passes (pass — connects — state)

- Ladakh–J&K: Karakoram Pass (Ladakh–Xinjiang), Khardung La (Leh–Nubra), Zoji La (Srinagar–Kargil–Leh), Banihal (Jammu–Srinagar; Jawahar and newer tunnels), Pir Panjal (Mughal Road).
- Himachal: Rohtang (Kullu–Lahaul; bypassed by the Atal Tunnel), Baralacha La (Lahaul–Ladakh on the Manali–Leh road), Shipki La (Himachal–Tibet; the Sutlej enters India here).
- Uttarakhand: Lipulekh (Kailash–Mansarovar route; claimed by Nepal), Mana and Niti passes (to Tibet).
- Sikkim: Nathu La (Sikkim–Chumbi valley, border trade and Kailash yatra route) and Jelep La.
- Arunachal: Bum La (Tawang–Tibet), Bomdi La, Diphu (near the India–China–Myanmar trijunction), Pangsau (Arunachal–Myanmar, on the old Stilwell Road).
- Peninsula: Thal Ghat (Mumbai–Nashik) and Bhor Ghat (Mumbai–Pune) in the Western Ghats; Palghat gap (Kerala–Tamil Nadu); Shencottah gap (Kerala–Tamil Nadu).
- The Kailash Mansarovar Yatra resumed in 2025 through Lipulekh and Nathu La after a gap of several years.

### Mapping list — peaks, ranges and Punjab's physiography

- Peaks: K2 (8,611 m, in the PoK-administered Gilgit-Baltistan area claimed by India), Kangchenjunga (8,586 m; highest in India proper, Sikkim–Nepal), Nanda Devi (7,816 m; highest wholly within India), Anamudi (2,695 m; highest in peninsular India), Doddabetta (Nilgiris), Guru Shikhar (Mount Abu; highest in the Aravallis), Dhupgarh (Satpura), Mahendragiri (Eastern Ghats, Odisha), Saddle Peak (highest in the Andaman Islands).
- Barren Island (Andamans) is India's only active volcano; Narcondam is dormant.
- Himalayan divisions: Himadri (Greater), Himachal (Lesser, with Pir Panjal and Dhauladhar), Shiwaliks with flat 'duns' (Dehradun, Patli Dun); Purvanchal hills (Patkai, Naga, Mizo); Karewas of Kashmir (lacustrine deposits, saffron).
- Punjab physiography: Shivalik foothills in the north-east, the Kandi belt (gravelly piedmont with seasonal 'choes'), the Sirowal (springline) zone, the alluvial plain with 'bet' floodplains and 'dhaya' bluffs along rivers, and once-extensive sand dunes in the south-west.
- Punjab's doabs: Bari Doab (Beas–Ravi; Majha region), Bist or Jalandhar Doab (Beas–Sutlej; Doaba region); Malwa lies south of the Sutlej and Puadh around Ropar–Mohali.
- Across the border, Rachna Doab (Ravi–Chenab) and Chaj Doab (Chenab–Jhelum) complete the Panjab doab system.

### Contemporary geographical problems in India (with year)

- Land subsidence and fragile Himalayan development: Joshimath (2023) led to Supreme Court and expert reviews of Himalayan carrying capacity.
- Urban flooding: Mumbai (2005), Chennai (2015), Bengaluru (2022); the Yamuna at Delhi hit a record 208.66 m in July 2023.
- Heat stress: 2024 was India's warmest year since 1901 (IMD) and lightning killed over 1,300 people in 2025.
- Coastal erosion: NCCR's analysis for 1990–2018 found about a third of India's mainland coast eroding.
- Water scarcity in cities (Shimla 2018, Bengaluru 2024) and falling groundwater in the north-west.
- Development–ecology debates: Great Nicobar project, Char Dham road widening (allowed by the Supreme Court in 2021 for defence needs), mining in central Indian forests.
- Seismic risk policy: a 2025 BIS seismic code that added a new highest Zone VI was withdrawn in March 2026 over cost concerns, restoring the 2016 zoning (II–V).

### Punjab — selected issues with data

- Groundwater: CGWB's 2024 Punjab report put extraction at 156.87% of availability with 115 of 153 units over-exploited; 2025 figures (about 152%, 110 units) improved slightly as canal use grew.
- Floods 2025: on 2 September 2025 all 23 districts were declared flood-hit; about 1,400 villages and 3.5 lakh people were affected and crops on over 4 lakh acres were submerged at that point; Pong dam crossed its danger mark.
- 2025 relief: ₹20,000 per acre for crop loss, ₹4 lakh for families of the dead, a 'Jisda Khet Usdi Ret' policy letting farmers remove and sell sand dumped on their fields, and ₹1,600 crore central assistance announced on 9 September 2025.
- Waterlogging and salinity in the south-west (Muktsar, Fazilka, Faridkot) sit beside falling water tables in central Punjab — two opposite water problems in one state.
- Air: paddy-straw burning, brick kilns and vehicles; winter inversions trap smog over the plains.
- Land: Kandi erosion and choe floods; loss of forest and tree cover (456 sq km between ISFR 2019 and 2021).

### Answer-writing tips and traps

- For any 'issue' question, write causes → consequences → government response → way forward, with one Punjab example.
- Use a sketch map: doabs of Punjab, flood-hit districts or over-exploited blocks earn marks quickly.
- Trap: Nanda Devi, not Kangchenjunga, is the highest peak lying entirely within India.
- Trap: Shipki La is where the Sutlej enters India; Nathu La links Sikkim with Tibet.
- Trap: the Bari Doab lies between the Beas and Ravi; the Bist Doab between the Beas and Sutlej.
- PPSC 2021 asked Thal Ghat and 'glaciatic wetlands'; 2025 asked volcano–place and mountain-type matches — keep physical mapping lists ready.

**4.** Consider the statements: 1. Temperature inversion worsens winter air pollution in north-west India. 2. Noise levels are measured in decibels. Which is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Both 1 and 2** — Both are correct: winter inversion traps pollutants near the ground over the Indo-Gangetic plain, and noise is measured in decibels (dB). Nothing in either statement is overstated, so 'Both' is the key.

</details>

**(d) Jhabua district, Madhya Pradesh** — Jhabua's watershed programmes reversed land degradation.

**7.** Consider the following statements: 1. Shipki La is the pass through which the Sutlej enters India. 2. Nathu La connects Sikkim with Tibet's Chumbi valley. Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

</details>

**11.** In July 2023 the Yamuna at Delhi reached its highest recorded level of about:

(a) 212.5 m  
(b) 208.66 m  
(c) 205.33 m  
(d) 200.1 m

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) 208.66 m** — On 13 July 2023 the Yamuna at the Old Railway Bridge in Delhi touched about 208.66 m, breaking the earlier record of 207.49 m set in 1978. The 205.33 m option is the danger level, not the record.

</details>

**12.** According to NCCR's analysis for 1990–2018, roughly what share of India's mainland coastline is affected by erosion?

(a) About two-thirds  
(b) About 90%  
(c) About 5%  
(d) About one-third

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) About one-third** — NCCR's 1990-2018 assessment found about 33.6% of India's mainland coast eroding, roughly one-third; about 26% was accreting and the rest stable. 'Two-thirds' reverses the picture, since most of the coast was not eroding. Figures as of Nov 2026.

</details>
