## Short notes

- Old regime (ancien regime) society had three estates: clergy (First), nobility (Second) and commoners — peasants, workers, middle class (Third, about 97% of people).
- The First and Second Estates were exempt from most taxes; the Third Estate paid the taille (direct tax), tithes to the Church and feudal dues.
- Louis XVI (Bourbon) faced bankruptcy from costly wars, including help to the American revolutionaries, and from court extravagance; bread prices rose sharply.
- Enlightenment thinkers: John Locke (Two Treatises of Government), Rousseau (The Social Contract, general will), Montesquieu (The Spirit of the Laws, separation of powers), Voltaire.
- The Estates-General met in May 1789 for the first time since 1614; the Third Estate declared itself the National Assembly (17 June) and took the Tennis Court Oath (20 June 1789).
- Storming of the Bastille on 14 July 1789 — now France's national day; peasant revolts called the 'Great Fear' followed.
- On 4 August 1789 feudal privileges, tithes and serfdom were abolished; the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen was adopted on 26 August 1789.
- Women marched to Versailles (October 1789); Olympe de Gouges wrote the Declaration of the Rights of Woman (1791); French women won the vote by an ordinance of 21 April 1944 and first voted in 1945 (NCERT Class 9 gives 1946, the year of the new constitution).
- Constitution of 1791 created a constitutional monarchy, with voting limited to 'active citizens' who paid taxes.
- France became a republic in September 1792 (National Convention); Louis XVI was executed in January 1793 and Marie Antoinette in October 1793.
- Jacobins under Maximilien Robespierre ran the Reign of Terror (1793–94) with the guillotine; Robespierre himself was executed in July 1794.
- The Directory (1795–99) was unstable; Napoleon Bonaparte seized power in November 1799, became emperor in 1804, issued the Civil Code (1804) and was finally defeated at Waterloo (1815).
- Slogan: Liberty, Equality, Fraternity; the revolution introduced the tricolour, the metric system and the idea of citizenship.
- Slavery in French colonies was abolished in 1794, restored by Napoleon in 1802 and finally ended in 1848.
- The political terms 'left' and 'right' come from seating in the revolutionary assemblies; the Girondins were moderate, the Jacobins radical (supported by the sans-culottes).
- Legacy: end of feudalism, spread of nationalism, democracy and rights; it influenced Indian thinkers such as Raja Rammohun Roy, and Tipu Sultan is said to have planted a 'tree of liberty'.

## Punjab angle

- Former officers of Napoleon's army, Jean-Baptiste Ventura and Jean-François Allard, joined Maharaja Ranjit Singh in the 1820s and trained the European-style Fauj-i-Khas.
- Other European officers in Ranjit Singh's service included the Frenchman Claude Auguste Court (artillery) and the Italian Paolo Avitabile.

## Deep dive

### Detailed timeline, 1774–1804 (year — event)

- 1774 — Louis XVI becomes king; finance ministers Turgot (1774–76) and Necker (1777–81) fail to reform taxes.
- 1778–83 — France fights Britain in support of the American colonists; the war adds heavily to royal debt.
- 1787 — Calonne's Assembly of Notables refuses a land tax on the privileged; the 'aristocratic revolt' and Parlement of Paris demand the Estates-General.
- 1788 — Hailstorm and poor harvest; bread prices soar; the king agrees (August) to summon the Estates-General and recalls Necker.
- January 1789 — Abbé Sieyès publishes 'What is the Third Estate?'; communities draft cahiers de doléances (lists of grievances).
- 5 May 1789 — Estates-General opens at Versailles; dispute over voting by order (one vote per estate) versus by head.
- 17 and 20 June 1789 — Third Estate declares itself the National Assembly; Tennis Court Oath not to separate until a constitution is made; 9 July it becomes the National Constituent Assembly.
- 14 July 1789 — after Necker's dismissal, Parisians storm the Bastille for arms and gunpowder; the Great Fear spreads in the countryside (late July–August).
- 5–6 October 1789 — women's march on Versailles brings the royal family to Paris; November: Church lands nationalised and assignats (paper money) issued against them.
- 1790–91 — Civil Constitution of the Clergy (July 1790) splits Catholics; Flight to Varennes (June 1791) destroys trust in the king; Champ de Mars massacre (July 1791); Constitution of 1791 and Legislative Assembly (October 1791).
- 1792 — War with Austria (April); Tuileries stormed (10 August); September Massacres; victory at Valmy (20 September); monarchy abolished and Year I of the Republic proclaimed (21–22 September).
- 1793–94 — Louis XVI executed (21 January 1793); Vendée revolt (March); Committee of Public Safety (April); Girondins purged (June); Law of Suspects (September); slavery abolished in the colonies (February 1794); Robespierre falls on 9 Thermidor (27 July 1794).
- 1795–99 — Constitution of Year III creates the Directory (five directors, two councils); Napoleon's Italian campaign (1796–97) and Egypt expedition (1798); coup of 18 Brumaire (9 November 1799) sets up the Consulate.
- 1801–04 — Concordat with the Pope (1801); Civil (Napoleonic) Code (March 1804); Napoleon crowns himself Emperor (2 December 1804).

### Key personalities and their roles

- Louis XVI and Marie Antoinette — weak, indecisive monarchy; the 'Let them eat cake' line is a later legend with no reliable source.
- Jacques Necker — Swiss banker and finance minister; his 1781 public budget statement (Compte rendu) and his 1789 dismissal both mattered.
- Abbé Sieyès — pamphlet 'What is the Third Estate?' (answer: everything); later helped Napoleon's coup.
- Comte de Mirabeau — noble elected for the Third Estate; leading orator for a constitutional monarchy; died 1791.
- Lafayette — commander of the National Guard; drafted the Declaration of Rights with advice from Jefferson.
- Georges Danton — Jacobin leader and orator of national defence (1792); guillotined in April 1794.
- Jean-Paul Marat — radical journalist ('The Friend of the People'); murdered by Charlotte Corday (July 1793).
- Maximilien Robespierre and Saint-Just — leaders of the Committee of Public Safety and the Terror; executed July 1794.
- Brissot and the Girondins — moderate republicans who pushed for war in 1792; purged in June 1793.
- Olympe de Gouges — Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen (1791); guillotined in 1793.
- Napoleon Bonaparte — general who ended the Revolution's instability; spread its legal and administrative reforms across Europe.
- Thinkers: Rousseau (general will, popular sovereignty), Montesquieu (separation of powers), Voltaire (religious toleration), Diderot (the Encyclopédie), the Physiocrats (free trade in grain).

### Causes in four boxes (with taxes and terms)

- Political: absolute monarchy ruling by decree; lettres de cachet allowed imprisonment without trial; the Estates-General had not met since 1614.
- Social: First Estate (clergy, about 0.5% of people) and Second Estate (nobility, about 1.5%) held privileges; the bourgeoisie had wealth but no matching status.
- Taxes on the Third Estate: taille (direct land tax), gabelle (salt tax), tithe (one-tenth of produce to the Church), corvée (forced unpaid labour on roads), plus feudal dues to lords.
- Economic: wars (Seven Years' War, American war) left a huge debt; by 1788 about half of royal spending went on interest payments.
- Subsistence crisis: the 1788 harvest failure pushed bread to over half a worker's daily wage in 1789.
- Intellectual: salons, cafés, pamphlets and the example of America spread the ideas of rights and popular sovereignty.
- Immediate: deadlock over voting by order versus by head in May–June 1789 turned a fiscal crisis into a political revolution.

### Achievements and lasting reforms

- End of feudalism and noble privilege; equality before law and careers open to talent.
- France reorganised into 83 departments (1790) replacing old provinces — a uniform administrative map that survives today.
- Metric system introduced (1790s) — decimal measures that later spread worldwide.
- Civil marriage and divorce legalised (1792); Jews granted full citizenship (1791); the Louvre opened as a public museum (1793).
- Le Chapelier Law (1791) abolished guilds but also banned workers' associations and strikes — shows the Revolution's bourgeois limits.
- Levée en masse (August 1793) — first mass national conscription; the 'nation in arms' changed European warfare.
- National symbols: tricolour flag, the Marseillaise (1792), and the slogan Liberty, Equality, Fraternity (official national motto only later, under the Second/Third Republic).
- Napoleonic Code (1804) carried equality before law, secular authority and property rights into the Netherlands, Italy, Germany and Spain.

### Historiography and debates

- Edmund Burke, 'Reflections on the Revolution in France' (1790) — warned that abstract rights would end in tyranny; the founding text of modern conservatism.
- Thomas Paine, 'Rights of Man' (1791) and Mary Wollstonecraft's replies — defended the Revolution against Burke.
- Alexis de Tocqueville, 'The Old Regime and the Revolution' (1856) — the Revolution continued the centralisation begun by the monarchy.
- Marxist/classical view (Georges Lefebvre, Albert Soboul) — a 'bourgeois revolution' that cleared feudalism for capitalism.
- Revisionist view (Alfred Cobban, 'The Myth of the French Revolution', 1955; François Furet) — not a simple class struggle; politics and ideas drove events.
- Eric Hobsbawm's 'dual revolution' — French political revolution plus British Industrial Revolution together made the modern world (The Age of Revolution, 1962).
- Exam use: in a 'significance' answer, give one line each from two schools to show balance.

### India links and PPSC-type facts

- Tipu Sultan was in touch with revolutionary France; French officers at Srirangapatna formed a Jacobin club (1797) and he is described as 'Citizen Tipu'.
- Napoleon's Egypt campaign (1798) alarmed Governor-General Wellesley and hardened British policy against Tipu (Fourth Anglo-Mysore War, 1799).
- Raja Rammohun Roy openly admired the Revolution's ideals (on his 1830–31 voyage to England he is said to have insisted, though injured, on boarding French ships at the Cape of Good Hope to honour the tricolour).
- The Preamble's liberty, equality and fraternity are usually traced to the French Revolution; the Drafting Committee under Ambedkar added fraternity.
- Static facts: Bastille Day, 14 July, is France's National Day; Marseillaise is the national anthem.
- Pair matching: 'Social Contract' — Rousseau (1762); 'Spirit of the Laws' — Montesquieu (1748); 'What is the Third Estate?' — Sieyès (1789).

### Common traps

- The Bastille held only seven prisoners in July 1789; it was stormed for gunpowder and as a symbol of royal despotism.
- The Tennis Court Oath was taken by the Third Estate deputies (National Assembly), not by the whole Estates-General.
- Jacobins were named after their meeting place, a former Dominican convent of St Jacques; Girondins after the Gironde department (Bordeaux).
- 'Left' and 'right' come from seating in the Assembly: radicals sat on the left of the president's chair.
- The Constitution of 1793 (most democratic, male universal suffrage) was never put into effect because of the war emergency.
- Slavery abolished in 1794, restored by Napoleon in 1802, finally abolished in 1848 — three dates often mixed up.
- Napoleon became First Consul in 1799 and Emperor in 1804; Waterloo is 1815 and the Congress of Vienna 1814–15.

**7.** The pamphlet 'What is the Third Estate?' (January 1789), which argued that the commoners were the real nation, was written by:

(a) Georges Danton  
(b) Jean-Paul Marat  
(c) Camille Desmoulins  
(d) Abbé Sieyès

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) Abbé Sieyès** — Emmanuel-Joseph Sieyès answered his own question: the Third Estate is 'everything', has been 'nothing', and wants to be 'something'. Marat ran the radical paper L'Ami du peuple, and Danton was a Jacobin leader of 1792–94.

**8.** Consider the following statements:  
1. The National Assembly abolished feudal privileges and tithes on the night of 4 August 1789.  
2. The Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen was adopted on 26 August 1789.  
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Both 1 and 2** — Both dates are correct: the 'August Decrees' ended the feudal regime, and three weeks later the Assembly adopted the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, which proclaimed liberty, equality before law and popular sovereignty.

</details>

**9.** Consider the following statements:  
1. In the National Convention the Girondins were the radical group and the Jacobins the moderate group.  
2. France was proclaimed a republic in September 1792.  
3. Slavery in the French colonies was finally abolished in 1848.  
Which of the statements given above are correct?

(a) 1 and 2 only  
(b) 2 and 3 only  
(c) 1 and 3 only  
(d) 1, 2 and 3

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) 2 and 3 only** — The Jacobins (the 'Mountain', backed by the sans-culottes) were the radicals and the Girondins the moderates, so the statement on the Convention groups reverses them. The republic dates from September 1792, and colonial slavery, abolished in 1794 and restored by Napoleon in 1802, ended finally in 1848.

</details>

**10.** Who wrote the 'Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen' (1791)?

(a) Olympe de Gouges  
(b) Madame Roland  
(c) Marie Antoinette  
(d) Mary Wollstonecraft

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) Olympe de Gouges** — Olympe de Gouges challenged the exclusion of women from the 1789 Declaration and was guillotined in 1793. Mary Wollstonecraft wrote the English 'A Vindication of the Rights of Woman' (1792), a common confusion.

</details>

**11.** Napoleon Bonaparte's coup of 18 Brumaire (November 1799) overthrew the:

(a) Committee of Public Safety  
(b) Directory  
(c) National Convention  
(d) Legislative Assembly

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Directory** — The coup ended the unstable Directory (1795–99) and set up the Consulate with Napoleon as First Consul; he became Emperor in 1804. The Committee of Public Safety had run the Terror and lost power after Robespierre's fall in 1794.

</details>

**12.** Consider the following statements:  
1. 'The Spirit of the Laws', which argued for the separation of powers, was written by Voltaire.  
2. French women gained the right to vote under the Constitution of 1791.  
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) Neither 1 nor 2** — Neither is correct: 'The Spirit of the Laws' (1748) is by Montesquieu, and the 1791 Constitution limited voting to male 'active citizens' who paid taxes. French women won the vote only in the 1940s.

</details>
