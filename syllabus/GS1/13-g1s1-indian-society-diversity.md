# Indian society & diversity

## Short notes

- Salient features: unity in diversity, pluralism, caste-based hierarchy, patriarchy, joint family giving way to nuclear families, predominantly rural base, centrality of religion, and coexistence of tradition with modernity.
- Census 2011: population 121.09 crore; decadal growth 17.7%; sex ratio 943; literacy 74.04%; urban share 31.16% (68.8% rural).
- Religious diversity (Census 2011): Hindus 79.8%, Muslims 14.2%, Christians 2.3%, Sikhs 1.7%, Buddhists 0.7%, Jains 0.4%.
- Linguistic diversity: 22 languages in the Eighth Schedule; Census 2011 recorded 121 languages (10,000+ speakers) and 19,569 raw mother-tongue returns; Hindi spoken by about 43.6%; 11 classical languages after five were added in Oct 2024 (Marathi, Pali, Prakrit, Assamese, Bengali).
- Racial/ethnic strands: B.S. Guha's classification - Negrito, Proto-Australoid, Mongoloid, Mediterranean, Western Brachycephals, Nordic.
- Tribal diversity: STs 8.6% of population (Census 2011), over 700 notified communities, 75 Particularly Vulnerable Tribal Groups; SCs 16.6%.
- Caste: varna (fourfold ideal) vs jati (thousands of endogamous occupational groups); Ghurye's features (segmental division, hierarchy, restrictions on commensality, occupation, endogamy); Dumont's purity-pollution ('Homo Hierarchicus').
- M.N. Srinivas' concepts: Sanskritisation, Westernisation, dominant caste; caste today is weaker in ritual terms but strong in politics, marriage and identity.
- Family and kinship: patrilineal and patrilocal norm in most of India; matrilineal traditions among Khasi and Garo (Meghalaya) and historically Nairs (Kerala); regional kinship differences (north vs south marriage rules).
- Regional diversity in geography, food, dress, festivals, architecture and folk traditions; 'little' and 'great' traditions (Robert Redfield; McKim Marriott's universalisation and parochialisation).
- Unifying forces: Constitution and common citizenship, shared history of the freedom struggle, pilgrimage networks (char dham, Shankaracharya's mathas), Bhakti-Sufi syncretism, festivals, cinema, sport, railways, markets, armed forces and all-India services.
- Divisive forces: communalism, casteism, regionalism, linguism, ethnic conflicts (North-East), economic inequality, identity politics, misinformation.
- Constitutional accommodation of diversity: Articles 25-30 (religious and cultural-educational rights), Art 29 (protection of minority culture), Art 350A (mother-tongue instruction), Art 350B (Special Officer for linguistic minorities), Fifth and Sixth Schedules, federalism.
- Changing features: urbanisation, education, migration, media and markets are loosening caste and family norms but also creating new identities and inequalities.
- Next Census (2027; reference date 1 March 2027) will include caste enumeration for the first time since 1931 - important for policy debates on diversity and justice.

## Punjab angle

- Punjab (Census 2011): population 2.77 crore; Sikhs 57.7%, Hindus 38.5%; Scheduled Castes 31.9% - the highest share among all states; sex ratio 895, child sex ratio 846, literacy 75.8%, urban 37.5%.
- Internal diversity: Majha, Doaba, Malwa and Puadh regions with distinct dialects (Majhi as the standard, Doabi, Malwai, Puadhi); Malerkotla is a Muslim-majority district.
- Syncretic heritage: Guru Granth Sahib includes Hindu Bhakti and Muslim Sufi saints; Baba Farid's shrine at Faridkot; langar, sangat and pangat promote equality.
- Malerkotla's Nawab Sher Muhammad Khan protested the execution of Guru Gobind Singh's younger sons (1705) - a lasting symbol of communal harmony.
- Dera culture (Radha Soami Beas, Dera Sachkhand Ballan and others) reflects caste and spiritual diversity; large Punjabi diaspora in Canada, UK, USA, Australia.

## How PPSC asks it

Questions test the 'unity in diversity' theme, persistence and change of caste and family, and constitutional handling of diversity. Use Census data, sociological concepts (Srinivas, Ghurye) and Punjab examples; avoid purely descriptive answers.

## Deep dive

### Diversity in numbers — India vs Punjab (Census 2011 unless stated)

- Population: India 121.09 crore; Punjab 2.77 crore (about 2.3% of India's people on about 1.5% of its area); Punjab's 2001-11 growth was 13.9% against India's 17.7%.
- Density: India 382 per sq km; Punjab 551 per sq km.
- Religion in Punjab: Sikhs 57.7%, Hindus 38.5%, Muslims 1.9%, Christians 1.3%; Punjab is India's only Sikh-majority state and holds roughly three-quarters of India's 2.08 crore Sikhs.
- Scheduled Castes: 31.9% of Punjab's people (about 88.6 lakh) — the highest share of any state (India 16.6%); Doaba has the densest Dalit population, with Shahid Bhagat Singh Nagar district above 40%.
- Scheduled Tribes: none notified in Punjab — a frequent one-liner; India's ST share is 8.6%.
- Language: Punjabi is the mother tongue of about 3.3 crore Indians (about 2.7%); within Punjab, Hindi and Punjabi speakers coexist, and the 1950s-60s language census controversy fed the Punjabi Suba movement.
- Sex ratio: India 943, Punjab 895; child sex ratio India 918, Punjab 846 — Punjab's diversity story also includes a deep gender deficit.
- Coming change: the next Census (reference date 1 March 2027) will enumerate caste for all communities, the first such count since 1931 — expect fresh data on Punjab's Jat Sikh, OBC and SC shares.

### Caste in Punjab — Sikh ideals and social practice

- Paradox: the Sikh Gurus rejected caste (langar, sangat, pangat; Guru Gobind Singh's Khalsa of 1699 with Panj Pyare from different castes), yet caste survives in marriage, land ownership and village power.
- Dominant caste: Jat Sikhs fit M.N. Srinivas's 'dominant caste' test — numerical strength in villages, ownership of most agricultural land, and political power (most Chief Ministers since 1966 have been Jat Sikhs).
- Dalit groups: Punjab notifies about 39 SC castes; the largest are the Mazhabi Sikhs (historically sweepers and agricultural labourers), Ravidasia/Ramdasia and Ad-Dharmi (historically leather-working Chamars), and Balmikis.
- Land: Dalits are about 32% of the population but own only about 3.5% of the land; about 73% of Punjab's Dalits live in villages, mostly as landless labourers.
- Everyday exclusion: separate gurdwaras and cremation grounds in many villages, social boycotts after disputes over wages or panchayat land, and inter-caste marriage resistance (honour killings).
- Assertion: the Ad-Dharm movement (Mangu Ram Mugowalia, mid-1920s) was recognised as a separate religion in the 1931 census, where about 4.19 lakh people in Punjab returned themselves as Ad-Dharmis; it built Dalit pride around Guru Ravidas.
- Mobility: Doaba Dalits used army service, education and early emigration (UK, Canada) to rise economically — visible in remittance-built houses and the Ravidassia diaspora.
- Politics: Kanshi Ram (born in Ropar district, 1934) founded the BSP in 1984; Punjab's first Dalit Chief Minister was Charanjit Singh Channi (Sept 2021-March 2022).
- Policy: within the SC quota, Punjab gave preference to Balmikis and Mazhabi Sikhs (from 1975) — the root of the 2024 Supreme Court ruling on sub-classification.

### Dera culture in Punjab

- Meaning: a dera is a religious centre led by a living spiritual head (sant, baba) with its own followers, rituals and welfare network; one survey counted more than 9,000 Sikh and non-Sikh deras across Punjab's roughly 12,000 villages.
- Radha Soami Satsang Beas (founded 1891 near Beas, Amritsar district): a large, apolitical-looking organisation with a strong urban and diaspora following.
- Dera Sachkhand Ballan (Jalandhar): the main Ravidassia centre; after the killing of its Sant Ramanand in Vienna (May 2009), the dera declared a separate Ravidassia religion with its own scripture in 2010.
- Dera Sacha Sauda (Sirsa, Haryana, 1948): huge Malwa following among Dalits and backward castes; clashes with Sikh bodies in 2007; violence in Panchkula after its head's conviction in Aug 2017.
- Older reform sects: Namdharis (Kukas) of Baba Ram Singh (Bhaini Sahib, from 1857), known for the 1872 Kuka episode at Malerkotla; Sant Nirankaris, whose clash with Sikh groups in Amritsar on 13 April 1978 is often seen as the spark of the militancy years.
- Why deras grow: caste exclusion in mainstream religious spaces, desire for dignity and a 'guru' accessible to the poor, free kitchens, de-addiction, medical camps and schools, and community belonging for migrants and the urban poor.
- Political role: deras deliver bloc votes, so parties court them before elections; critics see this as patronage politics that weakens secular citizenship.
- Sociological reading: deras show 'protest through religion' — Dalit consciousness expressed through separate shrines and saints (Ronki Ram's work on Ravidass deras).

### Caste: stratification or exploitation? — perspectives for the 2026-type question

- Structural-functional view: caste as a system of interdependent occupations (jajmani) that ensured stability — criticised for hiding coercion.
- Louis Dumont: hierarchy based on the purity-pollution opposition is the core of caste; Dipankar Gupta replies that every jati sees itself as superior, so caste is better read as 'discrete identities' than a single agreed ladder.
- B.R. Ambedkar: caste is 'graded inequality' — each caste looks down on the one below, which prevents the oppressed from uniting; endogamy is the mechanism that maintains it ('Castes in India', 1916; 'Annihilation of Caste', 1936).
- Gerald Berreman and Marxist scholars: caste is a system of power and economic exploitation, comparable to race in the US; land, labour and surplus are extracted from lower castes.
- Andre Beteille ('Caste, Class and Power', 1965): in modern India caste, class and power no longer overlap neatly — a useful frame for showing change without claiming caste has gone.
- Rajni Kothari ('Caste in Indian Politics', 1970): democracy politicised caste, turning it into an interest group and vote bloc.
- Changing trends (PPSC 2021 asked): weakening of ritual purity and occupational links; persistence of endogamy and violence; rise of caste associations and identity politics; demands for sub-classification and caste census; caste in the diaspora (UK caste-discrimination debate).
- Punjab illustration: Jat Sikh landowners and Dalit labourers — a class relation layered over caste — and Dalit land struggles over the reserved one-third of panchayat land.

### Is diversity under threat? (PPSC 2018 asked) — balanced points

- Threats: majoritarian polarisation, hate speech and online misinformation, pressure on minority languages and dialects, homogenising consumer culture, ethnic conflict (Manipur, 2023 onward).
- Resilience: constitutional protections and courts, federal language policy, regional parties, vibrant regional cinema and music, inter-faith practices (Punjab's langar and Sufi shrines), and diaspora-led cultural revival.
- Data point: Census 2011 recorded 121 languages with 10,000+ speakers, yet UNESCO lists many Indian languages as endangered — diversity can thin quietly even without conflict.
- Punjab angle: the shift of rural youth abroad, decline of Punjabi reading among urban children, and the spread of Pentecostal churches in Majha are new pressures on Punjab's traditional plural balance.
- Conclusion line for answers: diversity is less under threat from difference than from the loss of trust between groups — institutions that build trust (schools, local government, fair policing) are the real safeguards.
- Structure for a short note: define diversity (religious, linguistic, caste, regional, ethnic), give two threats with recent examples, two sources of resilience, and a one-line constitutional anchor (Articles 25-30, 29, 350A; federalism).

### Common traps

- Punjab has the highest SC percentage, but Uttar Pradesh has the largest SC number.
- Mizoram and Lakshadweep have the highest ST shares; Punjab and Haryana have no notified STs.
- Ad-Dharm was a 1920s Dalit movement in Punjab; Adi-Dravida was a Tamil Dalit movement — different regions.
- 'Sanskritisation' is mobility by imitating upper-caste rituals; Dalit assertion in Punjab (Ad-Dharm, Ravidassia) is closer to forming a separate identity than to Sanskritisation.
- Srinivas's 'dominant caste' need not be ritually high — numbers, land and power are the criteria.
- The 1931 census was the last full caste count; the 2011 Socio-Economic and Caste Census (SECC) collected caste data that was never fully released — do not call SECC a census of caste shares.

**2.** Is caste losing its relevance in contemporary India? Critically examine. (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: caste as hierarchy and identity
- Weakening: urbanisation, education, occupational mobility, legal equality
- Persistence: endogamy, discrimination, honour crimes, caste in politics and reservation debates
- New forms: caste associations, dominant-caste assertion, Dalit assertion
- Census 2027 caste enumeration debate
- Conclusion: caste transformed rather than disappeared
