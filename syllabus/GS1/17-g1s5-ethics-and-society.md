## Short notes

- Ethics: branch of philosophy dealing with right and wrong, good and bad conduct (Greek 'ethos' = character); morality is an individual's or group's code; law is the minimum ethics enforced by the state.
- Essence of ethics in human actions: actions must be voluntary, done with knowledge and free will; only 'human acts' (deliberate) are morally evaluable, not involuntary 'acts of man' (reflexes).
- Moral determinants of an act: (1) the object - what is done; (2) the intention or motive - why it is done; (3) the circumstances - who, when, where, how; an act is fully good only when all three are good.
- Social determinants of ethical behaviour: family, religion, education, peer group, culture, law, media, profession, personal experience and conscience.
- Consequences of ethical conduct: trust, cooperation, social capital, legitimacy of institutions, efficiency, personal integrity and peace of mind; unethical conduct leads to corruption, injustice, inefficiency, loss of trust and social disorder.
- Dimensions of ethics: meta-ethics (meaning and nature of moral terms), normative ethics (standards of right action), applied ethics (bioethics, environmental, business, media, cyber and public-administration ethics), descriptive ethics (what people actually believe).
- Normative theories: consequentialism/utilitarianism (Bentham, Mill - greatest happiness of the greatest number); deontology (Kant - duty and the categorical imperative; treat persons as ends); virtue ethics (Aristotle - character and the golden mean); ethics of care.
- Indian traditions: nishkama karma (Bhagavad Gita), dharma and rajadharma, Buddha's Middle Path, Jain ahimsa and anekantavada, Gandhian purity of ends and means, Kautilya's view that the king's welfare lies in the welfare of his subjects.
- Ethical relativism (morality varies with culture) vs universalism (some values like human dignity apply everywhere).
- Ethics in private relationships: family, friendship, neighbourhood - governed by love, care, loyalty, honesty, gratitude and sacrifice; informal and emotion-based.
- Ethics in public relationships: impartiality, objectivity, integrity, accountability, transparency, rule of law, non-partisanship, empathy and compassion for the weaker sections.
- Nolan Committee (UK, 1995) - Seven Principles of Public Life: selflessness, integrity, objectivity, accountability, openness, honesty, leadership.
- Second ARC 4th Report 'Ethics in Governance' (2007) recommended codes of ethics for ministers and civil servants, strengthening anti-corruption laws and institutions.
- Conflict between private and public: nepotism, favouritism, conflict of interest, misuse of discretion; private virtues (loyalty to family) can clash with public duty (impartiality).
- Legal-institutional framework: CCS (Conduct) Rules 1964, AIS (Conduct) Rules 1968, Prevention of Corruption Act 1988 (amended 2018), RTI Act 2005, Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act 2013, Whistle Blowers Protection Act 2014.
- Exemplars: Satyendra Dubey (2003) and S. Manjunath (2005), who paid with their lives for exposing corruption, illustrate courage in public ethics.

## Punjab angle

- Sikh ethical principles - kirat karo (honest labour), naam japo, vand chhako (sharing), seva and sarbat da bhala (welfare of all) - are useful illustrations for public-service ethics.
- The story of Guru Nanak preferring Bhai Lalo's honestly earned bread to Malik Bhago's feast is a classic example of ethics of honest earning.
- Punjab's Transparency and Accountability in Delivery of Public Services Act 2018 (which replaced the Right to Service Act 2011) makes time-bound service delivery a legal duty of officials.
- Punjab Vigilance Bureau and the state's anti-corruption action line (launched 2022) are institutional checks on unethical conduct.

## Deep dive

### Ethics in the age of digital technology and AI (PPSC 2026 asked)

- New dilemmas: algorithmic bias in welfare targeting or policing, opaque 'black-box' decisions, mass surveillance, deepfakes and misinformation, data leaks, automation of jobs, and the question of who is accountable when an AI system errs.
- Principles widely agreed: fairness and non-discrimination, transparency and explainability, accountability with a human in the loop, privacy and data minimisation, safety and robustness, inclusiveness.
- Indian framework: Justice K.S. Puttaswamy (2017) made privacy a fundamental right; the Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023 sets consent and purpose-limitation duties; NITI Aayog's 'Responsible AI for All' papers (2021); the IndiaAI Mission (approved March 2024, about Rs 10,372 crore) funds compute, datasets and safe-and-trusted AI.
- Global markers: UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI (Nov 2021, adopted by all member states), the EU AI Act (2024, risk-based), the Bletchley Declaration on AI safety (Nov 2023).
- Public-service angle: e-governance raises efficiency and cuts discretion (less scope for bribes), but exclusion errors from biometric failures and digital illiteracy harm the poor — 'digital by default, human by exception'.
- Civil-servant duties: verify AI outputs before acting, keep records of how decisions were made, protect citizens' data, give an offline grievance route, and never hide behind 'the system decided'.
- Thinkers to cite: Shoshana Zuboff ('surveillance capitalism'), Kant's rule to treat persons as ends (no manipulation by design), Rawls' veil of ignorance for designing fair algorithms.
- Punjab-style example: land-record digitisation, e-Sewa and online FIRs help citizens, but rural elderly and Dalit labourers need assisted service centres — an ethics-of-access point.

### Ethical dilemmas in public office (PPSC 2018 asked)

- Definition: an ethical dilemma is a situation where two or more moral duties or values conflict and choosing one means compromising another — not a choice between right and wrong, but between two rights (or two wrongs).
- Administrative discretion vs rules: helping a genuinely needy applicant who lacks one document.
- Loyalty vs whistle-blowing: reporting a superior's corruption against loyalty to the organisation.
- Political neutrality vs responsiveness: carrying out a minister's lawful but unwise order, or resisting an unlawful one.
- Transparency vs confidentiality: RTI disclosure against privacy, security or cabinet secrecy.
- Efficiency vs equity: speedy land acquisition for a highway vs fair compensation and rehabilitation of the displaced.
- Personal relations vs public duty: a relative, caste-fellow or friend seeking a favour; gifts and hospitality.
- Public interest vs legal formalism: allowing a protest that blocks a road, or enforcing order strictly.
- Resolution tools: identify stakeholders, facts and values; test options against law, the Constitution and service rules; apply consequences (utilitarian), duties (Kantian) and character ('what would a person of integrity do?'); use the 'newspaper test' and Gandhi's talisman; record reasons; consult seniors; act and own the decision.

### Psychological and situational determinants — research to cite

- Lawrence Kohlberg — three levels of moral development: pre-conventional (avoid punishment, self-interest), conventional (approval, law and order), post-conventional (social contract, universal principles); a good civil servant should reason at the post-conventional level.
- Carol Gilligan — critiqued Kohlberg for a male 'justice' bias; proposed an 'ethics of care' rooted in relationships and responsibility.
- Stanley Milgram's obedience experiments (early 1960s) — ordinary people inflicted what they believed were painful shocks when told to by authority; lesson: blind obedience is dangerous, so public servants need moral courage.
- Solomon Asch's conformity studies (1951) — people give wrong answers to agree with a group; explains 'collective' corruption in offices.
- Philip Zimbardo's Stanford prison study (1971) — roles and situations can corrupt behaviour; relevant to custodial violence and the need for oversight.
- Leon Festinger's cognitive dissonance (1957) — people rationalise wrong acts ('everyone takes a cut') to reduce inner conflict; Albert Bandura's 'moral disengagement' lists similar excuses.
- Bystander effect (Darley and Latane, 1968) — the more witnesses, the less each feels responsible; seen in road accidents and harassment cases; remedy is clear individual duty (Good Samaritan guidelines, 2016).
- Use: these show that ethics is determined not only by character but by institutions and situations — hence the need for codes, training, rotation and oversight.

### Concepts often confused — precise meanings

- Integrity — consistency between values, words and actions, even when unobserved; probity — complete honesty and uprightness, especially in handling public money and power.
- Impartiality — not favouring any party in applying rules; neutrality — not taking political sides; objectivity — deciding on evidence and merit.
- Accountability — answerability for outcomes to a higher authority or the public; responsibility — the duty to perform a task; answerability — the duty to explain actions.
- Conflict of interest — actual (personal interest affects a decision now), potential (could arise in future) and perceived (others reasonably think it exists); all three must be managed by disclosure or recusal.
- Conscience — the inner moral sense; 'crisis of conscience' is the inner conflict when duty and conscience diverge.
- Ethics vs morality vs law: ethics is the systematic study and the professional code; morality is personal or social belief; law is enforceable minimum — an act can be legal but unethical (tax avoidance) or illegal but moral (civil disobedience).
- Values vs attitudes: values are deep, stable beliefs; attitudes are evaluations of specific objects that can change with persuasion.

### Exemplars and case lessons from Indian public life

- T.N. Seshan (Chief Election Commissioner 1990-96) — enforcement of the Model Code of Conduct showed that existing rules, applied without fear, can change institutional culture.
- E. Sreedharan — Konkan Railway and Delhi Metro delivered on time and within budget; integrity plus professional competence.
- Armstrong Pame (IAS, Manipur, 2012) — built a 100-km road with crowd-funding and community labour; innovation and empathy.
- Ashok Khemka (IAS, Haryana) — cancelled a controversial land mutation in 2012 despite pressure; frequent transfers illustrate the cost of honesty and the need for tenure protection.
- Durga Shakti Nagpal (IAS, UP, 2013) — action against illegal sand mining and the controversy over her suspension highlight political pressure on field officers.
- Sant Balbir Singh Seechewal (Punjab) — community-led cleaning of the Kali Bein (from 2000) shows ethics of environmental stewardship and voluntary service.
- Lesson pattern for answers: name the value (courage, integrity, empathy, innovation), the situation, the cost paid and the institutional reform it suggests.

### Answer-writing tips and traps

- Define first, then illustrate: examiners reward a crisp definition followed by a public-administration example.
- Do not equate ethics with religion; cite secular sources (Constitution, codes, philosophers) along with religious teachings.
- Utilitarianism can justify harm to minorities; deontology can be rigid — show awareness of each theory's limits.
- A 'dilemma' needs two competing values; a simple temptation to take a bribe is not a dilemma.
- Kohlberg has three levels and six stages; Goleman's emotional intelligence has five components — do not mix the numbers.
- Close with a practical mechanism (code of conduct, RTI, social audit, training) rather than a slogan.

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** What do you understand by the 'determinants' and 'consequences' of ethics in human actions? Illustrate with examples from public life. (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: ethics evaluates voluntary actions
- Determinants: object, intention, circumstances; social influences
- Example: officer approving a file - legal act, but motive (bribe) or circumstances (favouring relative) makes it unethical
- Consequences: trust, legitimacy vs corruption, alienation
- Conclusion: good governance rests on ethical intention and consequence awareness

</details>

**2.** Distinguish between ethics in private and public relationships. How should a civil servant resolve conflicts between the two? (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: private vs public domain
- Private: love, loyalty, care, emotional bonds
- Public: impartiality, objectivity, accountability, rule of law (Nolan principles)
- Conflict examples: relative seeking favour, political pressure, social expectations
- Resolution: conflict-of-interest disclosure, recusal, codes of conduct, conscience, Gandhi's talisman
- Conclusion: public trust demands that public duty prevail

</details>

**3.** Explain the various dimensions of ethics. Which normative approach is most suitable for public administration? (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: ethics as a multi-level discipline
- Meta, normative, applied, descriptive ethics
- Normative theories: utilitarian, deontological, virtue, care
- Strengths and limits of each for administrators
- Argument for a blended approach: rules (deontology), welfare outcomes (utilitarian), character (virtue), compassion (care)
- Conclusion: constitutional values as the anchor

</details>
