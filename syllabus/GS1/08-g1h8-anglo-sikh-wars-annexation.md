# Anglo-Sikh wars & annexation

> Official syllabus line: Annexation of Punjab with special reference to the causes and consequences of the Anglo-Sikh wars.

## Short notes

- Post-1839 anarchy: Kharak Singh (died 1840), Nau Nihal Singh (killed by a falling gateway the same day as his father's cremation), Rani Chand Kaur, Sher Singh and wazir Dhian Singh (both assassinated by the Sandhawalias in 1843).
- Minor Maharaja Duleep Singh with Maharani Jind Kaur as regent; wazirs Hira Singh and Jawahar Singh killed; the Khalsa army, organised through regimental panchayats, became the arbiter of power.
- Treacherous leadership: wazir Lal Singh and commander Tej Singh sought to weaken the army and were in contact with the British during the war.
- British designs: annexation of Sindh (1843), troop build-up at Ludhiana, Ferozepur and Ambala, pontoon bridges, provocative conduct of agent Major Broadfoot; desire for a scientific frontier against Afghanistan and Russia.
- First Anglo-Sikh War (1845-46): Sikh army crossed the Sutlej (Dec 1845); battles of Mudki (18 Dec 1845), Ferozeshah (21-22 Dec), Baddowal and Aliwal (Jan 1846), Sobraon (10 Feb 1846, decisive).
- Treaty of Lahore (9 March 1846): Jalandhar Doab annexed; war indemnity of ₹1.5 crore; Sikh army reduced; British troops to remain at Lahore for a limited period.
- Treaty of Amritsar (16 March 1846): Kashmir sold to Gulab Singh Dogra for ₹75 lakh - origin of the Jammu and Kashmir princely state.
- Treaty of Bhyrowal (Dec 1846): Council of Regency under the British Resident; British troops stationed at Lahore until Duleep Singh came of age; Rani Jind Kaur removed with a pension.
- Causes of the Second Anglo-Sikh War: Multan revolt of Diwan Mulraj and killing of British officers Vans Agnew and Anderson (April 1848), revolt of Chattar Singh and Sher Singh Attariwala, harsh treatment and exile of Rani Jind Kaur, resentment of disbanded soldiers.
- Second Anglo-Sikh War (1848-49): Ramnagar (Nov 1848), Chillianwala (13 Jan 1849, heavy British losses), Gujrat (21 Feb 1849, decisive 'battle of guns').
- Annexation proclaimed by Lord Dalhousie on 29 March 1849; Duleep Singh pensioned and later taken to England; the Koh-i-noor presented to Queen Victoria.
- Administration after annexation: Board of Administration (Henry Lawrence, John Lawrence, Charles Mansel; 1849-53), then Chief Commissioner John Lawrence; Lieutenant-Governorship from 1859.
- Causes summarised: internal instability after Ranjit Singh, ambitious army without leadership, treachery of Lal Singh and Tej Singh, British expansionism, strategic and economic value of Punjab.
- Consequences: end of the last major independent Indian kingdom; British frontier reached Afghanistan; Punjab became the main recruiting ground of the Indian Army ('martial races'); Punjab largely did not join the 1857 revolt.
- Economic consequences: settlement of land revenue, canal colonies from the 1880s, roads and railways, growth of commercial agriculture - and rural indebtedness.
- Justification debate: annexing the kingdom of a ward under British guardianship (Bhyrowal) is criticised as a breach of trust; British defended it as necessary for peace and frontier security.

## Punjab angle

- Anglo-Sikh War Memorial at Ferozeshah (Ferozepur district) commemorates the first war; battle sites at Mudki and Sobraon are in Ferozepur/Tarn Taran region.
- Sardar Sham Singh Attariwala, killed at Sobraon, is remembered as a symbol of Sikh valour; Attari (Amritsar) is the ancestral seat of the Attariwala sardars.
- Shah Mohammad's Punjabi 'Jangnama' is a contemporary verse account of the First Anglo-Sikh War - a valuable literary source.
- Maharani Jind Kaur and Maharaja Duleep Singh are central figures in Punjabi memory of the loss of sovereignty.

## How PPSC asks it

PPSC asks causes and consequences of each war, the treaties of 1846 and whether annexation was justified. Name battles, treaties and actors; end with a balanced judgement on British motives and internal decay.

## Deep dive

### Background, 1839–45: why war came

- The Lahore treasury, said to hold several crores at Ranjit Singh's death, was drained by bribes to the army and by court factions. Pay increases won the army's loyalty only temporarily.
- Khalsa army panchayats (from 1841) elected regimental committees that made and unmade wazirs: Hira Singh was killed (Dec 1844) and Jawahar Singh executed by the army (Sept 1845).
- The court factions were the Dogras (Dhian Singh, Gulab Singh, Hira Singh), the Sandhawalias (Ajit Singh, Lehna Singh, Attar Singh), and Maharani Jind Kaur's party (Jawahar Singh, Lal Singh).
- British forward moves: Sindh annexed (1843); the Gwalior campaign (Maharajpur, 1843) removed a possible ally; troops on the Sutlej frontier grew from a few thousand to over 30,000; pontoon boats were brought up for bridging the Sutlej.
- Major George Broadfoot, Political Agent from Nov 1844, treated Lahore's cis-Sutlej estates as under British jurisdiction and behaved provocatively.
- Lal Singh (wazir) and Tej Singh (commander) were in contact with the British agent and, many historians argue, wanted the army broken. Shah Mohammad's Jangnama blames the leaders, not the soldiers.
- 11 Dec 1845: the Sikh army crossed the Sutlej near Harike/Ferozepur. Governor-General Hardinge's proclamation of 13 Dec 1845 declared war and annexed Lahore's cis-Sutlej territories.
- Who was the aggressor? Officially the Sikhs crossed first, but Cunningham, a British officer on the spot, argued that British provocation made war inevitable, and his career suffered for saying so.

### First Anglo-Sikh War (1845–46): battle by battle

- Mudki (18 Dec 1845): Gough and Hardinge against Lal Singh's cavalry-heavy force. A narrow British win in a dusk battle in which Generals Sale and McCaskill were killed.
- Ferozeshah (21–22 Dec 1845): the British nearly lost on the first night. On 22 Dec Tej Singh arrived with fresh troops but withdrew, which saved Gough's exhausted army. Broadfoot was killed.
- Baddowal (21 Jan 1846): Ranjodh Singh Majithia attacked Harry Smith's column and captured its baggage, the only Sikh success.
- Aliwal (28 Jan 1846): Harry Smith defeated Ranjodh Singh near Ludhiana and cleared the Sutlej's south bank.
- Sobraon (10 Feb 1846): the decisive battle. The entrenched Sikh camp was stormed, the bridge of boats gave way (sabotage by Tej Singh is alleged), thousands drowned or fell, and Sham Singh Attariwala died fighting.
- The British entered Lahore on 20 Feb 1846. Gulab Singh, who had stayed aloof, became the chief negotiator.
- Sources: Shah Mohammad's Punjabi Jangnama (contemporary verse), Cunningham Ch IX (an eyewitness at Aliwal and Sobraon) and Gough and Innes (1897).

### The settlement of 1846: three treaties in detail

- Treaty of Lahore (9 March 1846): Lahore gave up all territory south of the Sutlej and ceded the Jalandhar Doab (between the Beas and Sutlej).
- War indemnity of ₹1.5 crore: Lahore could pay only ₹50 lakh, so for the remaining ₹1 crore it ceded the hill country between the Beas and the Indus, including Kashmir and Hazara.
- Army limits: 25 battalions of infantry (about 20,000) and 12,000 cavalry. 36 guns that had faced the British were surrendered, and British troops gained right of passage.
- Political terms: Duleep Singh was recognised as Maharaja, with Rani Jind Kaur as regent and Lal Singh as wazir. Supplementary articles (11 March 1846) kept a British force at Lahore until the end of 1846.
- Treaty of Amritsar (16 March 1846): the British 'sold' Kashmir and the hill territories east of the Indus and west of the Ravi to Gulab Singh for ₹75 lakh. He accepted British supremacy and paid a token annual tribute (a horse, shawl goats and shawls). This created the princely state of Jammu and Kashmir.
- Kashmir episode: the outgoing governor Sheikh Imamuddin resisted handing over Kashmir (Oct 1846), secretly encouraged by Lal Singh. Lal Singh was tried and exiled (Dec 1846).
- Treaty of Bhairowal (16 Dec 1846): a Council of Regency of eight sardars (including Tej Singh, Sher Singh Attariwala, Dina Nath and Fakir Nuruddin) under the British Resident, who had full authority in every department. British troops stayed until Duleep Singh came of age (Sept 1854), paid for by the Darbar (₹22 lakh a year). Rani Jind Kaur was removed from power with a ₹1.5 lakh pension.
- Effect: Lahore became a British protectorate in all but name, with Henry Lawrence as Resident (1846–47) and his 'young men' (Edwardes, Abbott, Nicholson, Lumsden) as district agents.

### Second Anglo-Sikh War (1848–49): causes and course

- Treatment of Jind Kaur: she was removed to Sheikhupura (Aug 1847) over the alleged Prema plot, then deported to Benares (May 1848) with her pension cut to ₹48,000. This angered the army and the people.
- Multan: Diwan Mulraj (son of Sawan Mal) was pressed for higher succession dues and revenue arrears and resigned. Resident Frederick Currie sent Sardar Kahan Singh with Vans Agnew and Lt Anderson, who were killed on 20 April 1848. Mulraj was pushed into revolt.
- British delay: Lt Herbert Edwardes and Bahawalpur levies besieged Multan, but Currie and Dalhousie held back regular troops until the cold season. Critics see this as letting the rising spread to justify annexation.
- Hazara: Chattar Singh Attariwala (nazim) quarrelled with Capt. James Abbott, and Col. Canora was killed (Aug 1848). His son Sher Singh, sent to Multan with Darbar troops, joined the rebels on 14 Sept 1848, which turned the revolt into a national war.
- Dalhousie's Oct 1848 speech promised war 'with a vengeance' because the Sikh nation had chosen war. Gough took the field in Nov 1848.
- Ramnagar (22 Nov 1848, on the Chenab): a costly British cavalry charge in which Brig. Cureton was killed. Sadullapur followed (3 Dec 1848).
- Chillianwala (13 Jan 1849): the bloodiest British battle in India to that time (about 2,400 casualties) and tactically indecisive. Gough was ordered to be replaced by Charles Napier.
- Multan fell on 22 Jan 1849; Mulraj surrendered and died in captivity in 1851. Gujrat (21 Feb 1849), the 'battle of the guns', was decided by massed British artillery and was decisive.
- The Sikhs surrendered at Rawalpindi (Hurmuck) on 14 March 1849 under Gilbert's pursuit. Dost Mohammad's Afghan horse, sent on the promise of Peshawar, fled through the Khyber.
- Bhai Maharaj Singh led continued resistance (1848–49). He was arrested in Dec 1849, deported to Singapore and died there in 1856, a figure of early anti-British resistance.

### Annexation (29 March 1849): terms and the justification debate

- Terms signed by Duleep Singh and the Council: renunciation of sovereignty; confiscation of all state property to the Company; the Koh-i-noor to be surrendered to the Queen; a pension of ₹4–5 lakh a year for Duleep, who was to live where the government decided. He was sent to Fatehgarh under Dr John Login.
- Duleep Singh converted to Christianity (1853), was taken to England (1854), reconverted to Sikhism (1886), was stopped at Aden from returning, and died in Paris (1893). Jind Kaur escaped Chunar to Nepal (1849), joined him in England and died in London (1863).
- Dalhousie's case: the rising of the Sikh army and sardars amounted to war by the Lahore state, so the British had the right of conquest. The young Maharaja's minority did not matter.
- Critics' case: Duleep was a ward under British guardianship (Bhairowal) and the Resident ran the government, so a revolt by officials could not be the state's war. Major Evans Bell's 'The Annexation of the Punjaub and the Maharajah Duleep Singh' (1882) called it a breach of trust, and the deliberate delay at Multan suggests intent.
- Henry Lawrence favoured continuing the protectorate and John Lawrence favoured annexation. Their disagreement shaped the Board of Administration (1849–53).
- Immediate measures: the Khalsa army was disbanded and disarmed; the Punjab Irregular Force (later the Punjab Frontier Force) was raised; the sardars' jagirs were cut; and summary land settlements followed.

### Causes of Sikh defeat and consequences

- Causes of defeat: no leader after Ranjit Singh; treachery and inaction of Lal Singh and Tej Singh (and Gulab Singh's double game); the army had discipline but no political direction; poor co-ordination and supply; British superiority in artillery management (as at Gujrat) and in resources from all of India.
- Political consequences: the end of the last major independent Indian state; the British frontier reached the Khyber and Afghanistan; Kashmir became a separate Dogra state (1846); and the cis-Sutlej chiefs became the Raj's most loyal allies.
- Military consequences: Punjab became the Raj's recruiting ground. Many Khalsa veterans joined British Sikh regiments, and Punjab helped the British in 1857.
- Economic and social consequences: cash land revenue, settlements and records of rights; canals and later canal colonies; railways; commercial agriculture; and later indebtedness and the 1900 Act (see pbh4).
- Cultural consequences: the loss of court patronage hurt Persian, Punjabi and the arts; the Koh-i-noor and the toshakhana were taken. Missionary and Singh Sabha activity grew in the new order.
- Memory: Sham Singh Attariwala's martyrdom (Sobraon), the Anglo-Sikh War Memorial at Ferozeshah, and Maharani Jind Kaur and Duleep Singh as symbols of lost sovereignty.

### PPSC traps and answer pointers

- There were two Treaties of Amritsar: 1809 (Ranjit Singh with Metcalfe) and 1846 (British with Gulab Singh). The 1846 Treaty of Lahore is not the same as the Treaty of Bhairowal (Dec 1846).
- The 1846 indemnity was ₹1.5 crore, while Kashmir was sold to Gulab Singh for ₹75 lakh. These two figures are often swapped in options.
- Order of battles: Mudki → Ferozeshah → Baddowal → Aliwal → Sobraon (first war); Ramnagar → Sadullapur → Chillianwala → Multan's fall → Gujrat (second war).
- Governors-General: Hardinge (first war, 1845–46) and Dalhousie (second war and annexation, 1848–49). Commander-in-Chief: Gough in both wars.
- Mulraj was the diwan of Multan (not a Sikh sardar); Chattar Singh and Sher Singh were Attariwalas; Sham Singh Attariwala died at Sobraon in the first war.
- Mains structure for 'causes and consequences': internal (court, army, leaders), external (British expansion, frontier, economic motives), the 1846 settlement as a bridge, the second war as a pretext, and consequences under political, military, economic and social heads, ending with the justification debate.

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** Examine the causes of the First Anglo-Sikh War. Was it forced upon the Sikhs by the British? (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: kingdom's decline after 1839
- Internal causes: succession murders, army panchayats, treachery of Lal Singh and Tej Singh
- British causes: Sindh annexation, troop concentration, Broadfoot, strategic frontier aims
- Immediate cause: Sikh army crosses Sutlej, December 1845
- Arguments both ways on responsibility
- Conclusion: British aggression exploited internal disorder

</details>

**2.** Discuss the main provisions of the treaties of Lahore, Amritsar and Bhyrowal (1846) and their significance. (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: post-Sobraon settlement
- Lahore: Jalandhar Doab, indemnity, army reduction, Resident
- Amritsar: Kashmir sold to Gulab Singh
- Bhyrowal: Regency Council under Resident, British troops, Rani Jind Kaur sidelined
- Significance: Punjab made a protectorate, prelude to annexation, origin of J&K state
- Conclusion: sovereignty hollowed out in stages

</details>

**3.** Analyse the causes and consequences of the annexation of Punjab in 1849. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: Dalhousie's proclamation of 29 March 1849
- Causes: Multan revolt, Attariwala uprising, Jind Kaur's exile, Dalhousie's expansionism
- Second war: Ramnagar, Chillianwala, Gujrat
- Political consequences: end of Sikh rule, frontier to Afghanistan, Board of Administration
- Socio-economic consequences: army recruitment, canal colonies, land settlement; Punjab's loyalty in 1857
- Debate on justification (guardianship breached)
- Conclusion: annexation completed British paramountcy in India

</details>
