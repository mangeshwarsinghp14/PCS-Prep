## Short notes

- Social empowerment: enabling disadvantaged groups (SCs, STs, OBCs, women, minorities, persons with disabilities, transgender persons) to gain rights, resources, dignity and voice - social, economic, educational and political dimensions.
- Constitutional tools: Art 15(4), 16(4) (reservation), Art 17 (abolition of untouchability), Art 46 (DPSP for weaker sections), Arts 330 and 332 (legislative reservation); 103rd Amendment (2019) - 10% EWS quota, upheld in Janhit Abhiyan (2022).
- Judicial landmarks: Indra Sawhney (1992) - 50% ceiling and creamy layer; State of Punjab v. Davinder Singh (Aug 2024, seven-judge bench) - states may sub-classify SCs/STs for reservation.
- Protective laws: SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act 1989, RPwD Act 2016, Transgender Persons (Protection of Rights) Act 2019 (following NALSA, 2014).
- Communalism: ideology that a religious community has common secular interests opposed to other communities; Bipan Chandra identified three stages - communal nationalism, liberal communalism, extreme communalism.
- Causes of communalism: colonial divide-and-rule (separate electorates 1909), partition memory, economic competition, political mobilisation for votes, fundamentalism, misinformation on social media, weak policing.
- Major episodes: Partition riots (1947), anti-Sikh violence (1984), Mumbai riots (1992-93), Gujarat (2002), Muzaffarnagar (2013), Delhi (2020).
- Remedies: impartial and professional policing, fast-track trials, hate-speech action, peace committees, education for tolerance, inclusive development (Sachar Committee 2006 on Muslims' socio-economic status), responsible media.
- Regionalism: loyalty to a region over the nation; can be positive (demand for development, federal assertion, cultural preservation) or negative (secessionism, sons-of-the-soil, inter-state hostility).
- Causes of regionalism: uneven development, linguistic and cultural identity, historical grievances, political mobilisation; examples - Dravidian movement, Telangana (2014), Gorkhaland, Bodoland, North-East insurgencies, inter-state water disputes.
- Managing regionalism: balanced development (Aspirational Districts Programme 2018), fiscal devolution through Finance Commissions, Sarkaria (1983-88) and Punchhi (2007-10) Commission recommendations, cooperative federalism (NITI Aayog 2015), Inter-State Council.
- Secularism - Indian model: equal respect for all religions (sarva dharma sambhava) and 'principled distance' (Rajeev Bhargava), allowing state intervention for reform (temple entry, abolition of untouchability), unlike the Western wall of separation.
- Constitutional basis: Arts 25-30, Art 15, Art 44 (Uniform Civil Code); 'Secular' added to the Preamble by the 42nd Amendment (1976); S.R. Bommai (1994) held secularism part of the basic structure.
- In Nov 2024 the Supreme Court (Balram Singh v. Union of India) upheld the inclusion of 'socialist' and 'secular' in the Preamble; Uttarakhand's Uniform Civil Code came into force in Jan 2025.
- Challenges to secularism: majoritarianism, minority appeasement allegations, vote-bank politics, anti-conversion laws, hate speech, religious polarisation online.

## Punjab angle

- Punjab has the highest SC share (31.9%, Census 2011); the Davinder Singh judgment arose from Punjab's law giving preference to Balmikis and Mazhabi Sikhs within the SC quota.
- Regional issues in Punjab: Anandpur Sahib Resolution (1973) on state autonomy, river waters and the SYL canal, Chandigarh's status - federal grievances that fed the 1980s crisis.
- 1984 anti-Sikh violence: Nanavati Commission (report 2005); Delhi High Court convicted Sajjan Kumar in 2018 - an example of delayed justice in communal violence.
- Sacrilege incidents (Bargari, 2015) and resulting unrest show how religious sentiment can be mobilised; inter-faith traditions of Malerkotla and langar show Punjab's composite ethos.

Expect conceptual questions (Indian vs Western secularism; is regionalism a threat?) and problem-solution questions on communalism. Cite judgments, commissions and real episodes, and offer balanced remedies.

## Deep dive

### Regionalism in Punjab — timeline (year — event)

- 1949 — Sachar Formula divides Punjab into Punjabi- and Hindi-medium zones for schooling — the first attempt to manage the language question.
- 1955 — States Reorganisation Commission rejects a Punjabi-speaking state; 1956 — PEPSU merged with Punjab; the 'regional formula' sets up Punjabi and Hindi regional committees.
- 1960-66 — Punjabi Suba agitation under Master Tara Singh and then Sant Fateh Singh; 1966 Punjab Reorganisation Act (on the Shah Commission's report) — Haryana created and hill areas transferred to Himachal on 1 Nov 1966; Chandigarh made a Union Territory and shared capital.
- 1970 — Indira Gandhi's award gives Chandigarh to Punjab and Abohar-Fazilka areas to Haryana, but is never implemented; 1976 — Centre's notification splits surplus Ravi-Beas water between Punjab and Haryana.
- 1981-82 — water agreement and foundation of the SYL canal at Kapoori (April 1982); Akali Dharam Yudh Morcha (1982) links water, Chandigarh and the Anandpur Sahib Resolution.
- 1984-85 — Operation Blue Star (June 1984); Rajiv-Longowal Accord (24 July 1985) promises Chandigarh to Punjab, a tribunal on waters (Eradi Tribunal, 1986) and a commission on territorial claims; Longowal is assassinated within a month and the accord stalls.
- 2004 — Punjab Termination of Agreements Act cancels water-sharing pacts; 2016 — the Supreme Court in a Presidential Reference holds the Act invalid; the SYL canal remains incomplete on Punjab's side.
- 2022 onward — disputes over BBMB membership rules, Chandigarh's service rules and Haryana's claims; in 2025 a sharp Punjab-Haryana row over additional water releases from Bhakra-Beas shows the issue is alive.
- Reading: Punjab's regionalism is mostly 'federal regionalism' (water, capital, autonomy) that turned violent only when religious mobilisation, militancy and state repression combined in the 1980s.

### Communalism in colonial Punjab and Partition — roots

- Reform movements sharpened boundaries: Singh Sabha (Amritsar 1873, Lahore 1879), Arya Samaj in Punjab (Lahore branch 1877) and its shuddhi campaigns, Ahmadiyya movement (Qadian, 1889); census-taking created competition over numbers (Kenneth Jones's work).
- The Unionist Party (1923; Fazl-i-Husain, Sir Chhotu Ram, later Sikandar Hayat Khan) was a cross-communal agrarian coalition that kept Punjab's politics landlord-and-peasant based until the 1940s.
- Collapse of the Unionists after 1945-46 and the Muslim League's Pakistan campaign polarised the province; Rawalpindi riots (March 1947) preceded the August partition violence.
- Partition of Punjab (Radcliffe line, Aug 1947) brought mass killings, abductions of women and one of history's largest migrations — estimates of deaths range widely, from about two lakh to about a million.
- Memory and politics: refugee resettlement shaped Punjab's land, towns (model towns, new colonies) and politics; the Partition Museum at Amritsar (2017) records oral histories.
- Lesson for answers: communalism is modern and political, not 'ancient hatred' — it grows when elites mobilise religion for power and when the state fails to protect.

### Social empowerment — frameworks and Punjab practice

- Frameworks: Naila Kabeer — empowerment as resources, agency and achievements; Amartya Sen — expanding capabilities and freedoms; Ambedkar — 'educate, agitate, organise'.
- Dalit land rights: Punjab's Village Common Lands rules (1964) reserve one-third of the cultivable panchayat (shamlat) land put to lease for Scheduled Castes; about 1.7 lakh acres are common land in Punjab.
- Land struggle: the Zameen Prapti Sangharsh Committee (ZPSC, from about 2014 in Sangrur and Malwa) mobilises Dalits to bid collectively for reserved land; auctions are often rigged through proxy bidders and short notice; a large protest of about 10,000 people was held at Sangrur in Nov 2022.
- Political empowerment of women: Punjab raised women's reservation in panchayats and urban local bodies to 50% (approved 2017); the next challenge is ending 'sarpanch-pati' proxy rule.
- Religious-identity empowerment: Ad-Dharm (1920s), Ravidassia identity (2010) and Balmiki assertion show empowerment through cultural dignity, not only through quotas.
- Economic empowerment tools: Punjab Scheduled Castes Land Development and Finance Corporation loans, post-matric scholarships, SC sub-plan budgets, and skill-based schemes for landless youth.
- Measure of success: share of SCs in land, higher education, private jobs and elected leadership — not just the number of schemes.

### Secularism — Indian debates and Sikh religious institutions

- T.N. Madan ('Secularism in its Place', 1987) and Ashis Nandy argued that Western-style secularism is alien to deeply religious South Asian societies and may provoke backlash; Rajeev Bhargava's 'principled distance' and Amartya Sen's 'symmetric treatment' defend the Indian model.
- Essential religious practices doctrine: since Shirur Mutt (1954) courts decide which practices are 'essential' and protected under Article 25 — applied in Sabarimala (2018) and the Karnataka hijab case (split verdict, Oct 2022).
- Sikh institutions and the state: the Sikh Gurdwaras Act 1925 (a product of the Akali movement) created the SGPC, elected under state supervision — an example of the Indian state regulating religious administration.
- Miri-piri (temporal and spiritual authority) and the Akal Takht's edicts give Sikh politics a religious idiom; this coexists uneasily with secular party competition.
- Sacrilege (beadbi) politics: Punjab has repeatedly tried to legislate harsher punishment for desecration of scriptures (bills in 2016 and 2018 did not get assent); critics warn such laws can be misused, supporters see them as protecting public order.
- Criminal law: the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita 2023 (in force from 1 July 2024) carries forward the offences of promoting enmity between groups (Section 196) and outraging religious feelings (Section 299).
- Administrative tools: peace committees, MHA's communal harmony guidelines (2008), the National Foundation for Communal Harmony (1992), the National Integration Council (set up in 1961), and prompt action on hate speech per Supreme Court directions (Tehseen Poonawalla 2018 on lynching).

### PPSC patterns and answer frames

- Likely forms: 'Is regionalism a threat?', 'Indian vs Western secularism', 'causes and remedies of communalism', 'empowerment of SCs/women in Punjab'.
- Use three-level structure: concept (definition and thinker), Indian or Punjab evidence (dated events, laws, judgments), and a balanced way forward.
- For Punjab regionalism always mention water (SYL, BBMB), capital (Chandigarh), language (Punjabi Suba) and autonomy (Anandpur Sahib) — the four pillars.
- For communalism separate causes into historical, political, economic, psychological and technological (social media) — examiners reward classification.
- For empowerment give data: SC share 31.9%, land share about 3.5%, one-third shamlat reservation, women's 50% local-body reservation.
- End with constitutional morality (Ambedkar), fraternity (Preamble) and a reference to a Supreme Court judgment.

### Common traps

- Punjab was reorganised on 1 Nov 1966; the States Reorganisation Act was 1956 — different laws.
- The Rajiv-Longowal Accord was signed in 1985, after Blue Star (1984); the Anandpur Sahib Resolution dates from 1973.
- The SGPC is a statutory body under the 1925 Act, not a private society.
- 'Secular' and 'socialist' were added by the 42nd Amendment (1976), but secularism as a principle was present from 1950 through Articles 25-30.
- Sub-classification (Davinder Singh, 2024) concerns dividing the SC quota; it did not apply the creamy layer to SCs by binding law.
- The Unionist Party was a Punjab party (Muslim, Hindu Jat and Sikh landholders), not a branch of the Muslim League.

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** How is the Indian model of secularism different from the Western model? Discuss the challenges it faces today. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: secularism in the Constitution and 42nd Amendment
- Western model: strict separation, religion as private (France, USA)
- Indian model: principled distance, equal respect, state-led reform, minority rights
- Judicial support: Bommai, Balram Singh (2024)
- Challenges: majoritarianism, polarisation, hate speech, UCC debate, conversion laws
- Conclusion: constitutional morality and equal citizenship as the way forward

</details>

**2.** 'Regionalism is not necessarily a threat to national integration.' Critically examine with examples. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: define regionalism
- Positive regionalism: linguistic states, development demands, cultural pride, federal bargaining
- Negative regionalism: secessionism, sons-of-soil violence, water disputes
- Punjab example: Anandpur Sahib Resolution - legitimate autonomy demands vs militancy
- Management: fiscal federalism, special packages, dialogue, Inter-State Council
- Conclusion: accommodated regionalism strengthens unity

</details>

**3.** Discuss the causes of communalism in India and suggest measures to combat it. How does social empowerment help? (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: define communalism (Bipan Chandra)
- Causes: historical, political, economic, media
- Measures: policing, justice, hate-speech action, education, peace committees
- Empowerment link: inclusive development reduces insecurity and competition (Sachar)
- Conclusion: secular citizenship and equal opportunity are long-term antidotes

</details>
