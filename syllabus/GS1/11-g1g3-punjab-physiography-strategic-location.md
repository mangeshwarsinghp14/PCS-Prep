## Short notes

- Punjab covers 50,362 sq km (about 1.5% of India's area); approx. latitude 29.5-32.5 degrees N and longitude 73.9-76.8 degrees E; 23 districts (Malerkotla created 2021).
- Boundaries: Pakistan (west), Jammu and Kashmir (north), Himachal Pradesh (north-east), Haryana (south and south-east), Rajasthan (south-west); Chandigarh UT is the shared capital.
- Name from Persian 'panj' (five) + 'ab' (waters); present Indian Punjab has three perennial rivers - Sutlej, Beas, Ravi - and the seasonal Ghaggar in the south-east.
- Physiographic divisions: (1) Shivalik foothills in the north-east; (2) Kandi (piedmont) belt below them, cut by seasonal torrents called 'chos'; (3) alluvial plain covering most of the state; (4) sandy, semi-arid tract with dunes (tibbas) in the south-west.
- Shivalik/Kandi zone (Pathankot, Gurdaspur, Hoshiarpur, Rupnagar, SAS Nagar): erosion, deforestation, low groundwater, rain-fed farming; Kandi Area Development schemes target it.
- Alluvial plain: Bet/khadar (low flood plains along rivers, new alluvium) and higher uplands (old alluvium); land slopes gently from north-east to south-west.
- Doabs: Bari Doab (Beas-Ravi), Bist Doab (Beas-Sutlej); cultural regions - Majha (Amritsar, Tarn Taran, Gurdaspur), Doaba (Jalandhar, Hoshiarpur, Kapurthala, SBS Nagar), Malwa (south of the Sutlej, the largest region), Puadh (around Rupnagar-Mohali).
- Rivers and confluences: the Sutlej enters the plains at Rupnagar; the Beas joins the Sutlej at Harike, where the Harike headworks feed the Rajasthan (Indira Gandhi) canal; the Ravi forms part of the border in Gurdaspur-Pathankot.
- Dams: Bhakra (Sutlej, Gobind Sagar - in HP), Pong (Beas, in HP), Ranjit Sagar/Thein dam on the Ravi (Pathankot); Shahpur Kandi project downstream on the Ravi (completed 2024) to use water earlier flowing to Pakistan.
- Climate: continental, semi-arid to sub-humid; very hot summers, cold winters with fog; rainfall decreases from north-east (over 1,000 mm in sub-montane areas) to south-west (around 400 mm or less); winter rain from Western Disturbances.
- Soils: mostly alluvial; sandy/desert soils in the south-west; Kandi soils in the north-east; saline-alkaline patches and waterlogging in the south-west (Sri Muktsar Sahib, Fazilka).
- Forests: among the lowest forest cover in India (under 4% of area, ISFR); Shivalik forests in the north-east; Bir forests near Patiala.
- Wetlands: six Ramsar sites - Harike, Kanjli, Ropar (2002) and Keshopur-Miani, Beas Conservation Reserve, Nangal (2019); Harike hosts migratory birds and the Indus river dolphin occurs in the Beas.
- International Border: about 553 km with Pakistan along six districts - Pathankot, Gurdaspur, Amritsar, Tarn Taran, Ferozepur, Fazilka; guarded by BSF; fencing with riverine gaps.
- Strategic significance: historic gateway for invasions from the north-west; frontline in the 1965 and 1971 wars; major cantonments; Amritsar is about 50 km from Lahore.
- Border crossings: Attari-Wagah Integrated Check Post (2012) was India's only land trade route with Pakistan - trade curtailed after 2019 and the ICP closed in April 2025; Kartarpur Corridor (opened Nov 2019, Dera Baba Nanak) suspended by India in May 2025.
- Security challenges: drones dropping drugs and weapons, narcotics from the 'Golden Crescent', infiltration across rivers; BSF jurisdiction extended from 15 km to 50 km from the border in Punjab (Oct 2021), which Punjab challenged in the Supreme Court.
- Indus Waters Treaty (1960) gave India the eastern rivers (Ravi, Beas, Sutlej); India placed the treaty in abeyance in April 2025.

## Punjab angle

- Border Area Development Programme and state schemes target the six border districts, which lag in industry, education and health.
- Kandi Area Development and watershed projects address erosion and water scarcity in the Shivalik foothills.
- Harike wetland (Tarn Taran/Ferozepur), one of the largest man-made wetlands in northern India, is important for migratory birds and the Indus river dolphin conservation.
- Farmers with land beyond the border fence work under BSF-regulated timings and receive compensation - a frequent local grievance.
- Border districts face drug-addiction and smuggling pressures; the 'Yudh Nashian Virudh' campaign (2025) is the state's flagship anti-drug drive.

## Deep dive

### Geology and micro-landforms (term — meaning — where in Punjab)

- Shivalik hills — youngest Himalayan range, made of soft, poorly consolidated sandstones, clays and boulder conglomerates laid down by Himalayan rivers (Mio-Pliocene to Pleistocene) — Pathankot, Gurdaspur, Hoshiarpur, Rupnagar, SAS Nagar; easily eroded once forest cover is lost.
- Kandi — piedmont belt of coarse gravel and boulders at the foot of the Shivaliks (comparable to the bhabar of UP); porous, so rainwater sinks and the water table lies deep — rain-fed maize, wheat and fodder.
- Sirowal — the zone below the kandi where finer material and seepage bring the water table close to the surface; historically marshy, now well cultivated.
- Chos (PPSC 2021) — seasonal torrents that drain the Shivaliks; dry most of the year, they flood suddenly in the monsoon, widen their sandy beds, bury fields under sand and shift course; Hoshiarpur district was historically the worst affected.
- Bet (khadar) — low flood plain of new alluvium along the Sutlej, Beas, Ravi and Ghaggar; fertile but flood-prone; Dhaya — the steep bank or bluff (a few metres high) that separates bet land from the older upland.
- Upland plain (bangar / old alluvium) — the bulk of the state; deep loam to sandy loam soils, slopes very gently from north-east to south-west, which guides canal alignments and drainage.
- Tibbas — sand dunes and sandy ridges in Bathinda, Mansa, Fazilka and Sri Muktsar Sahib; many were levelled after the Green Revolution for canal and tubewell irrigation.
- Palaeochannels — buried old river courses; the Ghaggar-Hakra channel through Patiala-Sangrur-Mansa is linked by many scholars with the Vedic Saraswati and the Harappan sites (Ropar, Sanghol, Kotla Nihang Khan).
- Punjab Land Preservation Act 1900 — an early colonial law to stop grazing and tree-felling in the Shivaliks to control chos erosion; still the basis for closures of the hills.

### Chos and the Kandi belt — problems and remedies (PPSC 2021 asked)

- Causes: deforestation and over-grazing of the friable Shivaliks, steep slopes, intense monsoon bursts, and mining of sand and gravel from cho beds.
- Problems: sand-casting of fertile land, damage to roads, bridges and villages, gully erosion, falling water tables in the kandi, siltation of downstream drains and reservoirs.
- Socio-economic effects: small rain-fed holdings, low incomes, out-migration of youth, and the 'Kandi area' being among Punjab's poorer sub-regions.
- Remedies: afforestation and closure of hills under the 1900 Act, check-dams and gully plugs, cho channelisation and embankments, contour bunding, agroforestry (eucalyptus, poplar, bamboo), fodder banks.
- Programmes: Kandi Watershed and Area Development Project (World Bank-aided, 1990s), the Kandi Canal (about 130 km from Talwara via Hoshiarpur to Balachaur) to bring irrigation to the kandi belt, low-dams on Shivalik streams.
- Answer frame: define chos, show location on a sketch map, list problems in three groups (physical, economic, social) and end with an integrated watershed approach.

### Rivers, headworks and canals — Punjab's water geography

- Ravi: Ranjit Sagar (Thein) dam near Pathankot (commissioned 2001) and the Madhopur headworks, which feed the Upper Bari Doab Canal (opened 1859) — the oldest major British-era canal in Punjab; the Shahpur Kandi project between them was completed in 2024.
- Beas: Pong dam (Himachal, 1974) forms Maharana Pratap Sagar; the Beas-Sutlej Link at Pandoh diverts Beas water through tunnels to the Sutlej at Dehar; the Beas joins the Sutlej at Harike.
- Sutlej: Bhakra dam (1963) and Nangal; Ropar headworks feed the Sirhind Canal (opened 1882) that irrigates the Malwa plain, and the Bist Doab canal for the Doaba.
- Harike headworks (built in the early 1950s) at the Beas-Sutlej confluence feed the Rajasthan Feeder (Indira Gandhi Canal) and the Sirhind Feeder; the reservoir behind it created Harike wetland.
- Ferozepur (Hussainiwala) headworks feed the Gang (Bikaner) Canal (1927) to Sri Ganganagar and the Eastern Canal; Hussainiwala came to India in 1961 in exchange for villages near Sulemanki, and it holds the memorial of Bhagat Singh, Rajguru and Sukhdev.
- Ghaggar: seasonal river from the Shivaliks through Patiala, Sangrur and Mansa into Haryana-Rajasthan; floods in 2023 when its embankments breached; Tangri and Markanda are tributaries in the Haryana side.
- Drains and pollution: Buddha Nullah (Ludhiana) and the East Bein carry industrial and urban waste into the Sutlej; the Kali Bein at Sultanpur Lodhi was cleaned through Sant Balbir Singh Seechewal's kar seva from 2000 — a celebrated community model.
- Inter-state issues: SYL canal, BBMB control of Bhakra-Beas waters and the riparian principle (Punjab argues it alone is riparian to the Ravi-Beas-Sutlej within India).

### Agro-climatic zones and climate of Punjab

- National scheme: the Planning Commission's 15 agro-climatic regions (1989) place Punjab in Region VI, the Trans-Gangetic Plains, with Haryana, Delhi, Chandigarh and the Sri Ganganagar area of Rajasthan.
- PAU's zoning (six zones): I Sub-mountain undulating and II Undulating plain (Pathankot, Gurdaspur, Hoshiarpur, Rupnagar, SAS Nagar); III Central plain (Amritsar, Tarn Taran, Kapurthala, Jalandhar, Ludhiana, Fatehgarh Sahib, Patiala, Sangrur); IV Western plain (Ferozepur, Moga, Barnala, Faridkot); V Western zone (Sri Muktsar Sahib, Fazilka, Bathinda, Mansa); VI Flood plain (bet belts along the Beas, Sutlej, Ravi and Ghaggar).
- Zone character: I-II — highest rainfall, kandi soils, maize-wheat, erosion; III — the irrigated rice-wheat heartland with the deepest groundwater decline; IV — mixed rice-wheat with some cotton; V — semi-arid, sandy, canal-fed cotton-kinnow-wheat belt with waterlogging and salinity; VI — bet lands with floods and seasonal crops.
- Koppen: the north-east foothills are humid subtropical with dry winter (Cwa); the south-west grades into hot semi-arid steppe (BSh).
- Seasons: cold season (Dec-Feb) with fog and frost and western-disturbance rain; hot season (Apr-June) with dust storms and loo; south-west monsoon (July-Sept) brings about 70-80% of annual rain; post-monsoon (Oct-Nov) is the paddy harvest and stubble-burning window.
- Rainfall gradient: highest in the Shivalik districts and lowest in Fazilka-Bathinda-Mansa — this gradient explains rain-fed farming in the north-east and dependence on canals and tubewells in the south-west.
- Soils by zone: kandi and sub-montane soils in the north-east, loams in the central plain, sandy and desert soils with salinity-alkalinity in the south-west; rising water tables in parts of the south-west (Muktsar, Fazilka) are the opposite problem to central Punjab's falling tables.
- PPSC 2026 asked how physiographic regions shape soils, irrigation and cropping systems — the zone table above is the backbone of that answer.

### Upper Bari Doab — salient features (PPSC 2021 asked)

- Location: between the Beas and the Ravi in Gurdaspur, Pathankot (part), Amritsar and Tarn Taran — the core of the Majha region.
- Relief: sub-montane north (Pathankot-Gurdaspur) with kandi and chos, then a level alluvial plain sloping south-west towards the Ravi and the border.
- Drainage: bounded by the two rivers, with seasonal nallahs and drains, such as the Hudiara drain, flowing towards Pakistan; the riverine belt (bet) along the Ravi is flood-prone.
- Irrigation: the Upper Bari Doab Canal from Madhopur headworks plus a dense tubewell network; rice-wheat dominates, with basmati as a speciality of the Majha.
- Soils: fertile loams and clay loams; patches of waterlogging and kallar (salt) in low areas were historical problems.
- Strategic layer: the doab is a frontline region (Dera Baba Nanak, Ajnala, Attari) with fenced border, BSF posts and land beyond the fence.
- Settlement: dense nucleated villages, historic towns (Amritsar, Batala, Gurdaspur, Tarn Taran) and religious centres (Golden Temple, Kartarpur corridor point at Dera Baba Nanak).

### Border geography and strategic history (year — event)

- 1947 — Radcliffe Award (announced 17 Aug): Muslim-majority Gurdaspur, Batala and Pathankot tehsils went to India, giving the only road access to Jammu and Kashmir through Pathankot; Shakargarh tehsil went to Pakistan; Ferozepur and Zira tehsils stayed with India, keeping the Ferozepur headworks.
- 1961 — exchange of Hussainiwala (Ferozepur headworks area) to India for villages near Sulemanki; the Hussainiwala Retreat ceremony continues alongside Attari-Wagah and Sadqi (Fazilka).
- 1965 war — Battle of Asal Uttar near Khem Karan (Tarn Taran), where Pakistani Patton tanks were halted; CQMH Abdul Hamid won the Param Vir Chakra; Indian thrusts towards Lahore through Dograi and Barki.
- 1971 war — fighting on the Hussainiwala and Fazilka fronts; Punjab border villages were evacuated, an experience repeated during later mobilisations (2001-02 Operation Parakram).
- 1988 — at the height of militancy, fencing of the Punjab stretch of the India-Pakistan border began (Gurdaspur, Amritsar, Ferozepur) to stop militants and arms crossing; the Punjab sector was later fully fenced and floodlit.
- 2015-16 — Dinanagar (Gurdaspur, July 2015) and Pathankot air base (Jan 2016) attacks showed infiltration through riverine and nallah gaps.
- May 2025 — during Operation Sindoor (from 7 May) Pakistani drones and projectiles targeted border districts; blackouts and evacuations were ordered until the ceasefire of 10 May; Attari-Wagah trade and the Kartarpur Corridor had already been halted.
- Geopolitical value: Punjab lies on the shortest land route to Afghanistan and Central Asia (Grand Trunk Road), but this potential stays locked by India-Pakistan hostility.

### Common confusions

- Kandi is the gravelly piedmont belt; chos are the seasonal torrents that cross it — two different things.
- Bist Doab lies between the Beas and Sutlej; Bari Doab between the Beas and Ravi — 'Bari' takes its name from Beas + Ravi.
- The Sirhind Canal comes from the Ropar headworks; the Sirhind Feeder comes from Harike — different works.
- Bhakra and Pong dams are in Himachal Pradesh, not Punjab; Ranjit Sagar (Thein) is in Punjab near Pathankot.
- The Ghaggar is not one of Punjab's 'five rivers'; the five were the Sutlej, Beas, Ravi, Chenab and Jhelum of undivided Punjab.
- Harike wetland is man-made (formed behind the 1950s barrage), unlike natural wetlands such as Kanjli on the Kali Bein.
