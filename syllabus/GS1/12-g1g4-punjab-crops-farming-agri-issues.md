## Short notes

- Punjab is a leading contributor of wheat and rice to the central pool; net sown area is over 80% of geographical area, cropping intensity about 190%, and almost the entire cropped area is irrigated.
- Rabi crops: wheat (dominant), mustard, gram, barley, potato; Kharif crops: paddy (including basmati), cotton, maize, sugarcane, moong; horticulture - kinnow (Abohar-Fazilka belt), potato seed (Doaba), vegetables.
- Cotton belt in the south-west (Bathinda, Mansa, Fazilka, Sri Muktsar Sahib); maize and sugarcane in Doaba and the north-east; basmati in Majha.
- Green Revolution (mid-1960s): high-yielding dwarf wheat and rice, assured irrigation, fertilisers, MSP and procurement; Punjab Agricultural University (Ludhiana, 1962) drove research and extension.
- Wheat-paddy cycle: assured MSP and procurement made paddy-wheat the dominant rotation, displacing pulses, oilseeds and coarse grains.
- Groundwater crisis: CGWB assessments (2022-24) put Punjab's stage of groundwater extraction above 150%, the highest among states, with most blocks over-exploited; water tables falling sharply in central Punjab.
- Causes: water-intensive paddy in a semi-arid zone, free electricity for farm tubewells (since 1997), early transplanting, decline of canal irrigation share.
- Punjab Preservation of Subsoil Water Act 2009 bars paddy transplanting before a notified date in June to reduce evaporation losses; Punjab Water Resources (Management and Regulation) Act 2020 created a water regulatory authority.
- Remedies: crop diversification (Johl Committee reports 1986, 2002), direct seeding of rice (state incentive of ₹1,500 per acre), short-duration varieties such as PR-126 and a ban on long-duration PUSA-44 (seed sale banned from 2024), micro-irrigation, canal rejuvenation, groundwater recharge.
- Stubble burning: paddy straw burnt after combine harvesting due to a short window before wheat; fire counts fell from about 36,600 (2023) to about 10,900 (2024); solutions - in-situ (Happy Seeder, Super Seeder, Super SMS) and ex-situ (biomass power, compressed biogas, 2G ethanol).
- Soil health: excessive urea and nutrient imbalance, zinc and other micronutrient deficiencies, falling organic carbon; Soil Health Card scheme.
- Pest and chemical issues: whitefly attack on cotton (2015) and pink bollworm; heavy pesticide use and concerns over cancer prevalence in Malwa.
- Economic distress: small and shrinking holdings, rising input costs, stagnating yields, high rural indebtedness, farmer suicides; dependence on MSP and the arhtiya (commission agent) system.
- Political economy: 2020 farm laws triggered prolonged protests led largely by Punjab farmers; the laws were repealed in Nov 2021; demand for a legal guarantee of MSP continues.
- Modern farming concepts: precision farming, protected cultivation (polyhouses), natural/organic farming, agroforestry (poplar, eucalyptus), integrated farming (dairy, fisheries, beekeeping), FPOs, contract farming, drones for spraying, digital agriculture (AgriStack), hydroponics.
- Recent stress: agriculture GVA contracted 2.5% in 2025-26 due to the 2025 floods; agriculture forms about 23% of Punjab's GSVA (Punjab Economic Survey 2025-26).

## Punjab angle

- Direct Seeded Rice incentive of ₹1,500 per acre and the 'Pani Bachao Paise Kamao' pilot for metered power are state attempts to save water.
- A draft State Agriculture Policy (2023) by an expert committee recommended diversification, water conservation and support for small farmers.
- Central Crop Residue Management scheme (from 2018) subsidises residue machinery; Punjab promotes CBG plants that use paddy straw.
- Kinnow clusters around Abohar-Fazilka and Hoshiarpur, and the seed potato hub in Jalandhar-Hoshiarpur, show successful diversification.
- PAU Ludhiana's Package of Practices and Krishi Vigyan Kendras are the main extension channels.

## How PPSC asks it

Expect questions on the wheat-paddy cycle, groundwater depletion, stubble burning, diversification and modern farming practices. Examiners reward Punjab data, scheme names, causes-effects and a practical way forward balancing farmer income and ecology.

## Deep dive

### From canal colonies to the Green Revolution — agrarian timeline (year — event)

- 1859 — Upper Bari Doab Canal opens; 1882 — Sirhind Canal; colonial 'perennial canal' irrigation turns the doabs into surplus grain areas.
- 1885-1940 — Canal colonies in west Punjab (Lyallpur/Chenab colony from 1892, Montgomery, Shahpur): planned villages settled by peasant-soldiers from central Punjab; most of this land went to Pakistan in 1947.
- 1900 — Punjab Alienation of Land Act restricts sale of land by 'agricultural tribes' to moneylenders; 1930s — Sir Chhotu Ram's debt-relief laws (Punjab Relief of Indebtedness Act 1934 and others).
- 1925 — Malcolm Darling's 'The Punjab Peasant in Prosperity and Debt' shows that prosperity from canals coexisted with heavy debt to moneylenders — the long roots of today's debt problem.
- 1947-55 — Partition resettlement and consolidation of holdings (chakbandi) — Punjab was the first state to complete consolidation, which later made tubewells and tractors viable.
- 1962 — Punjab Agricultural University (Ludhiana); 1965-66 — Mexican dwarf wheat (Lerma Rojo, Sonora 64) and later IR-8 rice; MSP and FCI procurement (1965) make wheat-paddy risk-free.
- 1997 — free electricity for farm tubewells; 2009 — Punjab Preservation of Subsoil Water Act delays paddy transplanting; 2020-21 — farm-law agitation; 2023 and 2025 — floods; 2024-25 — ban on PUSA-44 and push to short-duration paddy.

### Farmer distress — the data (survey/source — figure)

- Income: NSS 77th round Situation Assessment Survey (July 2018-June 2019) — average monthly income of an agricultural household in Punjab Rs 26,701, second only to Meghalaya (Rs 29,348), against an all-India Rs 10,218.
- Debt: the same survey — average outstanding loan per agricultural household in Punjab Rs 2,03,249 against an all-India Rs 74,121; among the highest with Andhra Pradesh and Kerala.
- Suicides: a door-to-door census by PAU, Punjabi University and GNDU counted 16,606 farmer and farm-labourer suicides in Punjab in 2000-2015 (report released Jan 2018); about 87% were linked to debt and about 76% of farmer cases involved holdings under 5 acres.
- Landless labourers, mostly Dalit, form a large share of rural suicides — PAU's field study found the suicide rate among agricultural labourers higher than among farmers.
- Sources of credit: commission agents (arhtiyas) remain a major informal lender because they also handle the farmer's crop sale; institutional crop loans are often rolled over rather than repaid.
- Cost squeeze: rising diesel, fertiliser, pesticide, machinery and land-lease rents; mechanisation is excessive for small holdings (Punjab has far more tractors than its cropped area needs, a much-cited inefficiency).
- Distress drivers beyond money: stagnant yields of wheat and rice, crop losses from floods, hail and pests (whitefly 2015, pink bollworm 2021 in cotton), and the social cost of drugs and emigration.
- Use in answers: pair income (high) with debt (very high) — Punjab's paradox is high income with high indebtedness, i.e. a 'debt-financed' farm economy.

### Water resources — latest assessment and the irrigation economy

- Punjab's Dynamic Groundwater Resource Assessment for 2025-26 (reported Sept 2026): annual recharge 19.14 BCM, extractable 17.29 BCM, but extraction 26.32 BCM — a stage of extraction of about 152%; 24.95 BCM of it goes to irrigation.
- Block status (2025-26): of 153 blocks, 110 over-exploited, 6 critical, 17 semi-critical and only 20 safe — a slight improvement from 115 over-exploited blocks two years earlier.
- Two opposite problems: falling water tables in the central plain (Sangrur, Barnala, Moga, Patiala, Ludhiana, Jalandhar) and rising water tables with waterlogging and salinity in the south-west canal belt (Sri Muktsar Sahib, Fazilka, parts of Faridkot).
- Irrigation mix: tubewells (around 14 lakh farm connections on free power) irrigate most of the area; canals irrigate a smaller and shrinking share as many distributaries silted up or lost tail-end supply — hence the state's canal-revival drive in 2023-25.
- Water quality: arsenic and uranium traces in parts of Majha and Malwa groundwater, fluoride and salinity in the south-west, and nitrate from fertilisers; industrial effluents in Buddha Nullah and the Sutlej.
- Surface water politics: Punjab argues it has no surplus to share through the SYL canal; disputes with Haryana and Rajasthan over Bhakra-Beas water allocations and BBMB decisions recur, most visibly in 2025.
- PPSC 2018 asked how groundwater depletion relates to cropping pattern; PPSC 2026 asked the status of water resources and the ecological and socio-economic fallout of over-exploitation — use the block figures and the two-problem framing.

### Corporate and contract farming, leasing and modern models (PPSC 2021 asked)

- Contract farming: the buyer agrees in advance to purchase a crop at a set price or formula, often supplying seed and advice; Punjab's early example was PepsiCo's tomato processing plant at Zahura (Hoshiarpur, 1989) and later potato contracts.
- Corporate farming: a company owns or leases land and runs farming directly with hired labour; land ceiling laws (Punjab Land Reforms Act 1972) and the farm-law protests (2020-21) show strong resistance to corporate control of land.
- Laws: Punjab Contract Farming Act 2013; the Centre's Model Contract Farming Act 2018; the repealed Farmers (Empowerment and Protection) Agreement on Price Assurance and Farm Services Act 2020.
- Pros: assured market and price, technology transfer, credit linkage, quality-based premiums (seed potato, basmati, barley for malt).
- Cons: unequal bargaining power, rejection of produce on quality grounds, delayed payments, monocropping, weak dispute settlement (under the repealed 2020 central law the SDM was the first forum, which farmers felt was too weak against companies).
- Land leasing: a large share of Punjab's land is cultivated by informal tenants on cash rent; NITI Aayog's Model Agricultural Land Leasing Act 2016 aims to legalise leasing so tenants can get credit, insurance and compensation.
- Farmer Producer Organisations (FPOs) and cooperatives (Verka/Milkfed, Markfed, sugar cooperatives) are the bargaining-power alternative to corporate farming.
- Resource-conserving technologies: laser land levelling, zero-till and Happy/Super Seeder sowing, direct-seeded rice, tensiometers to schedule irrigation, drip for kinnow and vegetables, solar pumps under PM-KUSUM.

### Productivity, sustainability and diversification — an analytical frame (PPSC 2026 asked)

- Yield plateau: wheat and rice yields in Punjab are among India's highest but have grown slowly since the late 1990s; further gains need better varieties, soil health and precision input use rather than more water and fertiliser.
- Climate stress: a premature heatwave in March 2022 shrivelled wheat grains and cut yields; floods in 2023 and 2025 destroyed paddy and delayed wheat sowing; fog and smog cut sunshine in winter.
- Soil: falling organic carbon, imbalanced NPK use, zinc and sulphur deficiencies, compaction from heavy machinery; residue retention and green manuring (dhaincha) help.
- Allied sectors: Punjab has India's highest per-capita milk availability among states; dairy, poultry, fisheries on saline lands in the south-west (shrimp farming in Fazilka-Muktsar), and beekeeping diversify income.
- Diversification options by zone: maize, sugarcane and agroforestry in the sub-montane belt; vegetables, potato and basmati in the central plain; cotton, kinnow and guar in the south-west.
- Barriers: assured MSP procurement exists in practice only for wheat and paddy; alternative crops face price risk, weak value chains and higher pest risk.
- Policy ideas: crop-neutral income support per acre, procurement or price-deficiency payment for maize and pulses, water-saving payments, metered but compensated power, regional processing clusters.
- Answer frame: Productivity (yields, inputs) + Sustainability (water, soil, air) + Viability (income, debt) + Policy (MSP, power, markets) — four Ps that cover any Punjab agriculture question.

### Common traps

- Punjab's average household farm income is high (second in India), so 'poverty' is not the right word for its farmers — 'indebtedness and stagnation' is.
- The 2009 Subsoil Water Act regulates the date of transplanting, not the area under paddy.
- Over-exploited means extraction above 100% of annual extractable recharge; 'critical' is 90-100%; 'semi-critical' 70-90%; 'safe' 70% or less.
- Contract farming does not transfer land ownership; corporate farming may involve owning or leasing land.
- Darling's book (1925) is about undivided Punjab, including the canal colonies now in Pakistan — use it for history, not current data.
- Mahila Kisan Diwas and women farmers' role are often asked under Society (women) rather than Geography — link the two papers when answering.

**1.** 'Punjab's Green Revolution success has turned into an ecological crisis.' Discuss with reference to the wheat-paddy cycle and suggest a strategy for crop diversification. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: food security contribution of Punjab
- Drivers of wheat-paddy: MSP, procurement, free power, HYVs
- Ecological costs: groundwater depletion, soil degradation, stubble burning, pesticide load
- Barriers to diversification: no assured market for alternatives, price risk, lower returns
- Strategy: price support or procurement for maize, pulses, cotton; value chains and agro-processing; DSR; horticulture and dairy; FPOs
- Conclusion: diversification as both ecological and economic necessity

**2.** Analyse the causes of groundwater depletion in Punjab and evaluate the measures taken to address it. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: CGWB data - extraction above 150%, over-exploited blocks
- Causes: paddy in semi-arid zone, free power, early transplanting, decline of canal use, urban demand
- Measures: 2009 Subsoil Water Act, 2020 Water Act and authority, DSR incentive, short-duration varieties, micro-irrigation, canal revival
- Evaluation: partial success, political sensitivity of free power
- Way forward: crop-water pricing signals, recharge, diversification, community water budgeting
- Conclusion: water security is Punjab's survival issue

</details>

**3.** Explain the problem of stubble burning in Punjab and discuss how modern farming concepts can provide sustainable solutions. (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: paddy straw volume and short harvest-sowing window
- Impacts: air pollution in north India, soil nutrient loss, health
- In-situ solutions: Happy Seeder, Super Seeder, bio-decomposers
- Ex-situ: CBG, biomass power, 2G ethanol, paddy straw supply chains
- Recent trend: sharp fall in fire counts in 2024
- Conclusion: incentives plus diversification for lasting fix

</details>
