# Ranjit Singh — power, administration, British

> Official syllabus line: Ranjit Singh's rise to power, civil and military administration and relations with the British.

[Study this in the app](https://mangeshwarsinghp14.github.io/PCS-Prep/#node/g1h7) · [Index](../README.md)

## Short notes

- Ranjit Singh was born on 13 Nov 1780 at Gujranwala to Mahan Singh, chief of the Sukerchakia misl; lost the sight of one eye to smallpox in childhood; succeeded his father in 1792.
- Political setting: Punjab divided among 12 Sikh misls, Afghan invasions under Zaman Shah, Rajput hill chiefs, Muslim chiefs of Kasur, Multan, and the British advancing to the Sutlej.
- Factors in his rise: weakness and feuds of misls, decline of Afghan power, marriage alliance with the Kanhaiya misl (Mehtab Kaur) and support of mother-in-law Sada Kaur, alliance with the Nakai misl, his own military and diplomatic skill.
- Capture of Lahore on 7 July 1799; proclaimed Maharaja on Baisakhi 1801; ruled as 'Sarkar-i-Khalsa' and issued coins (Nanakshahi) in the name of the Sikh Gurus rather than his own.
- Conquests: Amritsar (1805) from the Bhangis, Kasur (1807), Kangra (1809, against the Gorkhas), Attock (1813), Multan (1818), Kashmir (1819), Dera Ismail Khan and Dera Ghazi Khan (early 1820s), Peshawar (annexed 1834).
- Obtained the Koh-i-noor from Shah Shuja (1813); General Hari Singh Nalwa secured the north-west frontier and died at Jamrud (1837).
- Central administration: monarchy with the Maharaja as fountain of authority; key officials - Raja Dhian Singh (wazir/prime minister), Fakir Azizuddin (foreign affairs), Diwan Bhawani Das and Raja Dina Nath (finance), Misr Beli Ram (toshakhana); appointments regardless of religion.
- Provincial administration: kingdom divided into subas (Lahore, Multan, Kashmir, Peshawar) under nazims; parganas under kardars; mauzas (villages) run by panchayats, muqaddams and patwaris.
- Land revenue - the main source of income: batai (crop-sharing), kankut (estimate of standing crop) and later zabti/cash assessment; share varied by fertility; jagirs granted for service; other income from customs, nazrana and fines.
- Justice: no codified law; decisions on custom and religious law; adalats at Lahore and in provinces; fines common, capital punishment rare.
- Military reforms: Fauj-i-Ain (regular army - infantry, cavalry, artillery) and Fauj-i-Be-Qawaid (irregular - ghorcharas, jagirdari forces); regular monthly pay and drill on European lines.
- European officers: Jean-Baptiste Ventura (infantry) and Jean-Francois Allard (cavalry) raised the Fauj-i-Khas (1822); Paolo Avitabile and Claude Auguste Court (artillery); Ilahi Bakhsh led the topkhana; Akalis under Phula Singh.
- Relations with the British: Metcalfe mission (1808); Treaty of Amritsar (25 April 1809) fixed the Sutlej as the boundary and placed cis-Sutlej states under British protection.
- Later relations: Ropar meeting with Lord William Bentinck (Oct 1831); British checked his advance into Sindh and Shikarpur; British occupation of Ferozepur; Tripartite Treaty (1838) with Shah Shuja and the British over Afghanistan.
- Assessment of British policy: he avoided war with the British, preserving his kingdom but allowing the British to encircle it; critics call it timidity, supporters call it realism given British strength.
- Died on 27 June 1839 at Lahore; absence of strong institutions and a clear succession led to rapid decline after his death.
- Legacy: secular, tolerant state; patronage of Harmandir Sahib's gilding; a modern army; unified Punjab from the Sutlej to the Khyber.

## Punjab angle

- Ram Bagh (Summer Palace) in Amritsar houses the Maharaja Ranjit Singh Panorama; Gobindgarh Fort (Amritsar) was strengthened during his reign.
- Gilding of Harmandir Sahib under his patronage gave it the name 'Golden Temple'.
- Maharaja Ranjit Singh Punjab Technical University (Bathinda) and the Maharaja Ranjit Singh Armed Forces Preparatory Institute (Mohali) carry his name.
- A 2020 poll by BBC World Histories magazine named Ranjit Singh the 'greatest leader of all time' - useful as a contemporary hook.

## How PPSC asks it

A PPSC favourite: rise to power, civil and military administration, and Anglo-Sikh relations (Treaty of Amritsar 1809). Use names of officials and generals, dates of conquests and a balanced verdict on his British policy.

## Deep dive

### Rise to power: a dated sequence (1792–1809)

- 1792: Mahan Singh died and the 12-year-old Ranjit Singh became Sukerchakia chief. His mother Raj Kaur and Diwan Lakhpat Rai ran affairs at first, and his mother-in-law Sada Kaur (Kanhaiya) was the key ally.
- Marriage alliances: Mehtab Kaur (Kanhaiya, 1796) and Raj Kaur, also called Datar Kaur (Nakai, 1798), the mother of Kharak Singh.
- 1797–99: Zaman Shah's invasions. The misls retreated before him, while Ranjit Singh harassed his army; by tradition he later returned guns lost in the Jhelum and gained standing with the Afghans.
- 7 July 1799: Ranjit Singh entered Lahore with Sada Kaur's help, invited by Lahore notables (Mian Ashiq Muhammad, Mian Mohkam Din) against the Bhangi sardars Chet Singh, Mohar Singh and Sahib Singh.
- 1800: battle of Bhasin. A coalition of Gulab Singh Bhangi, Jassa Singh Ramgarhia, Sahib Singh of Gujrat and Nizamuddin of Kasur broke up without a real fight, and Ranjit Singh's hold on Lahore was confirmed.
- Baisakhi (12 April) 1801: investiture as Maharaja by Baba Sahib Singh Bedi. The state was styled Sarkar-i-Khalsa; he struck the Nanakshahi rupee in the Gurus' names, used no throne in darbar, and was addressed as 'Singh Sahib'.
- Amritsar was taken from the Bhangi widow Mai Sukhan in 1802 (some texts give 1805), along with the Zamzama gun. Kasur followed in 1807 (Qutbuddin Khan).
- 1805–06: Jaswant Rao Holkar sought Sikh help against Lord Lake. A Sarbat Khalsa at Amritsar (1805) declined, and the Treaty of Lahore (1 Jan 1806) with the British made Holkar leave Punjab.
- 1806–08: three cis-Sutlej expeditions (Ludhiana, Naraingarh, Ambala area) alarmed the Malwa chiefs. The Metcalfe mission (1808) and Ochterlony's advance to Ludhiana led to the Treaty of Amritsar (25 April 1809).

### Conquests after 1809: what, when, who

- Kangra (1809): Ranjit Singh helped Sansar Chand Katoch against the Gorkhas (Amar Singh Thapa) and kept the fort for himself; Desa Singh Majithia became nazim of the hills.
- Koh-i-noor (1 June 1813): obtained from the exiled Afghan king Shah Shuja, whom Wafa Begum had brought to Lahore.
- Attock (1813): seized during a joint Kashmir expedition with the Afghan wazir Fateh Khan. Diwan Mohkam Chand defeated the Afghans at Haidru/Chhachh (13 July 1813).
- Multan (2 June 1818): Misr Diwan Chand, with Kharak Singh in nominal command, took the fort; Nawab Muzaffar Khan and his sons died fighting. Akali Sadhu Singh's dash is famous.
- Kashmir (July 1819): Misr Diwan Chand defeated Jabbar Khan at Shopian. Hari Singh Nalwa was nazim of Kashmir (1820–21) and struck the 'Hari Singhi' rupee.
- Derajat: Dera Ghazi Khan (1820–21), Mankera (1821, a hard siege) and Dera Ismail Khan.
- Nowshera/Teri (March 1823): victory over the Yusufzai and Afghan levies; Akali Phula Singh was killed. Peshawar became tributary under the Barakzai brothers Yar Mohammad and Sultan Mohammad.
- Peshawar was annexed in 1834 (Hari Singh Nalwa). Ladakh was conquered in 1834 by Zorawar Singh, general of the feudatory Gulab Singh of Jammu.
- Jamrud (30 April 1837): Dost Mohammad's army attacked the new fort and Hari Singh Nalwa was killed, but the Khyber frontier held.
- By 1839 the kingdom ran from the Sutlej to the Khyber and from Ladakh and Kashmir to Multan and the Derajat. Shikarpur and Sindh were blocked by the British.

### Central administration: offices and office-holders

- The Maharaja was the fountain of all authority but ruled in the name of the Khalsa. The Darbar met daily; there was no written constitution and he consulted favourites rather than any council.
- Wazir: Raja Dhian Singh (from 1828), the most powerful Dogra. His brothers Gulab Singh (Jammu) and Suchet Singh, and his son Hira Singh, formed the 'Dogra faction'.
- Diwans and finance: Diwan Mohkam Chand (commander, d. 1814), Diwan Ganga Ram and Bhawani Das (who organised the daftars from 1808), and Raja Dina Nath (later finance chief).
- Foreign affairs: Fakir Azizuddin, also the royal physician. His brothers Nuruddin (Lahore civil affairs) and Imamuddin (custodian of Gobindgarh fort) also served.
- Household: Misr Beli Ram (toshakhana, treasury of valuables); Jamadar Khushal Singh (deorhiwala, chamberlain), and later Tej Singh, his nephew.
- Secretariat daftars: Daftar-i-Abwab-ul-Mal (revenue, with land revenue 'jama' and other levies 'sair'), Daftar-i-Tojihat (royal household), Daftar-i-Mawajib (salaries and army pay) and Daftar-i-Roznamcha-i-Ikhrajat (daily expenditure).
- News-writers (akhbarnavis) and harkaras reported from the provinces; the Lahore akhbars are a major source (Khalsa Darbar Records).
- Composite ruling class: Sikh sardars (Majithias, Attariwalas, Sandhawalias, Nalwa), Dogras, Brahmins (the Misrs), Muslims (the Fakir brothers, Ilahi Bakhsh) and Europeans. Office went to merit and loyalty, not creed.
- Religious patronage: gilding of the Harmandir; gifts to Jwalamukhi and Kashi Vishwanath (gold plating); grants to mosques and Sufi shrines; a cow-slaughter ban. This patronage was a policy of legitimacy, not mere tolerance.

### Provincial, revenue and judicial administration

- Provinces (subas): Lahore, Multan, Kashmir and Peshawar, each under a nazim. Jammu (Gulab Singh) and some hill states were feudatories. Below the suba were parganas or taalluqas under kardars, and villages (mauzas) under the muqaddam, patwari and chaudhri, with the qanungo as record-keeper.
- The kardar was the key local official, combining revenue collection, police and petty justice. Abuses were common, and the Maharaja's tours and news-writers were the check.
- Land revenue was the main income. Methods: batai (crop division), kankut (estimate of the standing crop), zabti (cash rates per bigha for cash crops), and later ijara (farming out to the highest bidder), which became common. The state share was about two-fifths to one-third of the produce, varying with soil and irrigation.
- Concessions: remission in famine, takavi loans, and encouragement of new cultivation and wells. Indu Banga's study shows that these were pragmatic measures to expand revenue.
- Jagirs: service jagirs (with a duty to supply troops), inam (reward), dharmarth (religious) and subsistence grants. Jagirs were often resumed at death, and the Maharaja kept jagirdars dependent.
- Other revenue: nazrana (tribute and gifts), customs and transit duties (chungi), excise, fines and confiscations (zabti), monopolies such as the Khewra and Pind Dadan Khan salt mines, and Kashmir shawl duties.
- Justice: no codified law; decisions followed custom, the Shastras or the Sharia as appropriate. Kardars and nazims held courts, with an Adalat-i-Ala (high court) at Lahore and the Maharaja as final appeal. Punishments were mainly fines; mutilation was rare and capital punishment almost absent.
- Economy: Amritsar became the commercial capital, Kashmir shawls and Multan textiles flourished, and Nanakshahi rupees were minted at Lahore, Amritsar, Multan, Kashmir and Peshawar.

### Military administration

- Fauj-i-Ain (regular army): infantry in battalions (paltans), cavalry regiments and artillery, paid monthly from the treasury in later years, with European-style drill and uniforms; the drill words were often French.
- Fauj-i-Khas (1822): a model brigade raised by Jean-Baptiste Ventura (infantry) and Jean-François Allard (cavalry, dragoons) with its own eagle standard. Claude Auguste Court and Paolo Avitabile (from 1827) handled artillery and infantry; Avitabile later governed Wazirabad and Peshawar.
- Other foreigners: the Americans Alexander Gardner and Josiah Harlan; many British deserters and adventurers served briefly. Europeans had to settle, marry locally, avoid beef and grow beards, according to common accounts.
- Topkhana (artillery): by draught type, Topkhana-i-Aspi (horse), Gavi (bullock), Shutri or zamburaks (camel swivel guns) and Jinsi (mixed). Commanders were Mian Ilahi Bakhsh and Sultan Mahmud, with gun foundries at Lahore and Amritsar.
- Fauj-i-Be-Qawaid (irregular): ghorchara cavalry (Ghorchara Khas and misldar horse), jagirdari contingents and the Akalis (Nihangs) under Akali Phula Singh.
- Recruitment drew on Sikhs (the core), Dogras, Gurkhas, Punjabi Muslims, Pathans and Purbias. Regiments were mixed, and pay arrears were a chronic grievance.
- Size: grew from a few thousand regulars in the early 1800s to roughly 40,000–50,000 regulars plus irregular horse and jagirdari troops by 1839, with a large modern artillery park. Exact figures vary by source.
- Weaknesses: dependence on European and Dogra officers; neglect of the traditional Sikh cavalry ethos; and after 1839 the regimental panchayats turned the army into a political actor.
- Generals to know: Hari Singh Nalwa, Diwan Mohkam Chand, Misr Diwan Chand, Sham Singh Attariwala, Akali Phula Singh and Zorawar Singh (in Dogra service).

### Relations with the British, 1800–1839 (PSEB Class XII Ch 18)

- 1800: Mir Yusuf Ali Khan's mission to Lahore, mainly to watch Zaman Shah.
- 1806: Treaty of Lahore (with Lord Lake): Holkar was to leave Punjab and the British were to stay out of Ranjit Singh's territory while friendly.
- 1808–09: fear of Napoleon after Tilsit (1807) produced the Metcalfe mission. Ranjit Singh's Kasur talks and his 1808 cis-Sutlej expedition were followed by Ochterlony's advance. Under the Treaty of Amritsar (25 April 1809) Ranjit Singh kept cis-Sutlej lands held before 1809 but could not expand there, and he vacated Faridkot and other gains.
- 1809–30: generally cordial. Boundary frictions (Wadni, Ferozepur) were settled, and the British did not interfere with Multan, Kashmir or Peshawar.
- 1831: Alexander Burnes travelled up the Indus with dray horses gifted by William IV, which was also a survey. Ranjit Singh met Lord William Bentinck at Ropar (26 Oct 1831) amid great pageantry, while the British were negotiating separate treaties with the Sindh Amirs (Pottinger, 1832).
- 1832: Indus navigation and trade agreement, under which the British opened the Indus–Sutlej to commerce.
- 1835–38: Ferozepur was taken under British control, with a cantonment by 1838. Ranjit Singh's move on Shikarpur and Sindh was checked by Wade and Auckland (1836–37).
- 1838: Tripartite Treaty (26 June 1838) among the British, Shah Shuja and Ranjit Singh. Ranjit Singh's hold on Peshawar was recognised and Shah Shuja renounced his claims, but Ranjit Singh refused passage, so the Army of the Indus went through Sindh and the Bolan. Lord Auckland's visit (Dec 1838) was described by Emily Eden.
- Assessment: realists (N.K. Sinha, Grewal) say he saved his kingdom by avoiding a war he could not win. Critics say his compliance let the British encircle him on the Sutlej, in Sindh and at Ferozepur.

### Afghan relations and the North-West Frontier (PSEB Class XII Ch 19)

- Afghan rulers in his time: Zaman Shah (to 1800), Shah Mahmud with the Barakzai wazir Fateh Khan, Shah Shuja (ousted in 1809, at Lahore 1813–14), and Dost Mohammad Khan (Kabul from 1826, Amir from 1835).
- The 1813 joint Kashmir expedition with Fateh Khan ended in mutual betrayal, Ranjit Singh's seizure of Attock and the Afghan defeat at Haidru.
- Peshawar (1818–34): first raided in 1818, then held as a tributary under the Barakzai sardars (1823–34). Direct annexation came in 1834 under Hari Singh Nalwa.
- Sayyid Ahmad Barelvi's jihad (1826–31) among the Yusufzais was suppressed. He was killed at Balakot (May 1831) by forces under Prince Sher Singh.
- Dost Mohammad's 1835 attempt to recover Peshawar dissolved through diplomacy (Harlan's mission) and a show of force. The 1837 Jamrud battle cost Nalwa his life but not the frontier.
- Frontier management: forts (Jamrud, Shabqadar, Michni), mobile columns and harsh deterrence under Avitabile in Peshawar (1838–43), while Pathan maliks were kept under control through subsidies.
- Significance: for the first time in centuries the flow of invasion was reversed, from the plains to the hills. The British inherited this line in 1849 and built their 'scientific frontier' debates on it.

### Assessment and Mains framing (PSEB Class XII Ch 21)

- Strengths: a unifier of misl Punjab; a pragmatic diplomat; a military moderniser; a secular, composite court; patronage of trade (Amritsar) and crafts (Kashmir shawls); an accessible ruler known for personal clemency.
- Limits: a personalised state with no institutional succession or council; the rise of Dogra power at court; an army that became political after 1839; ijara farming that hurt peasants late in the reign; no planned check on British encirclement.
- Contemporary European views: Jacquemont, Baron von Hügel, Osborne and Emily Eden described his sharp curiosity and his court. Griffin (1892) called his rule a 'Sikh barrier' useful to the British.
- Model 250-word structure: (1) context of misls and Afghans, (2) rise in phases, (3) civil administration under central, provincial and revenue heads, (4) military reforms, (5) British policy as prudence versus encirclement, (6) a balanced verdict with historians' names.
- Useful quotable facts: the state was called 'Sarkar-i-Khalsa' and the coins were in the Gurus' names. He died on 27 June 1839 and his samadhi is beside the Badshahi Mosque, Lahore. The Ram Bagh summer palace and panorama are at Amritsar.
- Recent hooks: a BBC World Histories poll (2020) named him the greatest leader of all time; Maharaja Ranjit Singh's statue at Lahore fort (2019) was vandalised and restored, which made news; and Punjab institutions named after him (MRSPTU Bathinda, MRSAFPI Mohali).

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** Analyse the factors responsible for the rise of Maharaja Ranjit Singh to power. (150 words)

- Intro: Punjab at the end of the 18th century
- Political vacuum: decline of Mughals and Afghans, feuds among misls
- Matrimonial and misl alliances (Kanhaiya, Nakai); Sada Kaur's support
- Personal qualities: military leadership, diplomacy, secular outlook
