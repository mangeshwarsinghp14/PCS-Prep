## Short notes

- Values: enduring beliefs about what is desirable (honesty, compassion, tolerance, justice, responsibility) that guide behaviour; acquired through socialisation - primary (family) and secondary (school, peers, religion, media, society).
- Family: first school of values through example, love, discipline, storytelling, sharing in joint families; challenges - nuclearisation, screen time, parental absence, pressure for success.
- Society: norms, customs, religion, community institutions, civil society and media reinforce or distort values (prejudice, caste and gender bias, consumerism).
- Educational institutions: curriculum, teachers as role models, co-curricular activities (NSS, NCC, sports), value education; Kothari Commission (1964-66) stressed moral education; NEP 2020 emphasises constitutional values, ethics and holistic development.
- Gautam Buddha: Four Noble Truths and Eightfold Path, Middle Path, karuna (compassion) and maitri (loving kindness), rejection of ritualism and caste; encouraged testing teachings by reason and experience.
- Mahavira: 24th Jain Tirthankara; five vows - ahimsa, satya, asteya, aparigraha (non-possession), brahmacharya; anekantavada (many-sidedness of truth); lessons - non-violence, tolerance of other views, restraint against greed and corruption.
- Kabir (15th c.): weaver-saint of nirguna bhakti; criticised ritualism and hypocrisy in both Hindu and Muslim practice; stressed inner devotion, simplicity and human equality; his verses are included in the Guru Granth Sahib.
- Guru Nanak (1469-1539): born at Talwandi (Nankana Sahib); message of one formless God (Ik Onkar), kirat karo, naam japo, vand chhako; langar, sangat and pangat against caste; respect for women; dialogue with diverse traditions during his travels (udasis); settled at Kartarpur.
- Swami Vivekananda: Chicago Parliament of World's Religions (1893); founded Ramakrishna Mission (1897); 'Practical Vedanta' - service of humanity as worship of God; man-making, character-building education; his birthday (12 Jan) is National Youth Day.
- Jyotiba Phule: with Savitribai Phule opened a girls' school in Pune (1848); founded Satyashodhak Samaj (1873); 'Gulamgiri' (1873) attacked caste slavery; worked for widows and against infanticide.
- Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar: drove the Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act 1856; opposed child marriage and polygamy; promoted girls' schools; reformed Sanskrit College admissions; wrote the Bengali primer 'Barna Parichay'; known for compassion and integrity.
- Mahatma Gandhi: truth and non-violence, satyagraha, purity of means and ends, sarvodaya, trusteeship, swadeshi, simplicity; 'seven social sins' (Young India, 1925); the talisman of thinking of the poorest person before deciding.
- Common lessons for administrators: empathy for the marginalised, integrity, tolerance of diversity, dignity of labour, service orientation, courage to challenge injustice, simplicity and rational thinking.
- Applying values today: value-based education, ethical leadership, family dialogue, community service, media literacy and role models in public life.

## Punjab angle

- Sultanpur Lodhi (Kapurthala) - where Guru Nanak worked and received enlightenment by the Bein river; his 550th Prakash Purb (2019) coincided with the opening of the Kartarpur Corridor.
- Bhagat Puran Singh founded Pingalwara (Amritsar, 1947) to care for destitute and disabled people - a living example of seva.
- Punjab's Schools of Eminence and training of school principals abroad (Singapore, 2023) aim to improve quality and values in public education.
- Kabir's and Sheikh Farid's verses in the Guru Granth Sahib make Punjab a meeting ground of Bhakti and Sufi values.

## Deep dive

### Reformers — life map (name — key places and dates — institution or text)

- Gautam Buddha — born at Lumbini; enlightenment at Bodh Gaya; first sermon (turning the wheel of dharma) at Sarnath; parinirvana at Kushinagar; teachings preserved in the Tripitaka (Pali canon); Buddhist councils at Rajagriha, Vaishali, Pataliputra (under Ashoka) and Kashmir (under Kanishka).
- Mahavira — born at Kundagrama near Vaishali; attained kevala jnana after 12 years of austerity; nirvana at Pavapuri (Bihar); added brahmacharya to the four vows of Parshvanatha (23rd tirthankara); later split into Shvetambara and Digambara.
- Kabir — 15th-century weaver of Varanasi, raised in a Muslim julaha family; chose to die at Maghar to break the belief that death there led to hell; verses gathered in the Bijak (Kabir Panth) and the Guru Granth Sahib, where his is the largest contribution among the bhagats.
- Guru Nanak (1469-1539) — born at Talwandi (Nankana Sahib); worked at the Sultanpur Lodhi modikhana; four long journeys (udasis) to the east, south (up to Sri Lanka), north (Himalaya) and west (Mecca, Baghdad); witnessed Babur's sack of Saidpur and protested it in the Babarvani; founded Kartarpur where he farmed for his last years; named Bhai Lehna (Guru Angad) as successor.
- Swami Vivekananda (1863-1902) — disciple of Ramakrishna; meditated at the Kanyakumari rock (1892); Chicago Parliament of Religions speech (11 Sept 1893); Vedanta Society of New York (1894); Ramakrishna Mission (1 May 1897); Belur Math (1898).
- Jyotiba Phule (1827-1890) — with Savitribai opened a girls' school at Bhide Wada, Pune (1848); a home to prevent infanticide of widows' children (1863); Satyashodhak Samaj (24 Sept 1873); 'Gulamgiri' (1873, dedicated to American abolitionists); 'Shetkaryacha Asud' (The Cultivator's Whipcord) on peasant exploitation; honoured as 'Mahatma' in 1888.
- Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar (1820-1891) — principal of Sanskrit College, Calcutta (1851), opening it to non-Brahmin students; wrote 'Barna Parichay' (1855); led the campaign for the Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act (26 July 1856) and arranged the first lawful widow remarriage that December; founded the Metropolitan Institution.
- Mahatma Gandhi (1869-1948) — South Africa (1893-1914: Phoenix Settlement 1904, Tolstoy Farm 1910); 'Hind Swaraj' (1909); Champaran (1917); Sabarmati Ashram; Harijan Sevak Sangh (1932); Nai Talim at the Wardha conference (1937); Constructive Programme pamphlet (1941).

### Guru Nanak's philosophy for an egalitarian and just society (PPSC 2026 asked)

- One Creator present in all (Ik Onkar) — if the same light is in everyone, birth-based hierarchy has no basis; this is the metaphysical root of equality.
- Institutions of equality: sangat (congregation open to all), pangat and langar (everyone sits in a row and eats the same food), dharamsal (community centre) — practical anti-caste tools, not only sermons.
- Dignity of labour and honest earning: kirat karni; preferring a poor carpenter's honest bread to a rich official's feast; Guru Nanak himself farmed at Kartarpur.
- Sharing and welfare: vand chhakna and the 'Sacha Sauda' story (spending trade money to feed the hungry) — an early idea of social responsibility of wealth, close to Gandhi's trusteeship.
- Gender: he questioned the low status given to women, pointing out that women give birth to kings and sustain society — a direct challenge to ideas of female impurity.
- Critique of power: the Babarvani condemns the violence of Babur's army and the failure of rulers to protect people — an ethic of accountable rule.
- Critique of empty ritual: refusal to wear the sacred thread without inner virtue; dialogues with yogis (Sidh Gosht) and qazis stressing truthful living over outward forms.
- Dialogue and pluralism: travelled widely and debated across traditions; the Guru Granth Sahib later included Hindu and Muslim saints — a model of inter-faith respect.
- Answer frame: Equality (sangat-langar) + Justice (Babarvani) + Work and sharing (kirat, vand chhakna) + Gender + Pluralism, each linked to a present-day public value (non-discrimination, accountability, inclusive welfare).

### Kabir's critique of social evils (PPSC 2021 asked)

- Caste: Kabir mocked claims of purity by birth, asking whether Brahmins were born by a different route — all humans share one body and one blood.
- Ritualism: criticised idol worship, pilgrimages, fasting, ritual bathing and the sacred thread among Hindus, and loud calls to prayer and circumcision among Muslims, when not matched by inner devotion.
- Religious hypocrisy: equal criticism of pandits and qazis — Ram and Rahim are names of the same One.
- Simplicity and dignity of labour: a weaver who kept weaving; he valued honest work and plain living over renunciation for show.
- Inner devotion (nirguna bhakti): God is within the heart; the guru's word and the remembrance of the Name lead to liberation.
- Language: wrote in the people's tongue (sadhukkadi, a mix of dialects), so his ideas spread to the poor and illiterate — an early 'mass communication' model.
- Limits to acknowledge: some verses reflect the period's ascetic suspicion of women and worldly attachment — a balanced answer notes this.
- Legacy: influenced the Bhakti and Sikh traditions, Kabir Panth, and modern thinkers (Tagore translated 100 poems in 1915); Dalit movements claim him as an anti-caste voice.

### Gandhian model of social development — relevance today (PPSC 2021 asked)

- Core ideas: Sarvodaya (welfare of all, from Ruskin's 'Unto This Last'), Antyodaya (the last person first), Swadeshi, Gram Swaraj (self-reliant village republics), Trusteeship, Nai Talim (education through productive work), non-violence and purity of means.
- Constructive Programme: communal unity, removal of untouchability, prohibition, khadi and village industries, sanitation, basic education, adult education, upliftment of women, health, regional languages, economic equality, kisans, labour, adivasis.
- Constitutional footprint: Article 40 (village panchayats), Article 43 (cottage industries), Article 47 (prohibition), Article 48 (cow protection), and Article 17 (abolition of untouchability).
- Present relevance: 73rd Amendment and gram sabhas; Swachh Bharat (2014, launched on Gandhi Jayanti); KVIC and khadi; SHGs and local livelihoods; Mission LiFE (sustainable consumption); conflict resolution through dialogue; ethical leadership.
- Critiques: Ambedkar saw the idealised village as 'a sink of localism and a den of ignorance'; economists question small-scale production for a large, young workforce; trusteeship ignores power imbalances.
- Balanced conclusion: the Gandhian model is best read as a moral compass — decentralisation, dignity of labour, simplicity and sustainability — rather than a full economic blueprint.

### Values in family, society and education — evidence and reforms (PPSC 2018 asked)

- Policy documents: Radhakrishnan Commission (1948-49) and Kothari Commission (1964-66) on moral education; NCERT's 'Education for Values in Schools — A Framework' (2012); NEP 2020 and the National Curriculum Framework for School Education (2023) stress constitutional values, ethics and socio-emotional learning.
- Experiments: Delhi's Happiness Curriculum (2018) with daily mindfulness and value stories; NSS and NCC for service and discipline; school community-service hours.
- Why institutions fail: exam-centric rote learning, coaching culture, teachers as content-deliverers rather than role models, adults' own unethical conduct (copying, paper leaks, fake degrees), consumerist media.
- Family changes: nuclear families with working parents, less time with grandparents, screen-time replacing storytelling, and in Punjab the absence of parents or siblings who have emigrated.
- What works: values 'caught, not taught' — consistent role models, discussion of real dilemmas, service-learning, reward for honesty (not only marks), and parents and teachers acting together.
- Punjab illustration: langar and kar seva at gurdwaras as everyday training in equality and service; Pingalwara volunteers; Kali Bein kar seva by Sant Seechewal.
- One-line conclusion: education gives knowledge; values give direction — institutions must teach both.

### Comparison grid and traps

- On caste: Buddha and Mahavira rejected birth-based status within their orders; Kabir and Guru Nanak attacked caste through devotion and community kitchens; Phule organised the lower castes against Brahmin dominance; Gandhi fought untouchability while long accepting varna as a duty-based ideal — note the difference from Ambedkar.
- On women: Vidyasagar used scripture to argue for widow remarriage; Phule and Savitribai acted directly through schools and shelters; Guru Nanak rejected impurity ideas; Vivekananda stressed women's education.
- On method: Vidyasagar reformed within tradition (scriptural argument and law); Phule was radical (anti-Brahmin, new Samaj); Gandhi combined moral persuasion with mass non-violent action.
- Trap: the first girls' school by the Phules (1848) was in Pune, not Mumbai; the Widow Remarriage Act was 1856, while Sati was banned earlier in 1829 (Rammohun Roy era).
- Trap: Ramakrishna Mission (1897) was founded by Vivekananda, not by Ramakrishna, who died in 1886.
- Trap: Mahavira was the 24th tirthankara, not the founder of Jainism; Rishabhanatha is the first.
- Trap: Kabir's verses are in the Guru Granth Sahib, but Kabir was not a Sikh Guru.
