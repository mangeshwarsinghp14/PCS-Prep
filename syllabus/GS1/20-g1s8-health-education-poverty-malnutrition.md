## Short notes

- Health outcomes: SRS 2021 - IMR 27 per 1,000 live births, MMR 93 per lakh live births (2019-21), U5MR 31, NMR 19; NFHS-5 TFR 2.0.
- Health financing: government health spending is below 2% of GDP against the National Health Policy 2017 target of 2.5% by 2025; out-of-pocket expenditure fell to about 39.4% of total health expenditure (National Health Accounts 2021-22).
- Ayushman Bharat (2018): Ayushman Arogya Mandirs (earlier Health and Wellness Centres) for comprehensive primary care and PM-JAY (₹5 lakh per family per year); Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission; PM-ABHIM (2021) for health infrastructure.
- Health challenges: shortage of doctors and specialists in rural areas, rising non-communicable diseases, mental health (Mental Healthcare Act 2017; Tele-MANAS 2022), TB elimination goals, antimicrobial resistance, urban health gaps.
- Education framework: RTE Act 2009 (Art 21A via 86th Amendment, 2002); NEP 2020 - 5+3+3+4 structure, foundational literacy and numeracy (NIPUN Bharat 2021), mother tongue as medium preferably till Grade 5, 6% of GDP public spending goal, higher-education GER target of 50% by 2035.
- Education gaps: learning deficits shown by ASER and national surveys, teacher vacancies, dropouts at secondary level, digital divide; higher-education GER 28.4% (AISHE 2021-22).
- School schemes: Samagra Shiksha, PM POSHAN (mid-day meals), PM SHRI schools, Eklavya Model Residential Schools.
- Human resources: demographic dividend needs skills - Skill India, PMKVY, apprenticeships; low share of formally trained workers; female labour participation remains low.
- Poverty: multidimensional poverty fell from 29.17% (2013-14) to 11.28% (2022-23) (NITI Aayog, 2024); PMGKAY provides free foodgrains to about 81 crore people for five years from Jan 2024.
- Malnutrition (NFHS-5, 2019-21): stunting 35.5%, wasting 19.3%, underweight 32.1% among children under five; anaemia in 67.1% of children (6-59 months) and 57% of women (15-49).
- Triple burden: undernutrition, micronutrient deficiency (hidden hunger) and rising overweight/obesity.
- Nutrition interventions: POSHAN Abhiyaan (2018) and Saksham Anganwadi and Poshan 2.0, Poshan Tracker, Anaemia Mukt Bharat, rice fortification, ICDS, Eat Right India, PMMVY for pregnant and lactating women.
- Causes of persistent malnutrition: poverty, poor maternal health and early marriage, inadequate sanitation, low dietary diversity, poor infant feeding practices, weak last-mile delivery.
- Management of social sector: convergence across health, nutrition, water and sanitation; decentralised planning; outcome-based monitoring; partnership with civil society; SDG 2, 3 and 4 targets.

## Punjab angle

- Aam Aadmi Clinics (from 15 Aug 2022) provide free consultations, medicines and tests at neighbourhood level as Punjab's primary-care model.
- Punjab topped the Performance Grading Index (2019-20) and performed best in the National Achievement Survey 2021; Schools of Eminence aim at quality public schooling.
- Drug addiction is a major public health issue: Outpatient Opioid Assisted Treatment (OOAT) clinics (since 2017) and the 2025 'Yudh Nashian Virudh' campaign.
- Cancer burden in Malwa led to Homi Bhabha Cancer Hospitals at Sangrur and Mullanpur (Tata Memorial Centre); state cancer relief fund supports treatment.
- NFHS-5 shows Punjab's child stunting below the national average but very high anaemia among women and children - a key nutrition priority.

## Deep dive

### Women and child health after NFHS-5 — what worked and what did not (PPSC 2021 asked)

- Improved (NFHS-5, 2019-21, India): institutional births 88.6%, full immunisation of children 12-23 months 76.4%, women with a bank account they use 78.6%, child marriage (women 20-24 married before 18) down to 23.3%, TFR down to 2.0.
- Worsened or stuck: anaemia rose to 67.1% in children and 57% in women; stunting fell only slightly (38.4% to 35.5%); overweight or obese women rose to 24%; about 29% of ever-married women reported spousal violence.
- Interventions behind the gains: Janani Suraksha Yojana (2005, cash for institutional birth), Janani Shishu Suraksha Karyakram (2011, free delivery care), Mission Indradhanush (2014, immunisation), PM Surakshit Matritva Abhiyan (2016, ninth-day antenatal check-ups), PMMVY (2017), LaQshya (2017, labour-room quality).
- Why nutrition lags: anaemia programmes depend on daily iron-folic acid compliance, poor dietary diversity, worm infestation, early marriage and short birth spacing, and weak anganwadi infrastructure; Anaemia Mukt Bharat (2018) and rice fortification (phased 2021-24) are the responses.
- Mortality progress: SRS 2023 put India's infant mortality rate at a record low of 25 (40 in 2013) and the crude birth rate at 18.4; SDG 3 targets for 2030 are MMR below 70, neonatal mortality 12 or less and under-5 mortality 25 or less.
- Punjab picture: child anaemia about 71% (NFHS-5), above the national 67.1%; stunting below the national average; very high overweight among adults; sex ratio at birth still skewed.
- Answer frame: list the indicator, the scheme that targeted it, the result in NFHS-5, and the reason for success or failure — examiners want evaluation, not a list.

### Education — learning levels, inclusion and Punjab's performance

- PARAKH Rashtriya Sarvekshan 2024 (the renamed National Achievement Survey, held Dec 2024 for about 23 lakh students in over 87,000 schools, results July 2025): Punjab ranked first in Grade 3 (score 80) and Grade 9 (57), and tied for the top score in Grade 6 (67, with Kerala).
- National pattern in such surveys: learning falls sharply between Grades 3 and 9, mathematics is weakest, and state-government schools lag central and private schools; SC and ST students lag in Grades 6 and 9.
- NEP 2020 inclusion tools (PPSC 2021 asked): Socio-Economically Disadvantaged Groups (SEDGs) as a planning category, Gender Inclusion Fund, Special Education Zones for disadvantaged regions, bridge courses for dropouts, inclusive education for children with disabilities with resource teachers.
- RTE Act 2009 Section 12(1)(c): 25% of entry-level seats in private unaided schools for disadvantaged children — implementation varies widely across states.
- Higher education: AISHE 2021-22 put the GER at 28.4% against NEP's 50% target for 2035; GER for women has caught up with men, but SC and ST GERs remain lower.
- Punjab initiatives: Schools of Eminence, teacher training abroad, 'Mission Samarth' for foundational literacy and numeracy, and an entrepreneurship programme ('Business Blasters') for senior-secondary students.
- Challenges: teacher vacancies, low enrolment in government higher-secondary science, IELTS-oriented aspirations that pull students towards migration, and closure of small rural schools through merger.

### 'Poverty breeds poverty' — the poverty-trap argument (PPSC 2018 asked)

- Ragnar Nurkse's vicious circle: low income leads to low saving and investment, which keeps productivity and income low — 'a country is poor because it is poor'.
- Nutrition trap: undernourished mothers have low-birth-weight babies who grow into stunted, less productive adults (the intergenerational cycle of undernutrition).
- Health-shock trap: high out-of-pocket health spending pushes households into debt and asset sales; one serious illness can undo years of progress.
- Education trap: poor children attend weaker schools, drop out to work, and enter low-wage informal jobs; their own children repeat the pattern.
- Debt and asset traps: distress borrowing at high interest (moneylenders, commission agents) and sale of land or cattle reduce future income — visible in Punjab's farm-suicide data.
- Social traps: caste discrimination, lack of networks, and spatial poverty in backward districts limit mobility (Amartya Sen's 'capability deprivation').
- Breaking the cycle: first-1,000-days nutrition, universal health cover, quality public schooling, conditional and unconditional cash transfers, asset creation (MGNREGA, housing), credit through SHGs, and evidence from MPI decline (24.85% in 2015-16 to 14.96% in 2019-21) showing that multi-sector action works.
- Counter-view: many households escape poverty through migration, education and remittances (Punjab's own history) — poverty is persistent but not destiny.

### Human resources and employment (PLFS and Punjab)

- PLFS 2023-24 (July-June, usual status, 15+): labour force participation 60.1%, worker-population ratio 58.2%, unemployment 3.2%; female LFPR 41.7% (much of the rise is self-employment and unpaid family work in rural areas).
- Punjab: PLFS 2023-24 placed Punjab's unemployment rate at about 5.5%, well above the national 3.2%; educated youth unemployment is the main concern.
- Mismatch: graduates seek government jobs or emigration, while industry reports shortages of skilled technicians; most jobs remain informal without social security.
- Skilling: Skill India and PMKVY 4.0, ITIs, apprenticeships (NAPS), the Punjab Skill Development Mission, and overseas-placement facilitation; quality and placement rates remain uneven.
- Brain drain: the PAU study's finding that 13% of rural households have a member abroad shows Punjab exporting its trained youth — a human-capital loss partly offset by remittances.
- Health workforce: shortage of specialists in community health centres and rural postings; Punjab's medical colleges and nursing schools also feed overseas demand.
- Policy direction: labour-intensive manufacturing (agro-processing, textiles, light engineering), care-economy jobs (nursing, elder care), women-friendly workplaces (safe transport, creches), and portable social security.

### Punjab's health agenda beyond Aam Aadmi Clinics

- Drug addiction as public health: about 2.3 lakh opioid-dependent people (AIIMS survey, 2015); OOAT clinics, de-addiction centres and harm-reduction services; hepatitis C and HIV among injecting users.
- Hepatitis C: Punjab started free hepatitis C treatment for its residents in 2016 through a dedicated relief fund — one of the first states to do so.
- Cancer: higher incidence reported in the cotton belt of Malwa (Bathinda, Mansa, Faridkot) has been linked in public debate to pesticide use and contaminated water; the night train from Bathinda carrying patients to Bikaner's cancer hospital became known as the 'cancer train'; Tata Memorial's units at Sangrur and Mullanpur now serve the region.
- Non-communicable diseases: high obesity, hypertension and diabetes reflect rich diets and sedentary lifestyles; screening under the national NCD programme and Ayushman Arogya Mandirs.
- Water quality and health: uranium and arsenic traces in groundwater of parts of Malwa and Majha, industrial effluents in Buddha Nullah — reverse-osmosis plants are a stop-gap.
- Road safety: Punjab has a high road-accident burden; Good Samaritan protection and cashless trauma care for accident victims are key reforms.
- Financing: Punjab's fiscal stress (high debt, committed spending) limits health and education budgets — any answer on social-sector management should mention fiscal space.

### Common traps

- NFHS-5 anaemia rose compared with NFHS-4 — do not write that it fell.
- IMR is per 1,000 live births; MMR is per 1,00,000 live births; MMR comes from SRS three-year pooled data (for example 2019-21).
- PARAKH Rashtriya Sarvekshan replaced the National Achievement Survey (NAS) name; NAS 2021 and PRS 2024 are different rounds.
- PLFS 'usual status' and 'current weekly status' unemployment rates differ; always state which one you quote.
- GER can exceed 100% at school level because of over-age students; NER cannot.
- Stunting shows chronic undernutrition (height-for-age), wasting shows acute undernutrition (weight-for-height), underweight is weight-for-age — a composite of both.

**3.** 'High out-of-pocket health expenditure pushes millions into poverty.' Evaluate the role of Ayushman Bharat and state initiatives such as Punjab's Aam Aadmi Clinics in addressing this. (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: OOPE trends (NHA 2021-22)
- Ayushman Bharat: Arogya Mandirs, PM-JAY coverage
- Limitations: outpatient costs and medicines not covered, private hospital concentration
- Aam Aadmi Clinics: free primary care, medicines, diagnostics

- Conclusion: universal health coverage as anti-poverty policy
