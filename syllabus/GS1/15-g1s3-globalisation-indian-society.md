## Short notes

- Globalisation: growing integration of economies and societies through flows of goods, services, capital, technology, people and ideas; in India accelerated by the 1991 LPG reforms.
- Theoretical lenses: David Harvey's time-space compression; George Ritzer's 'McDonaldisation' (homogenisation); Roland Robertson's 'glocalisation' (local adaptation of global forms); cultural hybridisation.
- Economic effects: faster growth, IT/ITES and services boom, expansion of the middle class, consumerism, foreign investment and brands; but rising inequality, jobless growth concerns and informalisation.
- Employment: new jobs in services, BPOs, start-ups and the gig economy - NITI Aayog (2022) estimated 77 lakh gig workers in 2020-21, projected to reach 2.35 crore by 2029-30; Code on Social Security 2020 recognises gig and platform workers.
- Agriculture: integration with world markets and the WTO Agreement on Agriculture (1995), price volatility, seed and input corporatisation, debates on subsidies and MSP.
- Culture: spread of Western dress, food, music and English; at the same time global spread of Indian culture - yoga (International Day of Yoga since 2015), cinema, cuisine, diaspora festivals.
- Family: nuclearisation, dual-career couples, delayed marriage, live-in relationships, online matchmaking, weaker joint-family support for the elderly.
- Women: new opportunities in IT, services and garment exports, more mobility and financial independence; also double burden, workplace safety issues and commodification in media and advertising.
- Caste and class: new occupations reduce caste-based work, but new hierarchies of skill and access; digital divide between urban and rural, rich and poor.
- Youth and migration: aspirations shaped by global media; international migration and remittances - India the largest recipient of remittances (about $129 billion in 2024, World Bank).
- Education and health: private and international education, foreign university campuses allowed (UGC regulations 2023), brain drain and brain gain; medical tourism and corporate hospitals.
- Media and technology: smartphones and social media spread global ideas but also misinformation, cultural anxiety and online radicalisation.
- Language: English as language of mobility; pressure on regional languages and dialects; counter-movements to promote mother tongues.
- Local responses: revival of traditional identities, religious and cultural assertion, swadeshi and self-reliance (Atmanirbhar Bharat) narratives.
- Recent counter-trends: protectionism, tariff wars (including US tariff increases on Indian goods in 2025), supply-chain reshoring, tighter immigration rules in Western countries.

## Punjab angle

- Punjabi diaspora in Canada, UK, USA and Australia shapes village economies through remittances, NRI investment and cultural exchange.
- Emigration pressure drives IELTS coaching and study-visa culture; illegal 'dunki' routes and US deportation flights landing at Amritsar (Feb 2025) show its dark side.
- Ludhiana's hosiery and bicycle industries faced Chinese competition; Jalandhar's sports goods sector competes in global markets.
- Punjabi music and cinema have gone global - Diljit Dosanjh became the first Punjabi artist to perform at Coachella (2023).
- Globalised narcotics routes from the Golden Crescent feed Punjab's drug problem.

## Deep dive

### Overseas migration from rural Punjab — the PAU study (published Jan 2024)

- Study: 'Overseas Migration from Rural Punjab: Trends, Causes and Consequences' by Shalini Sharma, Manjeet Kaur and Amit Guleria (Department of Economics and Sociology, PAU, Ludhiana); survey of about 9,500 households in 44 villages across 22 districts, fieldwork 2021-2023, covering migration from 1990 to 2022.
- Scale: 13.34% of rural households had at least one member abroad; about 74% of the migration captured happened after 2016 — the study-visa boom years.
- Regions: Majha had the highest share of households with migrants (about 20.5%), then Malwa (14.3%) and Doaba (11.3%) — Doaba's older migration is now matched by newer outflows from Majha and Malwa.
- Destinations: Canada about 42% of migrants, Dubai 16%, Australia 10%, Italy 5.5%, UK 3.5%, USA 3.3%.
- Cost: households borrowed an estimated Rs 14,342 crore statewide (about 56% of migrant households took loans; average about Rs 3.1 lakh) and sold assets worth about Rs 5,636 crore — mainly houses and gold; even landless and Dalit households sold assets to send a member abroad.
- Gender: women obtained about 65% of study visas because they more often met IELTS band requirements — a new pathway for women's mobility, often tied to marriage strategies.
- Reasons given by households: low income and lack of jobs and employability plus corruption (about 72%), poor governance (62%), drugs (52%), village factionalism (51%) and debt (about 25%) — migration is driven by social despair as much as by wages.
- Use: this single study gives data for questions on globalisation, youth, drugs, women, demography and rural economy — quote it with year and institution.

### International migration after Covid — what changed (PPSC 2021 asked)

- 2020 shock: lockdowns stranded migrants; the Vande Bharat Mission (from May 2020) flew home lakhs of Indians, especially Gulf workers who lost jobs; remittances proved resilient as migrants sent savings home.
- Recovery and record remittances: India has remained the world's largest remittance recipient since 2008; inflows reached record levels in 2023-24 as Gulf, US and UK demand revived.
- Shift from Gulf labour to Western skilled and student migration: RBI's sixth remittances survey found that in 2023-24 advanced economies overtook the Gulf as the larger source of remittances (US 27.7%, UAE 19.2%, UK 10.8%).
- Policy tightening in the West: Canada capped study permits from Jan 2024 and later cut further; the UK barred most taught-master's students from bringing dependants from Jan 2024; Australia raised student visa fees and English requirements.
- Diplomatic and political shocks: the India-Canada row from Sept 2023 (Nijjar killing allegation) and expulsions of diplomats in Oct 2024 directly affected Punjabi families; US deportation flights of irregular migrants landed at Amritsar in Feb 2025.
- Irregular routes: 'donkey' (dunki) journeys via Latin America, Central America and Mexico, and via Europe; deaths and fraud cases led Punjab to tighten registration of travel and immigration consultants.
- Gulf reforms: Qatar (2020) and Saudi Arabia (2021) partially loosened the kafala sponsorship system — relevant for Punjabi and other Indian workers.
- Return migration and reintegration: India still lacks a modern emigration law (the Emigration Bill 2021 replacing the 1983 Act was not passed), a gap often cited in answers.

### Cultural homogenisation and heterogenisation (PPSC 2026 asked) — concepts and Indian examples

- Homogenisation (cultural imperialism, Herbert Schiller; 'McDonaldisation', George Ritzer): global brands, English, Western fashion, fast food, Hollywood and streaming templates make cultures look alike.
- Heterogenisation: globalisation also multiplies difference — identity revival, regional language content on OTT, niche diaspora markets, and reactive nationalisms.
- Hybridisation (Jan Nederveen Pieterse) and glocalisation (Roland Robertson): mixing creates new forms — bhangra-hip hop, 'Indo-Western' wear, paneer pizza, Punjabi-language Canadian rap.
- Arjun Appadurai's five 'scapes' explain disjunctures: ethnoscapes (people), mediascapes (images), technoscapes (technology), financescapes (capital), ideoscapes (ideas) — useful to structure a 250-word answer.
- Punjab examples of homogenisation: English-medium schooling and IELTS culture, malls and imported cars as status symbols, decline of folk forms such as kavishri and jaago in cities.
- Punjab examples of heterogenisation: global phulkari and jutti markets, Vaisakhi and Nagar Kirtan parades in Surrey and Brampton, Gurmukhi apps and fonts, Punjabi music charting worldwide, and diaspora funding of village schools and stadiums.
- Reverse flows: yoga, Indian cuisine, Bollywood and Punjabi music, diaspora politicians in Canada and the UK shape global culture.
- Conclusion line: India shows 'selective appropriation' — it absorbs global forms on its own terms; neither pure homogenisation nor pure resistance.

### Globalisation and Punjab's economy — farm, factory and services

- Agriculture and WTO: India's public stockholding for food security (procurement at MSP) is protected only by the 2013 Bali 'peace clause'; developed countries challenge Indian rice and wheat support — Punjab is at the heart of this debate.
- Basmati: Punjab is a major basmati grower for export; EU and US pesticide-residue limits (for example on tricyclazole) have pushed the state to restrict some agrochemicals on basmati.
- Industry: Ludhiana's bicycle, hosiery and auto-component clusters face Chinese competition but also export globally; Jalandhar exports sports goods; cheap imports hurt small units after tariff cuts.
- Services: IT and ITES in Mohali, global education consultancies, NRI-funded real estate; remittance-led consumption in Doaba villages.
- Diaspora capital: NRI investment in land, houses and gurdwaras; Punjab's NRI Sabha and NRI cells handle property disputes — a globalisation-specific governance issue.
- Illicit globalisation: drug routes from the Golden Crescent (Afghanistan-Pakistan-Iran), cross-border drones, arms and gangster networks with handlers abroad.
- Policy responses: export promotion for agro-processing (food parks), Mohali IT City, skilling for overseas jobs through state employment bureaus, regulation of travel agents under the Punjab Prevention of Human Smuggling Act (2012).

### Globalisation versus regionalism (PPSC 2018 asked) and key thinkers

- Economic regionalism: regional trade blocs (EU, ASEAN, USMCA, RCEP) are 'building blocks or stumbling blocks' of globalisation; India left RCEP negotiations in Nov 2019 over fears of Chinese imports and dairy competition, a decision Punjab's dairy sector welcomed.
- South Asian regionalism is weak: SAARC summits stalled after 2016, SAFTA trade is small, and the India-Pakistan land route through Punjab (Attari) is closed — Punjab loses its natural regional market.
- Cultural regionalism as reaction: globalisation produces anxieties that revive regional identities — language pride, sons-of-the-soil job quotas, regional political parties.
- Anthony Giddens: globalisation as 'action at a distance' and a 'runaway world' of manufactured risks.
- Zygmunt Bauman: globalisation divides people into mobile 'tourists' and stuck 'vagabonds' — fits Punjab's split between visa-holders and those left behind.
- Immanuel Wallerstein: world-systems theory of core, semi-periphery and periphery — explains why Punjab exports raw grain and imports finished goods and technology.
- Manuel Castells: the 'network society' where information flows decide power — relevant to IT hubs and digital divides.

### Common traps

- Glocalisation is Robertson's term; McDonaldisation is Ritzer's; scapes are Appadurai's — do not mix the attributions.
- Remittances are counted in the current account (private transfers), not as FDI.
- Study-visa migration is legal migration; 'dunki' refers to irregular routes — keep the two separate in answers.
- India's withdrawal was from RCEP, not from the WTO or SAFTA.
- Globalisation began well before 1991 (colonial trade, Gulf migration of the 1970s); 1991 accelerated economic integration.
- The PAU study covers rural households only; urban emigration is additional.

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** Does globalisation lead to cultural homogenisation or cultural hybridisation in India? Discuss with examples. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: define globalisation and cultural flows
- Homogenisation: global brands, fast food, Western dress, English, McDonaldisation
- Hybridisation and glocalisation: localised menus, fusion music, Indian cinema, festivals on social media
- Reverse flows: yoga, Indian cuisine, diaspora culture, Punjabi music
- Resistance and revival: identity assertion, regional language content
- Conclusion: Indian culture adapts selectively - hybridity dominates

**2.** Analyse the impact of globalisation on women and the family in India. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: economic and cultural channels of change
- Women: jobs in services and exports, education, autonomy; but precarious work, double burden, safety
- Family: nuclearisation, changing marriage patterns, elderly care gap
- Technology: dating apps, online work, connectivity with migrant members
- Data and schemes: FLFPR trends, maternity and POSH laws
- Conclusion: empowerment gains need social security and care infrastructure

</details>

**3.** Examine the social consequences of globalisation-driven youth emigration from Punjab. (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: scale of study-visa and work migration
- Positives: remittances, skill exposure, diaspora networks
- Negatives: brain drain, ageing villages, debt for agents, illegal routes and deportations
- Family and land impacts: locked houses, land sales
- Policy: regulation of travel agents, local job creation, skilling
- Conclusion: make migration safe and choice-based, not distress-driven

</details>
