## Short notes

- Context: Western education, Christian missionary activity, printing press, colonial census and rationalist-humanist ideas spurred 19th-century reform; reformers sought to purge religion of 'corruptions' and uplift women and lower castes.
- Brahmo Samaj (1828, Raja Rammohan Roy): monotheism, anti-idolatry, campaign against sati (abolished 1829 by Bentinck); later led by Debendranath Tagore and Keshab Chandra Sen.
- Other all-India movements: Prarthana Samaj (1867, Bombay; M.G. Ranade), Satyashodhak Samaj (1873, Jyotiba Phule), Ramakrishna Mission (1897, Vivekananda), Theosophical Society (1875; Adyar from 1886; Annie Besant).
- Muslim reform: Aligarh movement of Sir Syed Ahmad Khan (MAO College 1875); in Punjab, Anjuman-i-Himayat-i-Islam (Lahore, 1884) promoted modern education; Ahmadiyya movement founded at Qadian (Gurdaspur) in 1889 by Mirza Ghulam Ahmad.
- Reform legislation: Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act 1856 (Vidyasagar), Age of Consent Act 1891, Child Marriage Restraint (Sarda) Act 1929.
- Arya Samaj: founded by Swami Dayanand Saraswati in Bombay (1875); Lahore branch (1877) made Punjab its strongest base; 'Back to the Vedas', Satyarth Prakash, opposition to idolatry and caste by birth, shuddhi.
- DAV movement: DAV School/College Lahore (1886, Lala Hansraj); 1893 split into the 'College party' (English education) and 'Gurukul/Mahatma party' (Swami Shraddhanand; Gurukul Kangri 1902).
- Nirankari movement: Baba Dayal (Rawalpindi, mid-19th c.) - worship of the formless (Nirankar), rejection of idol worship and Brahmanical rituals within Sikhism, simple Anand marriage ceremony.
- Namdhari (Kuka) movement: Baba Balak Singh and Baba Ram Singh (Bhaini Sahib, Ludhiana; Sant Khalsa initiated 1857) - simple living, against caste, dowry, female infanticide, liquor and meat; cow protection.
- Kukas boycotted British goods, courts, schools and postal services - a precursor of swadeshi and non-cooperation; Kuka attack on Malerkotla (1872) led to execution of Kukas by blowing from cannon; Baba Ram Singh exiled to Rangoon.
- Singh Sabha movement: first Singh Sabha at Amritsar (1873), more radical Lahore Singh Sabha (1879; Prof. Gurmukh Singh, Giani Ditt Singh); aimed to restore Sikh practices, promote Gurmukhi/Punjabi and education.
- Singh Sabha outcomes: Khalsa College Amritsar (1892), Chief Khalsa Diwan (1902), Khalsa schools, Punjabi press; Bhai Kahn Singh Nabha's 'Ham Hindu Nahin' (1898) asserted distinct Sikh identity; Anand Marriage Act 1909.
- Bhai Vir Singh: literary leader of the Singh Sabha renaissance (Khalsa Tract Society, 'Khalsa Samachar', novels and poetry in Punjabi).
- Gurdwara Reform (Akali) movement 1920-25: freed gurdwaras from hereditary mahants; Nankana Sahib massacre (Feb 1921), keys affair (1922), Guru-ka-Bagh (1922), Jaito morcha (1923-25); Sikh Gurdwaras Act 1925 created a statutory SGPC.
- Ad-Dharm movement (1926) of Mangu Ram Mugowalia: Dalit assertion in Punjab claiming an original pre-Aryan religion; recorded separately in the 1931 Census.
- Limitations: largely urban, middle-class and upper-caste leadership; revivalist strands and competitive census politics (Arya Samaj vs Singh Sabha vs Ahmadiyya/Anjumans) sharpened communal identities.
- Legacy: spread of education (DAV, Khalsa, Islamia institutions), women's education, Punjabi print culture, social reform laws and a base for political mobilisation.

## Punjab angle

- Bhaini Sahib (Ludhiana) remains the Namdhari headquarters; the Kuka martyrs of Malerkotla (1872) are commemorated by a memorial at Malerkotla.
- SGPC, created under the Sikh Gurdwaras Act 1925, manages historic gurdwaras; the Supreme Court in 2022 upheld Haryana's separate gurdwara management law (2014).
- Ad-Dharm's legacy continues in Doaba (Ravidassia identity, Dera Sachkhand Ballan) - relevant to Punjab's high SC population (31.9%, Census 2011).
- Educational legacy: Khalsa College Amritsar, DAV institutions across Punjab, Kanya Mahavidyalaya Jalandhar (Arya Samaj women's education).
- Qadian (Gurdaspur) continues as the Indian centre of the Ahmadiyya community.

## How PPSC asks it

PPSC repeatedly asks about Kuka, Singh Sabha, Arya Samaj and Akali movements - their aims, methods and political significance. Answers should compare movements, cite founders and dates, and evaluate both reformist and communal outcomes.

## Deep dive

### Timeline of reform in Punjab (year — event)

- 1834 — American Presbyterian Ludhiana Mission; 1836 — first printing press in the region at Ludhiana.
- 1849 — Annexation; missionary schools, English education and Punjab-wide administration follow.
- 1857 (Baisakhi) — Baba Ram Singh initiates the Sant Khalsa at Bhaini Sahib: start of the Namdhari (Kuka) movement.
- 1863 — Brahmo Samaj branch at Lahore (Navin Chandra Rai).
- 1872 (Jan) — Kuka attack on Malerkotla; 65–66 Kukas blown from cannon at Malerkotla on orders of L. Cowan and T.D. Forsyth; Ram Singh exiled to Rangoon.
- 1873 — Sri Guru Singh Sabha, Amritsar (1 Oct), set up after four Sikh students of the Mission school announced conversion to Christianity.
- 1877 — Swami Dayanand's Punjab tour; Arya Samaj, Lahore founded; 1879 — Lahore Singh Sabha (Prof. Gurmukh Singh, Giani Ditt Singh).
- 1882 — Punjab University, Lahore; Hunter Education Commission hears rival claims for Hindi, Urdu and Punjabi in Gurmukhi.
- 1884 — Anjuman-i-Himayat-i-Islam, Lahore; 1886 — DAV School, Lahore (1 June); 1887 — Dev Samaj (Shiv Narayan Agnihotri).
- 1889 — Mirza Ghulam Ahmad takes the first bai'at at Ludhiana (23 Mar): Ahmadiyya movement, headquartered at Qadian.
- 1892 — Khalsa College, Amritsar; Sikh Kanya Mahavidyalaya, Ferozepur (Bhai Takht Singh); 1893 — Arya Samaj splits into College and Gurukul parties.
- 1898 — Kahn Singh Nabha's Ham Hindu Nahin; 1902 — Chief Khalsa Diwan (30 Oct); 1909 — Anand Marriage Act.
- 1920 — Golden Temple's pujaris accept karah prasad from Dalit Sikhs (12 Oct) and flee; Akal Takht taken over; SGPC (Nov) and Akali Dal (Dec) formed.
- 1925 — Sikh Gurdwaras Act; 1925–26 — Ad-Dharm movement (Mangu Ram); 1931 — Census records Ad-Dharmis separately.

### Comparative table — movement, leader, base, aims, methods, outcome

- Namdhari: Baba Ram Singh; Bhaini Sahib (Ludhiana); purity of Sikh practice, simple Anand marriage, no dowry, against infanticide, cow protection; boycott of British goods, courts, schools and post; outcome — repression after 1872, but remembered as a forerunner of non-cooperation.
- Nirankari: Baba Dayal; Rawalpindi; worship of the formless, rejection of idol worship and Brahmanical rites inside Sikhism; preaching and Anand marriage; outcome — small, urban, but first of the Sikh reform impulses.
- Singh Sabha: Amritsar (1873, moderate, Sanatan-leaning) and Lahore (1879, Tat Khalsa); Sikh identity, Gurmukhi/Punjabi education, scriptural purity; tracts, newspapers, schools, Khalsa College; outcome — Chief Khalsa Diwan, Anand Marriage Act, modern Sikh institutions.
- Arya Samaj: Dayanand (Lahore 1877); Vedic authority, anti-idolatry, women's education, widow remarriage, shuddhi; schools, DAV, Gurukul; outcome — strong urban Hindu middle-class base, Lajpat Rai's nationalism, but sharpened Hindu–Sikh–Muslim rivalry.
- Ahmadiyya: Mirza Ghulam Ahmad (Qadian 1889); claims of a renewer; tracts, debates; outcome — rejected by orthodox Muslims, later declared non-Muslim in Pakistan (1974).
- Anjuman-i-Himayat-i-Islam (Lahore 1884): modern education for Muslims — Islamia College Lahore; counter to missionary and Arya activity.
- Dev Samaj (Agnihotri, 1887): moral-ethical code, women's education, temperance; schools at Ferozepur and Moga.
- Ad-Dharm (Mangu Ram, 1925–26): Dalit claim to an original pre-Aryan faith; outcome — separate census category (1931), political mobilisation, later Ravidassia identity.
- Gurdwara Reform (Akali) movement (1920–25): SGPC/Akali Dal; free gurdwaras from mahants; non-violent jathas; outcome — Sikh Gurdwaras Act 1925 and a mass political party.

### Women, caste and education — the social content of reform

- Female education: Kanya Mahavidyalaya, Jalandhar (Arya Samaj, Lala Devraj); Sikh Kanya Mahavidyalaya, Ferozepur (1892); Dev Samaj girls' schools; missionary women's medical school at Ludhiana (1894).
- Namdharis opposed female infanticide, dowry and the sale of daughters, and conducted simple inter-caste Anand marriages.
- Arya Samaj promoted widow remarriage and opposed child marriage; it ran shuddhi drives among Meghs, Odhs and Rahtias (c. 1900).
- Singh Sabhas initiated Rahtia and Mazhabi Sikhs and campaigned for Anand Karaj (legalised by the Anand Marriage Act, 1909).
- The Akali takeover of the Akal Takht (Oct 1920) began with the acceptance of offerings from Dalit Sikhs — caste equality at the core of gurdwara reform.
- Ad-Dharm directly challenged caste Hinduism and Sikh caste practice; Babu Mangu Ram had been a Ghadar activist in the USA.
- Limits: leadership was urban, upper-caste and middle-class; women's issues were framed as 'uplift'; competing census claims fuelled communal boundaries.

### Colonial context that shaped the movements

- Missionary competition — Ludhiana Mission, Church Missionary Society at Amritsar (1852) — and conversions of notable figures (Duleep Singh, 1853) alarmed all communities.
- Census operations (1868, 1881, 1891...) counted religions separately, turning numbers into political capital.
- Print revolution: lithographic presses at Lahore and Amritsar produced tracts in Gurmukhi, Devanagari and Urdu; rival newspapers (Khalsa Akhbar, Arya Patrika, Paisa Akhbar).
- Language controversy: Arya Samaj backed Hindi in Devanagari; Singh Sabhas backed Punjabi in Gurmukhi; Muslims backed Urdu — later echoed in the Punjabi Suba debate.
- Punjab Land Alienation Act (1900) created 'agricultural tribes', mixing caste, religion and economic interest in politics.
- Army recruitment and 'martial races' policy encouraged the British to favour orthodox Sikh identity (e.g., Khalsa rites in Sikh regiments).

### Historiography and debates

- Kenneth W. Jones read Punjab's movements as responses to colonial modernity and classed them as 'transitional' (pre-colonial roots, e.g., Namdhari) or 'acculturative' (colonial-milieu, e.g., Arya Samaj, Singh Sabha).
- Harjot Oberoi argued that the Tat Khalsa reformers of the Singh Sabha replaced a fluid 'Sanatan' Sikh world with sharply bounded identity — a thesis contested by many Sikh scholars.
- Namdhari debate: British records called the Kukas fanatics; nationalist historians (e.g., Fauja Singh) present them as the first anti-colonial mass movement in Punjab.
- Akali movement: Gandhi hailed the Keys victory (Jan 1922) as the first decisive battle for India's freedom won; later writers stress its communitarian and anti-mahant character.
- Arya Samaj: seen both as a source of nationalism (Lajpat Rai, Bhagat Singh's family) and of communal polarisation (shuddhi vs tabligh).
- Balanced conclusion: reform movements modernised education, print and women's status but also institutionalised religious boundaries that shaped 20th-century Punjab politics.

### Answer-writing pointers for PPSC Mains

- For 'Compare Kuka and Singh Sabha movements': aims (purification vs identity and education), methods (boycott vs tracts and schools), attitude to British (hostile vs loyalist), outcome.
- For 'Role of the Arya Samaj in Punjab': education (DAV, Gurukul), social reform, nationalism (Lajpat Rai), communal side-effects — four headings.
- For 'Gurdwara Reform movement as a national movement': non-violence, mass participation, Congress support, Jaito and Nabha issue, the 1925 Act.
- Use one memorable fact per paragraph: 1872 Malerkotla, 1873 Amritsar Singh Sabha, 1886 DAV, 1892 Khalsa College, 1909 Anand Marriage Act, 1925 Gurdwara Act.
- End with legacy: institutions that still exist (SGPC, Chief Khalsa Diwan, DAV network, Bhaini Sahib, Qadian, Dera Ballan).
- Avoid the trap of calling all reformers anti-British: the Chief Khalsa Diwan and Anjumans were loyalist; the Kukas and later Akalis confronted the state.

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** Compare the aims and achievements of the Singh Sabha movement and the Arya Samaj in Punjab. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: late 19th-century Punjab - colonial education, missionaries, census
- Arya Samaj: Vedic revival, anti-idolatry, shuddhi, DAV and Gurukul institutions, women's education
- Singh Sabha: restoring Sikh doctrine, Gurmukhi/Punjabi promotion, Khalsa College, Chief Khalsa Diwan, Anand Marriage Act 1909
- Similarities: education, social reform, middle-class leadership, print culture
- Differences and friction: Arya Samaj critique of Sikh Gurus, 'Ham Hindu Nahin', shuddhi of Dalit Sikhs
- Achievements and limits: modern education vs sharpened religious boundaries
- Conclusion: both shaped modern Punjabi identity and later politics

</details>

**2.** 'The Kuka movement was as much a social reform movement as an anti-British movement.' Discuss. (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: Baba Balak Singh, Baba Ram Singh, Bhaini Sahib
- Social agenda: simple marriages, widow remarriage, against dowry, female infanticide, caste, liquor
- Political agenda: boycott of British goods, courts, schools, post - parallel administration via subas
- Malerkotla episode (1872) and brutal repression, exile of Ram Singh
- Assessment: forerunner of swadeshi and non-cooperation
- Conclusion: fusion of religious reform and anti-colonial resistance

</details>

**3.** Examine the causes and significance of the Gurdwara Reform (Akali) movement. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: control of gurdwaras by mahants with government backing
- Causes: corruption of mahants, Singh Sabha awakening, post-war discontent, Jallianwala Bagh
- Course: Nankana Sahib (1921), keys affair, Guru-ka-Bagh, Jaito; peaceful Akali jathas
- Link to national movement: Gandhi's praise, NCM, Akali langar at Vaikom
- Outcome: Sikh Gurdwaras Act 1925, statutory SGPC, rise of Shiromani Akali Dal
- Significance: mass non-violent mobilisation in Punjab; also strengthened religious-political identity
- Conclusion: reform and nationalism intertwined

</details>
