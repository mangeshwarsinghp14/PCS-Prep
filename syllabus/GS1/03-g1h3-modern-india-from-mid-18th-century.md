## Short notes

- 18th-century transition: Mughal decline after Aurangzeb (1707), Nadir Shah's sack of Delhi (1739), Third Battle of Panipat (1761) where Ahmad Shah Abdali defeated the Marathas; rise of regional states (Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad, Mysore, Sikh misls).
- Company's rise: Battle of Plassey 1757 (Clive vs Siraj-ud-Daulah), Battle of Buxar 1764, Treaty of Allahabad 1765 gave the Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa; dual government in Bengal.
- Expansion tools: Anglo-Mysore wars (Tipu killed 1799), Anglo-Maratha wars (third ended 1818), Wellesley's Subsidiary Alliance (1798), Dalhousie's Doctrine of Lapse (Satara 1848, Jhansi, Nagpur), annexation of Awadh (1856).
- Constitutional landmarks: Regulating Act 1773, Pitt's India Act 1784, Charter Acts 1813 (₹1 lakh for education), 1833 (Governor-General of India; trade monopoly ended), 1853; Government of India Act 1858 transferred power to the Crown.
- Land revenue: Permanent Settlement 1793 (Cornwallis, Bengal), Ryotwari (Munro, Madras/Bombay), Mahalwari (North-Western Provinces and Punjab) - all raised peasant burden.
- Revolt of 1857: began at Meerut (10 May 1857); causes - political, economic, socio-religious, military (greased cartridges); led to Crown rule and the Queen's Proclamation.
- Economic critique: Dadabhai Naoroji's drain of wealth theory ('Poverty and Un-British Rule in India'), R.C. Dutt; deindustrialisation and recurring famines.
- Moderate phase: INC founded 1885 (A.O. Hume; W.C. Bonnerjee first president); petitions, economic nationalism, Indian Councils Act 1892.
- Extremist/Swadeshi phase: Partition of Bengal 1905, Swadeshi and Boycott; Surat split 1907; Morley-Minto Reforms 1909 introduced separate electorates; Lucknow Pact 1916; Home Rule Leagues (Tilak, Besant).
- Gandhian phase: Champaran (1917), Kheda, Ahmedabad; Rowlatt Act and Jallianwala Bagh (13 April 1919); Non-Cooperation-Khilafat (1920-22, withdrawn after Chauri Chaura, Feb 1922).
- 1927-35: Simon Commission boycott (1928), Lahore Congress Purna Swaraj (1929), Dandi March (1930), Gandhi-Irwin Pact (1931), Poona Pact (1932), Government of India Act 1935 (provincial autonomy).
- Final phase: Quit India (8 Aug 1942), Cripps Mission (1942), INA trials (1945), RIN mutiny (1946), Cabinet Mission (1946), Mountbatten Plan (3 June 1947), Indian Independence Act 1947.
- Peasant and tribal movements: Santhal Hul (1855), Indigo revolt (1859-60), Deccan riots (1875), Moplah (1921), Bardoli (1928), Tebhaga (1946), Telangana (1946-51).
- Post-1947: integration of princely states, Constitution (26 Jan 1950), first general elections (1951-52), States Reorganisation Act 1956, Five-Year Plans.
- Later milestones: wars of 1962, 1965, 1971 (creation of Bangladesh); Green Revolution; Emergency (1975-77); 1991 economic reforms; 73rd/74th Amendments (1992).
- Key personalities: Raja Rammohan Roy, Dadabhai Naoroji, Tilak, Gokhale, Gandhi, Nehru, Patel, Subhas Chandra Bose, B.R. Ambedkar, Lala Lajpat Rai, Bhagat Singh.

## Punjab angle

- Punjab followed a distinct path: Sikh misls, Ranjit Singh's kingdom (1799-1839), Anglo-Sikh wars and annexation (1849).
- Punjab was a hub of anti-colonial activity: Kuka movement, Pagri Sambhal Jatta (1907), Ghadar (1913-15), Jallianwala Bagh (1919), Akali movement (1920-25).
- Gandhi hailed the Akali victory in the 'keys affair' (1922) as the first decisive battle for India's freedom won.
- Lala Lajpat Rai died after the lathi charge at Lahore during the Simon Commission protest (1928); Bhagat Singh, Sukhdev and Rajguru were hanged on 23 March 1931.
- Post-1947 issues: Partition, Punjabi Suba and reorganisation (1966), Green Revolution, militancy in the 1980s, Rajiv-Longowal Accord (1985).

## How PPSC asks it

Questions span the whole period: British expansion, economic critique, phases of nationalism and post-1947 milestones. Examiners reward precise dates, analytical linkage between events, and balanced assessment of personalities.

## Deep dive

### Chronology spine, 1740–2000 (one line per turning point)

- 1739 Nadir Shah; 1748 Dal Khalsa; 1757 Plassey; 1761 Panipat III; 1764 Buxar; 1765 Diwani and the Sikh occupation of Lahore; 1772–85 Warren Hastings; 1793 Permanent Settlement; 1799 Ranjit Singh takes Lahore and Tipu dies.
- 1803–05 Second Anglo-Maratha War; 1809 Treaty of Amritsar; 1818 end of the Maratha power; 1829 sati banned; 1833 Charter Act; 1835 Macaulay's Minute; 1839–42 First Afghan War; 1843 Sindh; 1845–49 Anglo-Sikh wars and annexation of Punjab; 1856 Awadh.
- 1857 Revolt; 1858 Crown rule; 1861 Indian Councils Act; 1872 Kuka episode; 1876–78 Great Famine; 1885 INC; 1892 Councils Act; 1900 Punjab Land Alienation Act; 1905 Partition of Bengal; 1906 Muslim League; 1907 Surat split and Punjab colony agitation; 1909 Morley–Minto.
- 1913–15 Ghadar; 1916 Lucknow Pact and Home Rule; 1919 Rowlatt, Jallianwala and the Montford Act; 1920–22 Non-Cooperation and Khilafat (parallel to the Akali movement); 1927–28 Simon Commission and Lajpat Rai's death; 1929 Lahore Congress; 1930 Dandi; 1931 Gandhi–Irwin and Bhagat Singh's execution; 1932 Communal Award and Poona Pact; 1935 GoI Act.
- 1937 provincial ministries; 1940 Lahore Resolution; 1942 Cripps and Quit India; 1943 Bengal famine; 1945–46 INA trials and RIN mutiny; 1946 Cabinet Mission and Direct Action; 1947 Mountbatten Plan, Partition and Independence.
- 1950 Constitution; 1951–52 first elections; 1956 States Reorganisation; 1962, 1965, 1971 wars; 1966 Punjab reorganisation; 1966–67 Green Revolution; 1969 bank nationalisation; 1975–77 Emergency; 1984 Blue Star and Indira Gandhi's assassination; 1985 Punjab and Assam accords; 1991 reforms; 1992 73rd and 74th Amendments.

### The 18th-century debate: 'dark age' or regional vitality?

- The older view (Jadunath Sarkar, Irvine) saw the 18th century as Mughal collapse, anarchy and decline that opened the door to the British.
- Revisionists (Muzaffar Alam, C.A. Bayly, Satish Chandra's jagirdari-crisis thesis) see the decentralisation of a strong system into vigorous regional states: Awadh, Bengal, Hyderabad, Mysore, the Marathas and the Sikh misls.
- Punjab fits both views: Mughal–Afghan breakdown and violence (Ghallugharas), but also the rise of a new political community (the Khalsa), new institutions (Sarbat Khalsa, rakhi) and finally a centralised state under Ranjit Singh.
- Economic continuity: bankers (Jagat Seth in Bengal, Khatri and Multani bankers in Punjab), revenue farming and trade networks survived and were used by the Company.
- The British advantage lay in finance (Bengal revenue), military fiscalism (sepoy armies paid in cash), diplomacy (subsidiary alliance) and the rivalries of regional powers. Punjab was the last to fall (1849).
- Answer tip: a balanced conclusion is that political fragmentation went with economic and cultural dynamism in the regions, and the Company exploited that fragmentation.

### Colonial economy: mechanisms and critiques

- Drain of wealth: Dadabhai Naoroji ('Poverty and Un-British Rule in India', 1901, built on his papers from 1867), R.C. Dutt ('Economic History of India', 1901–03) and M.G. Ranade. Channels of drain: home charges, guaranteed interest on railways, salaries and pensions, and profits.
- Deindustrialisation debate: nationalist and Marxist writers (Bipan Chandra) against revisionists (Morris D. Morris). Handloom decline was real but uneven by region.
- Land revenue systems: Permanent (zamindari, 1793), Ryotwari (Munro and Read) and Mahalwari (Holt Mackenzie, 1822; extended to Punjab as village-community settlement). All moved to cash demand, a land market and rising debt.
- Commercialisation and infrastructure: railways (1853), the Suez Canal (1869), cash crops (indigo, cotton, jute, wheat), and Punjab's canal colonies as the showpiece of irrigation-led commercial agriculture.
- Famines and policy: 1876–78, 1896–97 and 1899–1900. The Famine Commissions (1880 Strachey, 1898 Lyall, 1901 MacDonnell) led to the Famine Codes. The 1943 Bengal famine was a war-time policy failure.
- Peasant responses: the Indigo revolt (1859–60), Deccan riots (1875), Pabna (1873), Punjab's 1907 colony agitation, Moplah (1921), Bardoli (1928), Tebhaga (1946) and Telangana (1946–51).
- Punjab examples are useful in answers: the Alienation of Land Act (1900) as a colonial response to debt; Darling's Punjab Peasant (1925); and Chhotu Ram's debt laws (1934–39).

### Constitutional development: what each Act changed

- Regulating Act 1773: Governor-General of Bengal and a Supreme Court at Calcutta. Pitt's India Act 1784: a Board of Control, giving dual control.
- Charter Acts: 1813 ended the trade monopoly except in tea and China trade and set aside ₹1 lakh for education; 1833 created the Governor-General of India, ended trading functions and added a law member; 1853 introduced open competition for the civil service and a separate legislative wing.
- Government of India Act 1858: Crown rule, a Secretary of State and the India Council. Indian Councils Act 1861: Indians nominated to councils, the portfolio system and ordinance power.
- Indian Councils Act 1892: indirect election in principle, with budget discussion. Morley–Minto 1909: enlarged councils and separate electorates for Muslims.
- Montford (GoI Act 1919): provincial dyarchy (in Punjab from 1921, with transferred subjects under Indian ministers), a bicameral central legislature and a limited franchise. Sikh separate electorates came in Punjab.
- GoI Act 1935: provincial autonomy, a proposed federation (never formed), a Federal Court (1937), the RBI (1935) and a wider franchise. The 1937 elections brought the Unionist ministry in Punjab.
- Indian Independence Act 1947: two dominions, the end of paramountcy, and the Constituent Assembly as the legislature. Punjab and Bengal were partitioned by boundary commissions.

### Phases of nationalism, with Punjab hooks

- Moderates (1885–1905): petitions and economic critique. Punjab contributed The Tribune (1881), Dyal Singh Majithia and PNB (1894).
- Extremists and Swadeshi (1905–09): Lal–Bal–Pal. In Punjab, Lajpat Rai, Ajit Singh and the 1907 deportations.
- Revolutionaries: the Ghadar movement (1913–15, an overseas Punjabi base), the Babbar Akalis, the NBS (1926) and HSRA (1928), Bhagat Singh's socialist turn and Udham Singh (1940).
- Gandhian mass phases: the Rowlatt Satyagraha and Jallianwala (1919); Non-Cooperation, with the Akali movement as its parallel; Civil Disobedience (the Lahore Congress of 1929 declared Purna Swaraj); Quit India (1942), which was weaker in Unionist-ruled Punjab.
- Left and peasant politics: the Kirti Kisan Party (1928), the Congress Socialist Party (1934), the All-India Kisan Sabha (1936) and the Punjab Kisan Sabha morchas.
- Communal and provincial currents: the Muslim League (1906), the Hindu Mahasabha (1915), the Unionist Party (1923), the Akali Dal (1920) and the Communal Award (1932). Punjab's Unionist cross-communal model collapsed by 1946.
- INA and post-war upsurge: Mohan Singh's first INA (1942); the Red Fort trials (1945) of Shah Nawaz Khan, Prem Sahgal and Gurbaksh Singh Dhillon; the RIN mutiny (1946). These hastened the transfer of power.
- Historiography: imperialist (James Mill, Vincent Smith), nationalist, Marxist (R.P. Dutt, Bipan Chandra, Sumit Sarkar), Cambridge school (Anil Seal, Gallagher: elite competition) and Subaltern (Ranajit Guha, from 1982). Name at least two schools in analytical answers.

### Social reform, caste and identity movements

- Reform laws: sati banned (1829, Bentinck and Rammohun Roy); Widow Remarriage Act (1856, Vidyasagar); Age of Consent Act (1891); Sarda Act (1929, child marriage); Hindu Code Bills (1955–56).
- Reform bodies: Brahmo Samaj (1828), Prarthana Samaj (1867), Arya Samaj (1875; Lahore 1877), Ramakrishna Mission (1897), Aligarh movement (1875 MAO College), Deoband (1866), Ahmadiyya (1889, Qadian, Punjab) and Singh Sabha (1873).
- Caste movements: Satyashodhak Samaj (Phule, 1873), SNDP (1903), Self-Respect (Periyar, 1925), Ambedkar's Mahad Satyagraha (1927) and Ad-Dharm in Punjab (Mangu Ram, 1925–26; a separate religion in the 1931 census).
- Women in Punjab: Singh Sabha and Arya Samaj girls' schools (Kanya Mahavidyalaya, Jalandhar, 1891); women in the Akali morchas and the Ghadar (Gulab Kaur).
- Print and language: Urdu, Hindi and Punjabi in Gurmukhi became markers of identity in Punjab. This was the root of the 1950s–60s Punjabi Suba controversy.
- Assessment: modernising and rationalist, but often upper-caste and urban-led, and in Punjab it helped harden religious boundaries (Oberoi, Kenneth Jones).

### Independent India: significant issues (with the Punjab case)

- Integration of the princely states (Patel and V.P. Menon): Instruments of Accession and merger, and unions such as PEPSU (1948), Saurashtra and Rajasthan.
- Linguistic reorganisation: Potti Sriramulu's death (1952) created Andhra (1953); the SRC (1953–55) and the 1956 Act; Bombay split (1960); Punjab (1966); North-East reorganisation (1971–72); three new states (2000); Telangana (2014); J&K's reorganisation into UTs (2019).
- Regional and ethnic movements and accords: the Punjab (Rajiv–Longowal) Accord and Assam Accord (both 1985), the Mizo Accord (1986) and the Darjeeling (Gorkha) Accord (1988). Compare the causes, the role of religion and language, and outcomes.
- Agrarian transformation: land reforms (zamindari abolition, ceilings), Community Development (1952), the Green Revolution (Punjab, Haryana, western UP), the White Revolution (Operation Flood, 1970) and the farm-law agitation (2020–21).
- Centre–state relations: the Rajamannar (Tamil Nadu, 1969) and Anandpur Sahib (1973) demands, and the Sarkaria (1983–88) and Punchhi (2007–10) Commissions. Article 356 was first used in Punjab (1951).
- Economy: the planning era (1951–2014; NITI Aayog in 2015), the 1969 bank nationalisation, the 1991 liberalisation, and Punjab's relative decline in per capita rank after the 1990s.
- Personalities PPSC tends to ask about: Nehru, Patel, Ambedkar, Rajendra Prasad, Lal Bahadur Shastri ('Jai Jawan Jai Kisan', 1965), Partap Singh Kairon, Master Tara Singh, Sant Fateh Singh, Giani Zail Singh (President 1982–87) and Manmohan Singh.

### Answer-writing for PPSC Mains (GS-I modern history)

- The syllabus says 'significant events, personalities and issues', so organise answers by issue (economy, society, constitutional change, nationalism) rather than by narrative.
- Always add a Punjab paragraph: for example, Ranjit Singh's state within 18th-century regional states, canal colonies within the colonial economy, Ghadar and Jallianwala within nationalism, and 1966 and 1985 within post-1947 reorganisation and federalism.
- Use dated evidence and two historians' views: for instance Naoroji versus Morris on the drain, the Cambridge versus Subaltern schools on nationalism, and Grewal versus McLeod on Sikh history.
- For personality questions (Gandhi, Bhagat Singh, Lajpat Rai, Chhotu Ram, Master Tara Singh), give background, ideas, methods, achievements, limitations and legacy.
- Aim for 150 words for 10-mark and 250 words for 15/20-mark questions: an introduction with context, three or four sub-headed arguments, and a balanced conclusion linked to the present (for example federalism, farm distress or identity politics).
- A simple timeline or flow diagram (for example 1909 → 1919 → 1935 → 1947) earns marks in constitutional-development questions.

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** 'Plassey made the British traders; Buxar made them rulers.' Examine the significance of the two battles in the establishment of British power in India. (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: Bengal's wealth and Company ambitions after 1707
- Plassey 1757: conspiracy (Mir Jafar, Jagat Seths), not military superiority; puppet Nawabs; drain begins
- Buxar 1764: defeat of combined Mir Qasim, Shuja-ud-Daula, Shah Alam II - real military victory
- Treaty of Allahabad 1765: Diwani, legal sovereignty over revenue
- Consequences: Bengal's resources fund further conquest
- Conclusion: Buxar converted commercial dominance into political sovereignty

</details>

**2.** Critically examine the economic critique of British rule developed by the early nationalists. How did it contribute to the growth of nationalism? (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: Moderates' economic nationalism (Naoroji, Ranade, R.C. Dutt, Gokhale)
- Drain of wealth: home charges, salaries, profits; Naoroji's estimates
- Deindustrialisation, ruin of handicrafts, one-way free trade
- Land revenue, rural indebtedness, famines
- Contribution: exposed the exploitative nature of colonialism, prepared ground for Swadeshi
- Limits: elite, constitutional methods; but intellectual foundation of mass nationalism
- Conclusion: economic critique was the ideological backbone of the national movement

</details>

**3.** Trace the transformation of the Indian national movement from an elite-led to a mass movement under Gandhi. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: Moderate and Extremist phases had limited mass base
- Gandhi's methods: satyagraha, non-violence, constructive programme, local struggles (Champaran, Kheda, Ahmedabad)
- Mass campaigns: NCM, CDM, Quit India - peasants, women, workers, students
- Organisational changes: Nagpur 1920 constitution, PCCs on linguistic lines, four-anna membership
- Inclusion of regional streams (Akali movement in Punjab, Vaikom)
- Limits: Dalit and Muslim mobilisation, revolutionary alternative
- Conclusion: Gandhi turned nationalism into a people's movement

</details>
