## Short notes

- Vulnerable sections: SCs, STs (especially PVTGs), OBCs, minorities, women, children, elderly, persons with disabilities, transgender persons, landless labourers, migrant and informal workers, manual scavengers, drug-affected people.
- Constitutional safeguards: Arts 15(4), 16(4), 17, 21A, 23, 24, 39, 41, 46; Arts 243D/243T (local body reservation), 330, 332; Fifth and Sixth Schedules.
- Constitutional bodies: National Commission for SCs (Art 338), for STs (Art 338A), for Backward Classes (Art 338B, 102nd Amendment 2018).
- Statutory bodies: NCW (1992), National Commission for Minorities (1992), NHRC (1993), NCPCR (2007), National Commission for Safai Karamcharis, Chief Commissioner for Persons with Disabilities.
- Key laws: Protection of Civil Rights Act 1955; SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act 1989 (amended 2015, 2018); Prohibition of Employment as Manual Scavengers Act 2013; Forest Rights Act 2006; PESA 1996; Bonded Labour (Abolition) Act 1976.
- Laws for other groups: RPwD Act 2016 (21 disabilities, 4% reservation in government jobs); Transgender Persons Act 2019; Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act 2007; Juvenile Justice Act 2015; POCSO 2012; RTE 2009; NFSA 2013.
- Central schemes for SCs/STs: Post-Matric Scholarship, PM-AJAY (2021-22), Stand-Up India (2016), PM-JANMAN (2023) for PVTGs, Eklavya Model Residential Schools.
- Other central schemes: SMILE (2022) for transgender persons and people engaged in begging; NAMASTE (2023) for sanitation workers; PM-DAKSH (skilling); Accessible India Campaign (2015) and UDID cards; National Social Assistance Programme pensions.
- Universal welfare: PM-JAY (₹5 lakh health cover; extended to all people aged 70+ in 2024), PMAY, PMGKAY, e-Shram portal (2021) for unorganised workers, PM SVANidhi for street vendors, Mission Shakti for women.
- Performance positives: DBT and JAM trinity reduce leakages; Aadhaar-linked targeting; social audits under MGNREGA; increased coverage of housing, toilets, bank accounts.
- Performance gaps: low utilisation of funds, delayed scholarships, exclusion errors from digital authentication, low conviction rates under the PoA Act, continuing sewer deaths, weak awareness and monitoring, data gaps.
- Way forward: convergence of schemes, outcome budgeting, independent evaluation, strong grievance redressal, community participation, gender and disability budgeting, sensitised frontline officials.

## Punjab angle

- Punjab has the highest SC share among states (31.9%, Census 2011); the Punjab State Commission for Scheduled Castes handles complaints; Post-Matric Scholarship funding was restructured to 60:40 Centre-State sharing in 2020 after delays hit Punjab's SC students.
- Social security pensions (old age, widows, disabled) were raised to ₹1,500 per month in 2021; Ashirwad scheme gives marriage assistance to daughters of SC, BC and poor families.
- Mukh Mantri Mawan Dhian Satikar Yojana (₹1,000 per month to women 18+, ₹1,500 to SC women) and free bus travel for women target gender vulnerability.
- Aam Aadmi Clinics (from Aug 2022) extend free primary care; 'Yudh Nashian Virudh' (2025) targets drug-affected families with enforcement plus de-addiction.
- Punjab Budget 2026-27 (outlay ₹2,60,437 crore) - welfare commitments must be weighed against a fiscal deficit of about 4.1% of GSDP and high debt.

## Deep dive

### Punjab's vulnerability map — who and how many (source, year)

- Scheduled Castes: 31.9% of the population (Census 2011), about 73% of them rural and mostly landless; Dalits own only about 3.5% of the land — landlessness is the core vulnerability.
- Poor households: multidimensional poverty 4.75% (NITI MPI, 2019-21; 5.57% in 2015-16); nutrition contributes about 31% of Punjab's MPI score, so hidden hunger matters more than income poverty.
- Drug-affected: the Punjab Opioid Dependence Survey (AIIMS-NDDTC, 10 districts, Feb-April 2015) estimated about 2.3 lakh opioid-dependent people (0.84% of the population), about 1.23 lakh dependent on heroin, and roughly 75,000 injecting users; annual spending on opioids about Rs 7,500 crore.
- Farm labourers and small farmers: among the 16,606 farm suicides of 2000-2015, debt was the main factor; labourers' suicide rates exceeded farmers'.
- Left-behind elderly and women: emigration of children leaves aged parents alone in villages; wives deserted by NRI husbands form a distinct vulnerable group.
- Border-district families: land beyond the fence, displacement during mobilisations (1971, 2001-02, May 2025), weak schools and health facilities, and drug-smuggling exposure.
- Migrant workers from other states: seasonal paddy and construction labour with weak housing, ration portability and social-security access.
- Persons with disabilities, transgender persons and children in need of care complete the list; Punjab's protection depends heavily on district-level officers.

### Where the SDM / PCS officer fits in — statutory roles

- Senior citizens: under the Maintenance and Welfare of Parents and Senior Citizens Act 2007, the Maintenance Tribunal is presided over by an officer not below the rank of Sub-Divisional Officer; it can order monthly maintenance from children and void property transfers made on a promise of care.
- SC/ST atrocities: under the PoA Act 1989 and its 1995 Rules, the District Magistrate and SDM must visit the scene, assess and release relief, arrange protection for victims and witnesses, and can declare atrocity-prone areas; special and exclusive special courts try cases.
- Bonded labour: the Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act 1976 gives District Magistrates and SDMs powers to release and rehabilitate bonded labourers and to run vigilance committees.
- Domestic violence: under the PWDVA 2005, protection officers and magistrates provide protection, residence and monetary orders; the SDM's role is in coordination and emergency support.
- Child marriage: under the Prohibition of Child Marriage Act 2006 the District Magistrate can act as Child Marriage Prohibition Officer and stop mass marriages on festival days.
- Executive magistrate powers under the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita 2023 (preventive orders, inquiries into custodial deaths) protect vulnerable groups during communal tension or protests.
- Land and common property: SDMs and revenue officers oversee panchayat land auctions, where one-third of leased cultivable shamlat land is reserved for SCs — a direct field-level empowerment duty.
- Exam use: a question on 'mechanisms for protection' scores higher when it names the officer who acts, not only the law.

### Punjab's own institutions and laws for vulnerable sections

- Punjab State Commission for Scheduled Castes (statutory, Act of 2004) — hears complaints of atrocities, service matters and discrimination.
- Punjab State Commission for Women (Act of 2001), Punjab State Commission for Backward Classes, Punjab State Commission for Protection of Child Rights, and Punjab State Human Rights Commission (1997).
- Punjab Scheduled Castes and Backward Classes (Reservation in Services) Act 2006 — its preference clause for Balmikis and Mazhabi Sikhs was the subject of the 2024 Supreme Court sub-classification ruling.
- Punjab Scheduled Castes Land Development and Finance Corporation — subsidised loans for self-employment; Ashirwad scheme for marriage assistance; post-matric scholarship top-ups.
- Punjab Right to Service Act 2011 — notified timelines for certificates (caste, income, residence) that vulnerable groups need to access schemes.
- Social security: old-age, widow, destitute women, dependent children and disability pensions through the Department of Social Security, Women and Child Development.
- Anti-drug architecture: the state Special Task Force against drugs (2017), later restructured as an Anti-Narcotics Task Force, plus de-addiction and rehabilitation centres and OOAT clinics in every district.

### Drug-affected people as a vulnerable group — policy evaluation

- Law: the NDPS Act 1985 punishes trafficking heavily but allows a softer path for addicts — Section 64A gives immunity from prosecution to an addict who volunteers for treatment, and Section 39 allows courts to send offenders for de-addiction.
- National response: Nasha Mukt Bharat Abhiyaan (launched Aug 2020 in the most affected districts, including Punjab's); the 2019 national survey on the magnitude of substance use (AIIMS-NDDTC for the Social Justice Ministry) found Punjab among the states with the highest opioid use.
- Treatment model: Punjab's Outpatient Opioid Assisted Treatment (OOAT) clinics use buprenorphine-naloxone maintenance, recognising addiction as a chronic health condition; government de-addiction centres provide detoxification and counselling.
- Gaps: relapse rates are high, private rehab centres sometimes confine patients illegally, women and injecting users face stigma, HIV and hepatitis C spread through shared needles, and jobs for recovering users are scarce.
- Supply side: drones from across the border, pharmaceutical opioids diverted from chemists, and gang networks; enforcement campaigns (such as 'Yudh Nashian Virudh', 2025) combine arrests, property attachment and community pledges.
- Balanced approach for answers: supply reduction (border, chemists, finance), demand reduction (schools, sports, jobs), harm reduction (OST, needle-syringe programmes) and rehabilitation (skills, family counselling) — the four pillars.
- Human rights angle: treat users as patients, traffickers as criminals; avoid mass incarceration of users, which overcrowds jails and deepens addiction.

### Evaluating central protective mechanisms — beyond the scheme list

- Atrocities law: the Supreme Court diluted arrest provisions in Subhash Kashinath Mahajan (March 2018); Parliament restored them by the 2018 amendment (Section 18A), which the Court upheld in Prithvi Raj Chauhan (2020).
- Budget instruments: the old SC Sub-Plan and Tribal Sub-Plan became the Development Action Plans for SCs and STs; critics point to 'notional' allocations not targeted at SC/ST households.
- Manual scavenging: banned by the 2013 Act, yet sewer and septic-tank deaths continue; the Supreme Court's Balram Singh (Oct 2023) ruling ordered compensation of Rs 30 lakh for each sewer death; NAMASTE (2023) shifts the focus to mechanised cleaning.
- Disability: RPwD Act 2016 promises accessible buildings and transport, but compliance audits under the Accessible India Campaign showed slow progress; the UDID card has eased certification.
- Transgender persons: the 2019 Act was criticised for requiring a district magistrate's certificate; the SMILE scheme offers shelter homes (Garima Greh) and skilling.
- Elderly: the Atal Vayo Abhyuday Yojana (AVYAY) funds old-age homes and helplines (Elder Line 14567); PM-JAY now covers everyone aged 70 and above.
- Evidence tools for answers: social audits, CAG performance audits, independent evaluations by NITI's DMEO, and grievance data from CPGRAMS.

### Common traps

- The National Commission for SCs (Article 338) and for STs (Article 338A) are constitutional; the National Commission for Safai Karamcharis and the NCW are statutory.
- Punjab has no notified Scheduled Tribes, so ST schemes (PM-JANMAN, EMRS) have little footprint there.
- The Maintenance Tribunal for senior citizens is headed by an SDM-rank officer, not by a judicial magistrate.
- Section 64A of the NDPS Act protects addicts who seek treatment, not those caught with commercial quantities.
- Sub-classification of SCs is allowed (2024); creamy layer for SCs was only suggested by some judges, not mandated.
- Two different Supreme Court cases carry the title Balram Singh v. Union of India: the Oct 2023 judgment on sewer deaths and manual scavenging, and the Nov 2024 judgment upholding 'socialist' and 'secular' in the Preamble.

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** Evaluate the effectiveness of constitutional and legal mechanisms for the protection of Scheduled Castes, with special reference to Punjab. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: Punjab's highest SC share; constitutional vision
- Mechanisms: Arts 15-17, 46, 338; PCR Act, PoA Act; NCSC and state commission
- Achievements: reservation-led mobility, Ad-Dharm legacy, political representation
- Gaps: landlessness, low conviction under PoA, scholarship delays, caste discrimination in gurdwaras/cremation grounds in some villages
- Davinder Singh (2024) sub-classification and Punjab's Balmiki-Mazhabi preference
- Way forward: land access, education quality, fast-track courts, awareness
- Conclusion: rights on paper must become rights in practice

</details>

**2.** Critically assess welfare schemes for persons with disabilities, transgender persons and the elderly in India. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: rights-based approach - RPwD 2016, Transgender Act 2019, Senior Citizens Act 2007
- Schemes: Accessible India, UDID, SMILE, NSAP, PM-JAY for 70+
- Performance: coverage gains, but accessibility gaps, certification hurdles, low pension amounts
- Social barriers: stigma, family neglect
- Punjab: social security pensions, Aam Aadmi Clinics
- Way forward: universal design, community care, inclusive employment
- Conclusion: dignity, not charity

</details>

**3.** Has Direct Benefit Transfer improved the delivery of welfare to vulnerable sections? Discuss. (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: DBT and JAM trinity
- Gains: reduced leakage, faster transfers, financial inclusion
- Problems: authentication failures, exclusion, bank access in remote areas, digital literacy
- Evidence from scholarships, PM-KISAN, pensions
- Way forward: offline fallbacks, grievance redress, social audits
- Conclusion: technology must be inclusive by design

</details>
