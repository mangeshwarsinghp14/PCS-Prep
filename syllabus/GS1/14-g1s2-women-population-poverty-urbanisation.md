## Short notes

- Women's status indicators: NFHS-5 (2019-21) - overall sex ratio 1,020 women per 1,000 men, sex ratio at birth 929, TFR 2.0; SRS 2021 sex ratio at birth 913; female labour force participation rate 41.7% (PLFS 2023-24, 15+, usual status).
- Historic women's organisations: Bharat Stree Mahamandal (1910, Sarala Devi Chaudhurani), Women's Indian Association (1917), All India Women's Conference (1927); after independence - SEWA (1972, Ela Bhatt), women in Chipko, anti-arrack and anti-dowry campaigns.
- Self-help groups: about 10 crore women mobilised under DAY-NRLM; Kerala's Kudumbashree (1998) is a model of women-led local development.
- Laws for women: Dowry Prohibition Act 1961, PWDVA 2005, POSH Act 2013, Criminal Law (Amendment) 2013 after the Justice Verma Committee, Maternity Benefit (Amendment) Act 2017 (26 weeks).
- Political empowerment: one-third reservation in panchayats and municipalities (73rd/74th Amendments, many states 50%); Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam 2023 (106th Amendment) reserves one-third of Lok Sabha and Assembly seats after the next census and delimitation.
- Persistent issues: son preference, violence against women, unpaid care work, low workforce participation, digital and wage gaps, under-representation in leadership.
- Population: India became the world's most populous country (UN estimates, 2023); TFR has fallen below replacement (2.0, NFHS-5) but with regional gaps (higher in Bihar, UP; below 2 in the South).
- Population issues: demographic dividend window, youth unemployment, ageing and elderly care, migration, skewed sex ratios; National Population Policy 2000 aimed at population stabilisation.
- Poverty estimates: Tendulkar method gave 21.9% (2011-12), Rangarajan 29.5% (2011-12); NITI Aayog's multidimensional poverty fell from 29.17% (2013-14) to 11.28% (2022-23), with about 24.8 crore people exiting.
- World Bank (2025) estimated India's extreme poverty fell from 27.1% (2011-12) to 5.3% (2022-23) at the $3/day (2021 PPP) line.
- Development issues: informality of most of the workforce, regional disparities, rural distress, inequality, quality of jobs; PMGKAY gives free foodgrains to about 81 crore people (extended for five years from Jan 2024).
- Urbanisation: urban share 31.16% (Census 2011), 53 million-plus urban agglomerations; slum population about 6.5 crore (Census 2011).
- Urban problems: housing shortage and slums, water and sanitation, waste management, traffic congestion, air pollution, urban floods, heat islands, unemployment and informal settlements, weak municipal finances.
- Remedies and schemes: Smart Cities Mission (2015), AMRUT (2015) and AMRUT 2.0 (2021), PMAY-Urban and PMAY-U 2.0 (2024), Swachh Bharat Mission-Urban 2.0, transit-oriented development, metro rail, empowering ULBs under the 74th Amendment, municipal bonds.
- Way forward: gender budgeting, care infrastructure, skilling, regional balanced growth, planned urbanisation with strong local governments.

## Punjab angle

- Punjab's child sex ratio was 846 (Census 2011), among the lowest - a legacy of son preference; Beti Bachao Beti Padhao and PC-PNDT enforcement target it.
- Mukh Mantri Mawan Dhian Satikar Yojana gives ₹1,000 per month to women aged 18+ (₹1,500 to SC women); free bus travel for women in state buses since 2021.
- Punjab is more urbanised than the national average (37.5%, Census 2011); Ludhiana, Amritsar and Jalandhar are Smart Cities Mission cities.
- Large-scale youth emigration (Canada, Australia, UK) is changing Punjab's demography - ageing villages, locked houses in Doaba, remittance-dependent households.
- Punjab has among the lower multidimensional poverty levels in India, but pockets of deprivation persist among landless SC households and in border districts.

## Deep dive

### Declining sex ratio in Punjab — causes, consequences, measures (PPSC 2026 asked)

- Trend: Punjab's child sex ratio (0-6) was 798 in 2001 — among the worst in India, with Fatehgarh Sahib near the bottom nationally — and recovered to 846 in 2011; NFHS-5 (2019-21) shows the overall sex ratio at 938 and a sex ratio at birth of about 904, still well below the natural level of about 950.
- Causes: strong son preference tied to land inheritance and lineage, dowry costs, small family norm (TFR about 1.6) that squeezes out daughters, easy access to ultrasound from the 1990s, and the prosperity that made sex selection affordable.
- Consequences: shortage of brides, 'bought' brides from poorer states, trafficking risk, more violence against women, and later demographic imbalance in marriage markets.
- Laws: PC-PNDT Act 1994 (amended 2003 to cover pre-conception selection); sealing of unregistered ultrasound machines, decoy operations and rewards for informers.
- State schemes: Balri Rakshak Yojana (2005) for families with daughters, the 'Nanhi Chhaan' campaign (2008) linking girl child and tree planting, Beti Bachao Beti Padhao (2015) in all districts.
- Social measures: Sikh religious bodies' edicts against female foeticide, panchayat-level honours for villages with good sex ratios, celebrating Lohri for daughters ('dhiyan di Lohri').
- Way forward: equal inheritance in practice, girls' higher education and jobs, old-age security that reduces reliance on sons, strict tracking of every pregnancy through ANMs and the RCH portal.

### Women in Punjab — organisations, work and voice

- Early organisations: Sikh Kanya Mahavidyalaya at Ferozepur (1892), founded by Bhai Takht Singh and Bibi Harnam Kaur, was a pioneer of girls' education in Punjab; the Singh Sabha and Arya Samaj ran girls' schools.
- Freedom struggle: Bibi Gulab Kaur of the Ghadar movement and women in the Gurdwara Reform (Akali) movement of the 1920s.
- Agrarian protest (PPSC 2021 asked): during the 2020-21 farm-law agitation women ran langars, managed stages at the Delhi borders, and 18 Jan 2021 was observed as Mahila Kisan Diwas; Dalit and landless women joined through labour unions.
- Self-help groups (PPSC 2026 asked): the Punjab State Rural Livelihoods Mission (PSRLM, under DAY-NRLM) organises rural women into SHGs and village organisations for thrift, credit, dairy, food processing and stitching; SHG products are sold through Saras melas and Verka-type cooperative links.
- Role in agrarian economy: women dominate dairy and animal care, seed preservation, kitchen gardens and post-harvest work, but are rarely recorded as 'farmers' because land is in men's names.
- Issues specific to Punjab: NRI marriages and abandoned brides, dowry demands linked to foreign settlement, wives left behind by emigrant husbands, drug addiction of male family members, and domestic violence.
- Institutions: Punjab State Commission for Women; NRI cell of Punjab Police and the Punjab State Commission for NRIs; one-stop centres (Sakhi) in every district.
- Answer tip: tie SHGs to the agrarian economy through dairy, value addition (pickles, phulkari, jaggery) and collective marketing, and admit limits — small loans, weak markets and male control of income.

### Population and demographic trends — India and Punjab

- Transition: India is in the late third stage of demographic transition — SRS 2023 put the crude birth rate at 18.4 (21.4 in 2013) and SRS 2021 the TFR at 2.0.
- Projection: the Technical Group on Population Projections (2020) projected India's population at about 152 crore by 2036, with a rising median age and a shrinking child share.
- Ageing: the India Ageing Report 2023 (UNFPA-IIPS) expected the 60+ share to roughly double from about 10% in the early 2020s to about 21% by 2050; Punjab, Kerala, Himachal and Tamil Nadu are among the 'older' states.
- Punjab's demographic signature: low fertility (NFHS-5 TFR about 1.6), slow growth (13.9% in 2001-11), heavy youth emigration abroad, and in-migration of labourers from Uttar Pradesh and Bihar for paddy transplantation, construction and industry.
- Effects: ageing villages and locked houses in Doaba, a rising share of migrant workers in Ludhiana's industry, shortage of local farm labour, and changing linguistic and religious mix in towns.
- North-South divergence: states with low fertility (South, Punjab) fear losing Lok Sabha seats in delimitation after the next census; high-fertility states (Bihar, UP) will dominate the working-age pool.
- PPSC 2018 asked about the changing rural-urban demography of India — use urban share rising, census towns, peri-urban growth and rural-to-urban male migration.

### Poverty — measurement and the latest numbers

- Committees: Alagh task force (1979, calorie norms of 2,400 rural and 2,100 urban), Lakdawala (1993), Tendulkar (2009, consumption basket incl. health and education), Rangarajan (2014, higher line); no official consumption poverty line has been notified since.
- Multidimensional Poverty Index (NITI Aayog, Alkire-Foster method; 3 dimensions — health, education, standard of living; 12 indicators): national headcount fell from 24.85% (2015-16) to 14.96% (2019-21), about 13.5 crore people exiting.
- Punjab MPI: 5.57% (2015-16) to 4.75% (2019-21); rural Punjab 6.38% to 4.74%; about 2.5 lakh people exited; nutrition is the largest single contributor (about 31%) to Punjab's MPI score.
- Consumption (HCES 2023-24, without imputation): all-India average monthly per-capita spending Rs 4,122 rural and Rs 6,996 urban (gap about 70%); Punjab Rs 5,817 rural and Rs 7,359 urban — rural Punjab spends well above the national rural average.
- Inequality: consumption Gini fell to 0.237 (rural) and 0.284 (urban) in 2023-24 from 0.266 and 0.314 in 2022-23 — but consumption surveys understate top-end inequality compared with income and wealth data.
- Punjab's poor: landless Dalit labourers, drug-affected households, border-district families and small tenants — poverty here is pocketed rather than widespread.
- Remember: MPI counts deprivations, not money; the World Bank line counts money ($3/day at 2021 PPP); never compare the two numbers directly.

### Urbanisation and migration — concepts, Punjab and policy

- Definitions: a statutory town has a municipality or corporation; a census town has at least 5,000 people, 75% or more of male main workers outside agriculture and density of at least 400 per sq km; Census 2011 counted 7,933 towns (4,041 statutory and 3,892 census towns).
- Hidden urbanisation: census towns are governed as villages (gram panchayats), so they lack urban services — a key reason India's official urban share understates reality.
- Punjab (PPSC 2021 asked): urban share 37.5% (2011); Ludhiana is the largest city, and Ludhiana and Amritsar are million-plus agglomerations; growth follows the GT Road corridor (Rajpura-Ludhiana-Jalandhar-Amritsar) and the Mohali-Chandigarh-Panchkula tricity.
- Punjab's urban problems: unauthorised colonies on farmland, polluted drains (Buddha Nullah), legacy garbage dumps, waterlogging in monsoons, traffic on the GT Road, weak municipal finances; governance under the Punjab Municipal Act 1911 and the Punjab Municipal Corporation Act 1976 with 13 municipal corporations.
- Migration data: Census 2011 counted about 45.6 crore migrants by place of last residence; marriage was the largest reason (about 46%, mostly women), work the main reason for men; the Economic Survey 2016-17 estimated about 90 lakh inter-state migrants a year in 2011-16 using railway data.
- Main corridors: Uttar Pradesh and Bihar are the largest source states; Maharashtra, Delhi, Gujarat and Haryana-Punjab the main destinations.
- Policy: One Nation One Ration Card, e-Shram registration of unorganised workers, the OSH Code 2020 (which subsumed the Inter-State Migrant Workmen Act 1979), affordable rental housing complexes after the 2020 Covid exodus.
- Two-way Punjab: out-migration of educated youth abroad and in-migration of poorer workers from the Hindi belt — answer questions on Punjab's urbanisation and demography with both flows.

### Common traps

- Child sex ratio (0-6 years) and sex ratio at birth are different measures; overall sex ratio can rise while child sex ratio stays low.
- NFHS overall sex ratio (household survey) is not comparable with the Census sex ratio.
- HCES gives consumption, not income; MPI gives deprivation, not consumption.
- Census towns are not statutory towns; their number almost tripled between 2001 and 2011.
- Most Indian migrants are women migrating for marriage, not men migrating for work.
- Tendulkar's 21.9% (2011-12) is a consumption-poverty ratio; NITI's 11.28% (2022-23) is a multidimensional ratio — different concepts, not a time series.

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** Assess the role of women's organisations and self-help groups in the empowerment of women in India. (250 words)

*Like a real PPSC question: [Mains 2026 · GS1 Q17](../../past-papers/mains_2026_GS1.md#q17)*

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: empowerment as agency, resources and achievement
- Historical role: AIWC and others in reform laws and suffrage
- Post-independence: SEWA, environmental and anti-liquor movements, legal campaigns
- SHGs: DAY-NRLM, Kudumbashree - credit, livelihoods, local leadership
- Limitations: elite capture, debt cycles, patriarchal resistance, limited market access
- Punjab: Mawan Dhian Satikar Yojana, women SHGs in rural livelihoods
- Conclusion: collective action converts schemes into real empowerment

</details>

**2.** 'India's demographic dividend can become a demographic disaster.' Discuss in the context of population trends and associated issues. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: TFR decline, large working-age population
- Opportunities: labour supply, savings, growth
- Risks: jobless growth, low FLFPR, poor skills, health and nutrition gaps
- Regional asymmetry: north-south divergence, migration
- Ageing ahead: elderly care, pensions
- Way forward: education, skilling, women's work, health, labour-intensive manufacturing
- Conclusion: dividend is time-bound and policy-dependent

</details>

**3.** Identify the major problems of urbanisation in India and suggest remedies. (150 words)

*Like a real PPSC question: [Mains 2021 · GS1 Q12(a)](../../past-papers/mains_2021_GS1.md#q12-a)*

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: urbanisation trends (Census 2011)
- Problems: housing and slums, water, waste, congestion, pollution, floods
- Governance gaps: weak ULBs, poor planning, finances
- Schemes: Smart Cities, AMRUT 2.0, PMAY-U 2.0, SBM-U 2.0
- Remedies: empowered city governments, compact planning, public transport, climate resilience
- Conclusion: cities as engines of growth need capable local states

</details>
