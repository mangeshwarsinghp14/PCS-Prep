## Short notes

- Earth's interior: crust, mantle, core separated by the Mohorovicic (crust-mantle) and Gutenberg (mantle-core) discontinuities; rigid lithosphere rides on the plastic asthenosphere.
- Wegener's continental drift (1912; Pangaea and Panthalassa) was revived by sea-floor spreading (Harry Hess) and plate tectonics; boundaries are divergent (Mid-Atlantic Ridge), convergent (Himalaya, Andes, trenches) and transform (San Andreas).
- Major landforms: fold mountains (Himalaya, Alps, Andes, Rockies), block mountains (Black Forest, Vosges), volcanic mountains, plateaus (Deccan, Tibet), plains; landforms shaped by rivers, glaciers, wind, groundwater (karst) and waves.
- Atmospheric circulation: pressure belts and planetary winds (trade winds, westerlies, polar easterlies), seasonal shift of the ITCZ, jet streams; Koppen's climate classification uses temperature and precipitation.
- Ocean currents: warm (Gulf Stream, Kuroshio, Brazil) and cold (Labrador, Humboldt/Peru, Benguela, Canary); warm-cold mixing creates rich fishing grounds (Grand Banks, Dogger Bank); cold currents border west-coast deserts (Atacama, Namib).
- Biomes: tropical rainforest, savanna, hot desert, Mediterranean, temperate grasslands (Prairies, Steppes, Pampas, Veld, Downs), taiga (boreal), tundra.
- Petroleum: large reserves in West Asia (Saudi Arabia, Iran, Iraq), Venezuela (largest proven reserves), Russia, USA (largest producer in recent years), Canada (oil sands).
- Coal: China is the largest producer and consumer; India the second-largest producer; other major producers - Indonesia, Australia, USA, Russia.
- Metallic minerals: iron ore (Australia, Brazil, China, India); bauxite (Australia, Guinea); copper (Chile the top producer, Peru, DR Congo); cobalt (DR Congo dominant); lithium ('lithium triangle' of Chile, Argentina, Bolivia; Australia a major producer); rare earths (China dominant).
- South Asia/India: Chota Nagpur plateau is the mineral heartland (coal - Jharia, Raniganj; iron ore - Odisha, Jharkhand, Bailadila); petroleum at Mumbai High and Assam; gas in KG basin; monazite (thorium) in Kerala coastal sands; GSI reported inferred lithium resources at Reasi, J&K (2023).
- Primary activities locate where natural resources are: fertile soils, water, climate, forests, mineral deposits, fishing grounds.
- Weber's least-cost theory (1909): industry locates to minimise transport, labour and agglomeration costs; weight-losing raw materials pull industry to the source (iron and steel near coal/iron ore, sugar mills near cane fields).
- Other location factors: market (perishable or weight-gaining goods), power, labour skills and cost, transport and ports, capital, water, government policy (SEZs, tax incentives, industrial corridors), agglomeration economies.
- Footloose industries (electronics, software) are not tied to raw materials and locate by skilled labour, connectivity and policy; examples - Silicon Valley, Bengaluru, Hyderabad.
- Tertiary and quaternary activities (finance, IT, R&D, education, health) cluster in large cities with skilled labour, infrastructure, connectivity and institutions - global cities such as London, New York, Mumbai.
- Shifts in the global industrial map: relocation to low-cost economies (China, Vietnam), 'China plus one' strategies, reshoring and friend-shoring, critical-mineral supply chain security.

## Punjab angle

- Punjab has almost no major mineral deposits, so its industry is agro-based and dominated by MSMEs: hosiery, bicycles and auto parts (Ludhiana), sports goods and hand tools (Jalandhar), steel re-rolling (Mandi Gobindgarh), machine tools (Batala), textiles (Amritsar).
- Location handicaps: landlocked, far from ports and mineral belts, and a sensitive border; the 2003 central industrial package for Himachal Pradesh and Uttarakhand drew units away from Punjab.
- Mohali is being developed as an IT and electronics hub; the Eastern Dedicated Freight Corridor begins near Ludhiana (Sahnewal), improving freight access to the east.
- Agro-processing (food parks, dairy - Verka/Milkfed) fits Punjab's raw-material base; sector shares in GSVA 2025-26: agriculture 23%, industry 29%, services 48% (Punjab Economic Survey 2025-26).

## Deep dive

### World climatic regions at a glance (Koppen code — type — where — signature)

- Af — tropical rainforest — Amazon, Congo basin, Indonesia — rain every month, convectional afternoon showers, hardwoods (mahogany, ebony), shifting cultivation and plantations.
- Am — tropical monsoon — Western Ghats, NE India, Myanmar, Bangladesh — short dry season, very heavy seasonal rain; rice, jute, tea.
- Aw — tropical savanna — Sudan belt, East African plateau, Brazilian campos, Deccan interior — wet summer, long dry winter; grasslands with scattered trees, pastoralism and big-game parks.
- BWh / BSh — hot desert and steppe — Sahara, Arabia, Thar, Kalahari, Atacama, Australian interior — trade-wind belt or cold-current coasts; oases, nomadic herding; south-west Punjab grades into BSh.
- Cs — Mediterranean — Mediterranean rim, California, central Chile, SW Australia, Cape Town — winter rain, dry summer; citrus, olives, grapes and wine (the 'orchard lands of the world').
- Cwa / Cfa — humid subtropical — north Indian plains including Punjab (Cwa: dry winter), south China, SE USA — hot wet summer; rice, cotton, wheat in winter.
- Cfb — west-European (marine) — Britain, NW Europe, NZ, British Columbia — rain all year from westerlies, mild winters thanks to the North Atlantic Drift; dairying, mixed farming.
- Dfc / Dwc — taiga — Siberia, Canada, Scandinavia — long severe winters, coniferous softwood (pulp, paper), fur trapping; Dw types only in NE Asia (Siberian high gives dry winters).
- ET / EF — tundra and ice cap — Arctic fringe, Greenland, Antarctica — permafrost, mosses and lichens, Inuit and Sami herding; permafrost thaw is now a climate feedback.
- Exam use: PPSC 2018 asked 'Soils and vegetation are a true index of climate' — answer by pairing each climate type with its zonal soil (laterite in Af/Am, chernozem under steppe grass, podzol under taiga) and its natural vegetation.

### Where the world's key resources are — latest figures (USGS Mineral Commodity Summaries 2025, data for 2024)

- Lithium: mine output led by Australia (about 88,000 t), Chile (49,000 t) and China (41,000 t); Zimbabwe (22,000 t) and Argentina (18,000 t) are rising; largest reserves are in Chile (9.3 Mt), then Australia (7 Mt) and Argentina (4 Mt); identified resources are largest in Argentina and Bolivia (23 Mt each).
- Cobalt: DR Congo mined about 2,20,000 t of a world total of about 2,90,000 t (roughly three-quarters); Indonesia is second (28,000 t) as a by-product of nickel laterite processing.
- Nickel: Indonesia produced about 2.2 Mt of roughly 3.7 Mt (close to 60%) after banning raw-ore exports to force domestic smelting — a case study of resource nationalism.
- Rare earths: China mined about 2,70,000 t of roughly 3,90,000 t and dominates processing; reserves — China 44 Mt, Brazil 21 Mt, India 6.9 Mt (third largest; mainly monazite beach sands of Kerala, Tamil Nadu, Odisha); Myanmar (31,000 t) is a major supplier of heavy rare earths.
- Natural graphite: China about 1.27 Mt of 1.6 Mt; India produced about 27,800 t with reserves of 8.6 Mt; Mozambique and Madagascar are emerging African sources.
- Copper: Chile is the top miner (about 5.3 Mt), then DR Congo (3.3 Mt); China mines only about 1.8 Mt but refines about 12 Mt — the gap between mining and refining geography is a favourite analytical point.
- Iron ore (usable ore): Australia about 930 Mt, Brazil 440 Mt, China and India about 270 Mt each — India is self-sufficient and an exporter of low-grade fines.
- Energy: largest proven oil reserves — Venezuela (Orinoco heavy oil), Saudi Arabia, Iran, Canada (oil sands); largest gas reserves — Russia, Iran, Qatar (North Field shared with Iran's South Pars); coal reserves — USA, Russia, Australia, China, India.
- Concept: 'resources are not, they become' (Zimmermann) — a stock becomes a resource only with technology, demand and access; PPSC 2021 asked 'Resource: a cultural concept', so cite shale gas (fracking) and lithium (EV batteries) as examples of resources created by technology.

### South Asia and the Indian subcontinent — resource map beyond India

- Pakistan: Reko Diq copper-gold deposit (Chagai, Balochistan) is among the world's largest undeveloped copper-gold finds; Thar coalfield (Sindh) holds large lignite reserves; Sui gas field (Balochistan) was its gas backbone; Indus plains support wheat-cotton-rice farming.
- Afghanistan: Mes Aynak copper (Logar, leased to a Chinese consortium in 2007-08), Hajigak iron ore, and lithium-bearing pegmatites; largely unexploited because of conflict.
- Bangladesh: natural gas fields (Titas, Bibiyana) supply most of its power; Barapukuria coal; Ganga-Brahmaputra delta fertility supports rice-jute, but arsenic in groundwater is a known hazard.
- Nepal and Bhutan: rivers with huge hydropower potential; Bhutan earns a large share of its revenue from selling hydroelectricity to India (Chukha, Tala, Mangdechhu, Punatsangchhu projects).
- Sri Lanka: vein graphite (Bogala, Kahatagaha) prized for purity; ilmenite and monazite beach sands at Pulmoddai; tea and rubber plantations in the wet zone.
- Myanmar: jade, rubies (Mogok), tin-tungsten and rare earths in Kachin; oil and gas in the Irrawaddy basin and offshore (Shwe, Yadana fields supply China and Thailand).
- India's gaps: heavy import dependence for crude oil, natural gas, coking coal, rock phosphate, potash and most critical minerals; the National Critical Mineral Mission (2025) and KABIL's overseas lithium blocks in Argentina's Catamarca province aim to reduce this.
- Punjab link: Punjab has no significant metallic minerals or fossil fuels, so its industry depends on inputs moved from Chota Nagpur and imports through Gujarat ports — a classic 'raw-material-deficit, market-based' location.

### Industrial location theory beyond Weber (thinker — idea — exam use)

- Alfred Weber (1909) — least-cost location using the material index (weight of localised raw material / weight of product); index above 1 pulls industry to raw material (sugar, cement, iron and steel), below 1 to market (beverages, bakeries).
- Edgar Hoover (1948) — transport costs fall with distance (tapering freight) and break-of-bulk points (ports, rail junctions) attract industry — explains refineries at Jamnagar and Paradip and steel at Visakhapatnam.
- August Losch (1954) — maximum-profit (revenue) approach: a firm locates where it can capture the largest market area, giving hexagonal market regions.
- Walter Christaller (1933) — central place theory: services (tertiary activities) are arranged in a hierarchy of settlements with hexagonal market areas based on 'range' and 'threshold' — use it to explain why hospitals, universities and courts cluster in district headquarters such as Ludhiana or Patiala.
- D.M. Smith — spatial margins to profitability: a firm can locate anywhere within a margin where revenue exceeds cost, which leaves room for personal and policy factors.
- Behavioural and institutional factors: subsidies, tax holidays, land banks, single-window clearances, PLI schemes (2020 onward) and special category status (the 2003 package for Himachal and Uttarakhand pulled units away from Punjab).
- Agglomeration and clusters: Alfred Marshall's 'industrial districts' and Michael Porter's 'clusters' (shared skilled labour, suppliers, knowledge spill-overs) explain Ludhiana hosiery, Jalandhar sports goods, Batala foundries and Mandi Gobindgarh steel re-rolling.
- Quaternary/quinary activities (R&D, consultancy, top decision-making) locate by talent, connectivity and quality of life — Bengaluru, Hyderabad, Gurugram; Mohali's IT city is Punjab's attempt.

### Classic industrial regions and comparisons asked by PPSC

- Ruhr (Germany): coalfield-based heavy industry on the Rhine with cheap water transport; declined from the 1960s and restructured into services, logistics and the 'Emscher Park' green regeneration — a model of de-industrialisation management.
- Flanders and the Franco-Belgian coalfield (Lille-Charleroi): textiles, steel and engineering grew on coal and the Scheldt-Antwerp port outlet; PPSC 2018 asked to compare the Ruhr-Flanders 'western triangle' with the Chota Nagpur belt.
- Chota Nagpur (Jharkhand-Odisha-West Bengal): Damodar valley coal plus Singhbhum and Odisha iron ore, manganese and limestone; steel at Jamshedpur (1907), Bokaro, Rourkela, Durgapur; DVC (1948) was modelled on the Tennessee Valley Authority.
- Comparison points: both are mineral-based; Europe's regions benefited from navigable rivers and ports, whereas Chota Nagpur relies on rail to Haldia and Paradip; Ruhr restructured early, while Chota Nagpur still faces displacement, pollution and tribal land issues.
- Other world regions to name in answers: Great Lakes manufacturing belt (USA), Pittsburgh steel, Silicon Valley (high-tech), Pearl River Delta (export manufacturing), Kyushu-Kanto (Japan), Ural-Kuznetsk (Russia).
- Pharmaceutical clusters (PPSC 2026 Mains): Hyderabad (bulk drugs, 'Genome Valley'), Ahmedabad-Vadodara, Baddi (Himachal — formulations, drawn by the 2003 tax package), Goa, Sikkim, Visakhapatnam; India supplies about one-fifth of global generic volumes; challenges — dependence on Chinese active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs), quality-control alerts, PLI for bulk drugs (2020).
- Shifts in Indian industry: sugar moving from UP-Bihar to Maharashtra-Karnataka (higher recovery, longer crushing season), cotton textiles from Mumbai to Surat, Tiruppur and Ludhiana, automobiles clustering in Chennai, Pune-Chakan, Gurugram-Manesar and Sanand.
- Answer tip: pair every factor with a named example and finish with a policy line (industrial corridors such as DMIC, freight corridors, PM Gati Shakti).

### Common traps

- Largest producer is not the same as largest reserve holder — Australia leads lithium mining, Chile leads lithium reserves; China leads rare-earth mining but its reserve share is lower than its output share.
- Venezuela has the largest proven oil reserves, but the USA has been the largest producer — do not mix the two lists.
- Weber's 'material index' above 1 means weight-losing (locate near raw material); students often reverse it.
- Christaller's theory is about services and settlements, not manufacturing.
- India's monazite is valued mainly for thorium and rare earths, and is handled by IREL (India) Ltd under the Atomic Energy framework — do not call it a private mining sector mineral.
- Mediterranean climate has winter, not summer, rainfall; Punjab is Cwa (dry winter), not Cs.
- Ruhr is in Germany on the Rhine; Flanders is in Belgium-France; Lorraine (France) is the iron-ore field — three different regions often merged by mistake.

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** Discuss the factors responsible for the location of secondary sector industries. How has globalisation changed these factors? (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: industrial location as a cost-minimising decision
- Classical factors: raw material, power, labour, market, transport; Weber's least-cost theory
- Examples: iron and steel near Chota Nagpur, sugar in UP/Maharashtra, cotton textiles in Mumbai/Ahmedabad
- Globalisation: falling transport costs, global value chains, footloose industries, policy competition (SEZs, PLI)
- New factors: skilled labour, digital connectivity, ease of doing business, supply-chain resilience
- Punjab example: MSME clusters without minerals
- Conclusion: location now a mix of geography and policy

</details>

**2.** Describe the global distribution of critical minerals and explain why it has become a geopolitical concern for India. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: lithium, cobalt, rare earths, nickel for energy transition
- Distribution: lithium triangle and Australia, cobalt in DR Congo, rare earths processing in China
- Concentration risk and export controls
- India's position: limited reserves, Reasi lithium find, import dependence
- Responses: critical minerals list, KABIL overseas acquisitions, Minerals Security Partnership, recycling
- Conclusion: resource geography now shapes strategic policy

</details>

**3.** How do ocean currents influence the climate and economic activities of coastal regions? Illustrate with examples. (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: currents as heat conveyors
- Warm currents: Gulf Stream keeps NW Europe mild and ports ice-free
- Cold currents: aridity of Atacama and Namib, fog
- Fishing grounds where warm and cold currents meet (Grand Banks, Japan)
- El Nino and Peru's anchovy fishery; navigation
- Conclusion: currents link physical geography to livelihoods

</details>
