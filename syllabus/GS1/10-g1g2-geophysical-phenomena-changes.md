## Short notes

- Earthquakes: energy released at the focus (hypocentre), felt first at the epicentre; P-waves are fastest and pass through solids and liquids; S-waves only through solids (their shadow zone shows a liquid outer core); surface waves cause most damage.
- Measurement: magnitude on the Richter/moment-magnitude scale (energy), intensity on the Modified Mercalli scale (damage).
- Distribution: Circum-Pacific 'Ring of Fire' (most earthquakes and volcanoes), Alpine-Himalayan belt, mid-oceanic ridges.
- India's seismic zonation (IS 1893:2016) uses Zones II-V; Zone V covers the North-East, parts of J&K/Ladakh, Himachal, Uttarakhand, Rann of Kutch and Andaman-Nicobar. BIS's revised IS 1893:2025 (Nov 2025), which added a new Zone VI for the Himalayan arc, was withdrawn in March 2026 after cost and feasibility objections, so the 2016 zoning (Zones II-V) remains in force.
- Tsunami: generated by undersea thrust earthquakes, submarine landslides or volcanic eruptions; waves slow and grow taller in shallow water; 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami (Sumatra-Andaman quake, 26 Dec 2004); 2011 Tohoku tsunami in Japan (Fukushima accident).
- India's Tsunami Early Warning Centre at INCOIS, Hyderabad (operational 2007) also serves the Indian Ocean region.
- Volcanoes: shield (Hawaiian, basaltic, gentle), composite/strato (Fuji, Vesuvius - explosive), caldera, fissure/flood basalts (Deccan Traps); hotspots (Hawaii, Iceland); India's only active volcano is Barren Island (Andamans); Narcondam is dormant.
- Tropical cyclones need sea-surface temperature above about 26-27 degrees C, Coriolis force (so none form within roughly 5 degrees of the equator), low vertical wind shear, a pre-existing low and high humidity.
- Names: hurricanes (Atlantic, NE Pacific), typhoons (NW Pacific), cyclones (Indian Ocean); North Indian Ocean names come from a list of 169 names contributed by 13 WMO/ESCAP Panel member countries (2020); IMD New Delhi is the Regional Specialised Meteorological Centre.
- The Bay of Bengal gets more cyclones than the Arabian Sea, but warming of the Arabian Sea has increased the frequency and intensity of its cyclones in recent decades.
- Temperate cyclones form along fronts in the westerlies; Western Disturbances are their extension bringing winter rain and snow to north-west India.
- Other hazards: landslides and cloudbursts in the Himalaya and Western Ghats (Wayanad, July 2024), glacial lake outburst floods (South Lhonak lake, Sikkim, Oct 2023), urban floods, heatwaves.
- Changes in ice: Himalayan glaciers are retreating; Arctic sea ice is declining; Greenland and Antarctic ice loss (e.g., Thwaites Glacier) add to sea-level rise; IPCC AR6 (2021) found global surface temperature about 1.1 degrees C above 1850-1900 in 2011-2020.
- Changes in water bodies: Aral Sea shrank after Soviet diversion of the Amu Darya and Syr Darya; Lake Chad has shrunk sharply; Indian wetlands lost to encroachment; Ramsar Convention (1971) protects wetlands.
- Changes in flora and fauna: coral bleaching (Great Barrier Reef), upslope and poleward range shifts, invasive species (Lantana, Prosopis juliflora), mangrove loss; IPBES (2019) warned about 1 million species face extinction risk.
- Effects: sea-level rise threatens small island states (Maldives, Tuvalu) and coastal megacities; permafrost thaw releases methane; altered monsoon and river flows threaten food and water security in South Asia.
- Disaster risk reduction: Sendai Framework 2015-2030; India's Disaster Management Act 2005, NDMA, NDRF; India-led Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure (CDRI, 2019).

## Punjab angle

- Under IS 1893:2016 (still in force) Punjab lies in Zones III and IV; the sub-montane north (Pathankot, Gurdaspur, Hoshiarpur, Rupnagar belt) is in Zone IV, while the south-western plains are in Zone III.
- Western Disturbances bring winter rain vital for rabi wheat, but untimely rain and hailstorms in March-April damage the standing crop.
- Floods are Punjab's main hazard: July 2023 floods and the severe Aug-Sept 2025 floods, when all 23 districts were declared flood-affected; agriculture GVA contracted 2.5% in 2025-26 (Punjab Economic Survey 2025-26).
- Retreat of Himalayan glaciers and changing snowfall affect long-term flows of the Sutlej, Beas and Ravi feeding Bhakra, Pong and Ranjit Sagar - core to Punjab's water security.
- Punjab State Disaster Management Authority and district authorities operate under the DM Act 2005.

## Deep dive

### Himalayan and Indian seismicity — timeline (year — event — lesson)

- Structure: from south to north the Himalaya is cut by the Main Frontal Thrust (Shivalik front), Main Boundary Thrust and Main Central Thrust; the Indian plate still moves north at roughly 4-5 cm a year, so strain keeps building along these thrusts.
- 1897 — Shillong (Assam) earthquake, about M8; Richard Oldham's study of it helped identify the Earth's core from seismic records.
- 1905 — Kangra earthquake (Himachal), about M7.8; heavy damage at Kangra and Dharamshala and shaking felt across the Punjab plains, including Lahore and Amritsar — the nearest great quake to present Punjab.
- 1934 — Bihar-Nepal earthquake (M8+); widespread liquefaction in the Gangetic plain showed the danger of thick, water-saturated alluvium.
- 1950 — Assam-Tibet (Medog) earthquake, M8.6, one of the largest continental quakes recorded; it changed river courses in the Brahmaputra basin.
- 1991 Uttarkashi, 1999 Chamoli, 2005 Kashmir (M7.6, severe in Muzaffarabad), 2015 Gorkha, Nepal (M7.8) — repeated reminders of the 'central seismic gap' between the 1905 and 1934 ruptures, where a great quake is considered overdue.
- 1993 Latur (M6.2, stable peninsular shield) and 2001 Bhuj (M7.7, Kutch) — proved that intraplate India is not 'safe'; Bhuj led to the Gujarat State Disaster Management Act 2003 and fed into the national DM Act 2005.
- Punjab relevance: thick alluvium amplifies shaking (site effects), and mid-rise urban buildings in Ludhiana, Jalandhar, Amritsar and Mohali are the main risk; enforcement of IS 1893 and IS 13920 (ductile detailing) is the practical remedy.

### Global geophysical events 2022–2025 to quote with year

- Jan 2022 — Hunga Tonga-Hunga Ha'apai (Tonga): submarine eruption whose pressure wave circled the Earth several times and injected record water vapour into the stratosphere; triggered a Pacific-wide tsunami.
- 6 Feb 2023 — Turkey-Syria earthquakes (M7.8 and M7.5) on the East Anatolian Fault; over 50,000 deaths; India sent NDRF teams under 'Operation Dost'.
- Sep 2023 — Morocco (Al Haouz, High Atlas) earthquake, about M6.8; showed vulnerability of mud-brick mountain villages.
- 1 Jan 2024 — Noto Peninsula earthquake, Japan (M7.5): coastal uplift of several metres permanently changed the shoreline.
- 28 Mar 2025 — Myanmar earthquake (Mw 7.7) on the strike-slip Sagaing Fault near Mandalay; a supershear rupture about 500 km long; over 5,000 deaths including about 100 in Bangkok; India's relief effort was 'Operation Brahma'.
- 30 Jul 2025 — Kamchatka (Russia) megathrust earthquake, Mw 8.8, one of the largest since 2011; Pacific-wide tsunami warnings, but most coasts saw waves of about 1 m or less, while local run-up on Kamchatka reached about 33 m.
- 23 Nov 2025 — Hayli Gubbi shield volcano in Ethiopia's Afar Rift erupted for the first time on record; its ash plume (up to about 14 km) drifted over Arabia to north India, disrupting flights — a rift-valley (divergent boundary) volcano, not a Ring of Fire one.
- Use in answers: one recent event per hazard type, with date, mechanism (plate boundary type) and one governance lesson.

### Cyclones in depth — IMD scale and recent Indian storms

- IMD classification by maximum sustained wind (3-minute average): Depression 31-49 km/h; Deep Depression 50-61; Cyclonic Storm 62-88; Severe Cyclonic Storm 89-117; Very Severe 118-166; Extremely Severe 167-221; Super Cyclonic Storm 222 km/h or more.
- Structure: eye (calm, sinking air), eyewall (strongest winds and rain), spiral rain bands; storm surge is usually the biggest killer, not wind.
- Tropical vs temperate (PPSC 2021 asked a comparison): tropical cyclones form over warm oceans between about 5 and 30 degrees latitude, have no fronts, a calm eye, move east to west with the trades and die over land; temperate cyclones form along fronts at 35-65 degrees, are larger, move west to east with the westerlies, can form over land and sea, and have no eye.
- Recent Indian storms: Amphan (May 2020, super cyclone, Bay of Bengal), Tauktae (May 2021, Arabian Sea, Gujarat), Biparjoy (June 2023, long-lived Arabian Sea storm, Kutch), Michaung (Dec 2023, Chennai floods), Remal (May 2024, Bengal-Bangladesh), Dana (Oct 2024, Odisha), Fengal (Nov-Dec 2024, Tamil Nadu-Puducherry).
- Montha (Oct 2025): formed over the south-east Bay of Bengal on 25 Oct 2025 and crossed the Andhra Pradesh coast near Narasapur around midnight of 28-29 Oct 2025 as a Severe Cyclonic Storm (about 95 km/h).
- Why fewer deaths now: IMD's improved track forecasts, the 2013 Phailin and 2019 Fani evacuations in Odisha (over a million people moved), multipurpose cyclone shelters under the National Cyclone Risk Mitigation Project.
- Rapid intensification over a warmer Arabian Sea (Ockhi 2017, Tauktae 2021) is the new forecasting challenge; Arabian Sea storms now affect Gujarat, Maharashtra and even Oman.

### Hydro-meteorological and mountain hazards (landslides, cloudbursts, GLOFs)

- Cloudburst (PPSC 2026 asked a note): IMD treats rainfall of about 100 mm or more in an hour over a small area (roughly 20-30 sq km) as a cloudburst; caused by orographic lifting of moisture-laden monsoon air, often when monsoon winds meet a western disturbance.
- Examples: Leh (Aug 2010), Kedarnath (June 2013, combined with Chorabari lake burst), Himachal and Kullu-Mandi events in 2023 and 2025; flash floods in steep valleys carry boulders and destroy roads and hydro projects.
- Landslides (PPSC 2021 asked a mitigation strategy): causes — steep slopes, weak fractured rock, heavy rain, toe-cutting by rivers and roads, deforestation, hill-cutting for highways and hydro tunnels; ISRO's Landslide Atlas of India (2023) placed Rudraprayag and Tehri (Uttarakhand) at the top of district-level risk.
- Landslide mitigation: hazard zonation mapping (GSI), slope-stability engineering (retaining walls, rock bolting, drainage), bio-engineering with grasses and shrubs, early-warning rain gauges, building bye-laws, carrying-capacity studies for hill towns (Joshimath land subsidence, Jan 2023).
- GLOF: moraine-dammed lakes formed by glacier retreat burst suddenly — South Lhonak (Sikkim, Oct 2023) destroyed the Teesta-III dam at Chungthang; NDMA's GLOF guidelines (2020) call for lake mapping, early warning and controlled lowering of lake levels.
- Heat waves: IMD declares a heat wave when plains reach at least 40 degrees C and departure from normal is 4.5 degrees or more (or actual 45 degrees C); Punjab and Haryana saw extreme heat in May-June 2024; Heat Action Plans began with Ahmedabad (2013).
- Floods: Punjab's 2023 and 2025 floods came from extreme monsoon rain in the Himachal catchment, high releases from Bhakra and Pong, and breaches of dhussi bundhs (earthen embankments) along the Sutlej, Beas and Ghaggar.

### Changes in ice, water bodies, flora and fauna — data and examples

- Arctic: September sea-ice extent has fallen by roughly 12-13% per decade since satellite records began in 1979; the Arctic is warming about three to four times faster than the global average ('Arctic amplification').
- Himalaya-Hindu Kush: ICIMOD's 2023 assessment warned that glaciers could lose a large share of their volume this century under high emissions; peak meltwater followed by long-term decline threatens the Indus system that feeds Punjab.
- Antarctica and Greenland: ice-shelf collapse (Larsen B, 2002) and thinning of Thwaites ('Doomsday glacier') raise sea-level risk; Greenland is now the largest single land-ice contributor to sea-level rise.
- Coral reefs: the fourth global coral bleaching event was confirmed by NOAA in April 2024 after record ocean heat in 2023-24; Indian reefs in the Gulf of Mannar, Lakshadweep and Andamans were affected.
- Shrinking water bodies: Aral Sea (Soviet cotton irrigation), Lake Chad, Lake Urmia (Iran), Lake Poopo (Bolivia, dried up in 2015), Dead Sea (falling about 1 m a year); in India, Bengaluru and Hyderabad lakes lost to encroachment and Loktak's phumdis degraded.
- Flora and fauna shifts: treeline and alpine plants moving upslope in the Himalaya; earlier flowering of rhododendron; invasive species — Lantana camara, Prosopis juliflora, Parthenium, water hyacinth, African catfish; reintroduction of cheetahs at Kuno (Sept 2022) shows active restoration.
- Wetlands: the Ramsar list in India crossed 90 sites in 2025; Punjab's six Ramsar sites (Harike, Kanjli, Ropar, Keshopur-Miani, Beas Conservation Reserve, Nangal) are wintering grounds on the Central Asian Flyway.
- Effects chain for answers: warming leads to ice loss, then sea-level rise and altered river regimes, then food, water and migration stress — always end with mitigation (net-zero 2070 target for India) and adaptation (early warning, resilient infrastructure, CDRI).

### What PPSC has asked and common traps

- Asked: brief notes on Fohn, depression, tsunami and the Richter scale (2018); spatial distribution of tropical cyclones (2018); tropical vs temperate cyclones and coral bleaching (2021); landslide mitigation (2021); the revised seismic zonation map of Nov 2025, glaciers and ice caps, and cloudbursts (2026).
- Fohn is a warm, dry wind descending the leeward side of the Alps (Chinook in the Rockies); it melts snow — do not confuse it with the cold Bora or Mistral.
- The Richter scale is logarithmic: each whole number is about 10 times the wave amplitude and about 32 times the energy; modern agencies report moment magnitude (Mw).
- Magnitude measures energy (one value per quake); intensity (Modified Mercalli, I to XII) varies from place to place.
- A tsunami is not a tidal wave; it has a very long wavelength and travels at jet-plane speed in the deep ocean, where it is barely noticeable.
- Tropical cyclones rotate anticlockwise in the northern hemisphere and clockwise in the southern hemisphere.
- Hayli Gubbi (2025) is on a divergent rift; the Ring of Fire is mostly convergent margins — use the right plate setting.

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** Explain the conditions necessary for the formation of tropical cyclones. Why does the Bay of Bengal experience more cyclones than the Arabian Sea, and what is changing? (250 words)

*Like a real PPSC question: [Mains 2018 · GS1 Q10(b)](../../past-papers/mains_2018_GS1.md#q10-b)*

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: definition and naming of tropical cyclones
- Conditions: warm SST, Coriolis force, low wind shear, pre-existing disturbance, moisture
- Bay of Bengal: warmer, shallower, low-salinity surface layer, remnants of Pacific systems, funnel-shaped coast
- Arabian Sea: cooler historically, but recent warming raising frequency and intensity
- Impacts and India's early-warning success (IMD, evacuation in Odisha)
- Conclusion: climate change demands stronger coastal resilience

</details>

**2.** Discuss the causes and consequences of the melting of glaciers and ice caps, with special reference to India. (250 words)

*Like a real PPSC question: [Mains 2026 · GS1 Q16](../../past-papers/mains_2026_GS1.md#q16)*

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: cryosphere as climate indicator; IPCC findings
- Causes: global warming, black carbon, reduced snowfall
- Global consequences: sea-level rise, ocean circulation changes, permafrost methane
- Indian consequences: Himalayan glacier retreat, GLOFs (South Lhonak 2023), river-flow changes, coastal flooding
- Punjab angle: Sutlej-Beas-Ravi flows and reservoir storage
- Way forward: monitoring, GLOF early warning, mitigation, adaptation
- Conclusion: water security is climate security

</details>

**3.** How prepared is India to deal with earthquakes and tsunamis? Suggest measures to improve resilience. (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: high seismic exposure; 2001 Bhuj and 2004 tsunami lessons
- Institutions: NDMA, NDRF, INCOIS warning centre, seismic zonation and building codes
- Gaps: code enforcement, old building stock, urban density, awareness
- Measures: retrofitting, land-use zoning, drills, coastal shelterbelts and mangroves
- Conclusion: shift from relief to risk reduction (Sendai)

</details>
