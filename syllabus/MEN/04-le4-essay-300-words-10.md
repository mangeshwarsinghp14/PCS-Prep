# Essay, 300 words (10)

**Paper 2 — English, 10+2 standard** › English · Subject: Language papers (Punjabi & English) · Priority: Medium

## Short notes

- Short essay of about 300 words on a general topic; plan for 5 paragraphs: introduction (50), three body paragraphs (60-70 each), conclusion (50).
- Spend 3-4 minutes planning: jot 6-8 points, group them into 3 themes, and decide the opening and closing lines.
- Introduction: define or contextualise the topic and state your line of argument in one sentence.
- Body: one idea per paragraph, each opening with a clear topic sentence followed by explanation and one example.
- Use dimensions for coverage: social, economic, political, environmental, ethical, technological (choose the relevant 3).
- Conclusion: summarise and give a balanced, forward-looking way out; do not introduce a new argument.
- Language: simple, correct sentences; vary sentence length; use linking words (moreover, however, therefore, as a result).
- Avoid: slang, over-long quotations, invented statistics, one-sided rants, and spelling errors in key words.
- If using data, give only what you are sure of and name the year or source generally (e.g. Census 2011).
- Title: write the given topic as the heading; do not change it.
- Common topics at 10+2 level: environment, technology and youth, role of women, value of education, discipline, a festival, social media, drug abuse.
- Examiners value relevance, organisation, clear expression and correct grammar more than ornate vocabulary.
- Count lines in your answer sheet once (words per line) so you can judge 300 words without counting every word.
- Practise one essay a week with a 25-minute limit.

## Punjab angle

- Punjab examples work well: Green Revolution and its costs, Guru Nanak Dev ji's message of equality, langar and seva, migration abroad, drug abuse.
- Use verified Punjab facts only, e.g. 23 districts, 117 assembly seats, the 2025 floods that cut agricultural output (Economic Survey 2025-26: agriculture -2.5%).
- Mention state initiatives only by names you are sure of; a general reference ('state campaigns against drug abuse') is safer than a wrong name.

## How PPSC asks it

A 10-mark, 300-word general essay rewards structure, relevance and clean language. Learn a reusable 5-paragraph template and keep 2-3 examples ready for common themes.

## Deep dive

### Introduction techniques (pick one, 40–50 words)

- Fact/data hook: 'Punjab, with about 1.5% of India's area, has long been a leading contributor of wheat and rice to the central pool — yet its groundwater is falling every year.'
- Definition hook: 'Discipline is not blind obedience; it is the habit of doing the right thing at the right time without being told.'
- Contrast hook: 'The smartphone that connects a village student to the world can also isolate her from her own family.'
- Anecdote hook (2 lines only): a short, realistic scene — a farmer at a mandi, a girl cycling to school.
- Question hook: 'Can a state that feeds the nation afford to let its youth go hungry for jobs?'
- End the introduction with a one-line thesis that tells the examiner your direction.

### Body paragraph engine (PEEL)

- Point — a topic sentence that states one idea.
- Explain — 2–3 sentences on why or how.
- Example — one concrete case: Punjab, India or world (scheme, event, statistic you are sure of).
- Link — a closing sentence tying back to the thesis.
- Use dimensions to find points quickly: social, economic, political, environmental, technological, ethical, legal.
- For 'advantages and disadvantages' topics, give two body paragraphs to the side you finally support and one to the other side.

### Linkers and upgrades

- Adding: moreover, furthermore, in addition, equally important.
- Contrasting: however, nevertheless, on the other hand, conversely.
- Cause/effect: consequently, as a result, hence, therefore.
- Example: for instance, a case in point is, this is evident in.
- Concluding: in sum, ultimately, on balance, taken together.
- Upgrades: 'very important' → crucial/vital; 'big problem' → serious challenge; 'good effect' → positive impact; 'a lot of' → considerable/substantial.

### Outline bank — ten common 10+2-level topics

- Social media and youth: connection and learning; misinformation and addiction; cyber safety; digital literacy; balanced use.
- Drug abuse among the young: causes (peer pressure, unemployment, supply); effects (health, family, crime); remedies (treatment, sports, awareness, enforcement).
- Value of education / girls' education: individual dignity, economic gains, health and smaller families; barriers; schemes and community role.
- Environment and pollution: air (stubble, vehicles), water, plastic; laws and missions; individual habits.
- Water crisis: falling water table, wasteful irrigation, urban leakage; drip irrigation, crop diversification, rainwater harvesting.
- Role of women in society: work, leadership, safety, panchayat reservation, changing attitudes.
- Discipline / punctuality / honesty: definition, examples, effect on success and society.
- A festival or fair of Punjab (Baisakhi, Maghi, Hola Mohalla): history, scenes, community spirit, changes over time.
- Unemployment and migration abroad: push and pull factors, costs to families, skill development and local opportunities.
- Science/technology in daily life: health, farming, communication, banking; dependence and ethical limits.

### Conclusion techniques (40–50 words)

- Summary + way forward: restate the thesis in fresh words and give 2–3 practical steps.
- Return to the opening image (the farmer, the student) to close the circle.
- Shared-responsibility ending: government, community and individual each have a role.
- Hopeful but realistic: avoid slogans and promises that nothing will ever go wrong.
- Never introduce a new argument or statistic in the conclusion.
- Last line short and memorable, not a borrowed quotation you are unsure of.

### Common faults and timing

- Changing or rephrasing the given title — write it exactly as set.
- Memorised generic paragraphs that do not fit the topic; examiners spot them.
- Invented statistics or wrongly attributed quotations — use verified facts with a year or speak generally.
- Slang, abbreviations (govt., etc.) and exclamation marks in formal essays.
- Paragraphs of one sentence or one giant paragraph; aim for 5 paragraphs of 50–70 words.
- Timing: 4 minutes planning, 18 writing, 3 revising — about 25 minutes for 300 words.

## Mains practice questions (7)

**1.** Write an essay of about 300 words on 'Social Media and Youth'.

- Intro: social media as the main source of news, entertainment and contact for young people; it can empower or distract.
- Benefits: learning resources, career networking, voice for social causes, small online businesses.
- Harms: addiction and lost study time, misinformation, cyberbullying, comparison and anxiety.
- Safety: privacy settings, fact-checking before sharing, reporting abuse; role of parents and schools.

**2.** Write an essay of about 300 words on 'Save Water, Save Life'.

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: water as the basis of life and farming; Punjab's name itself refers to rivers, yet groundwater is falling.
- Causes of scarcity: over-pumping, water-intensive cropping, wastage in homes, pollution of rivers.
- Effects: deeper tubewells, higher costs for farmers, health risks from polluted water.
- Solutions: drip and sprinkler irrigation, crop diversification, rainwater harvesting, fixing leaks, reuse of treated water.
- Conclusion: every drop saved today secures the future; citizen action plus government policy.

</details>

**3.** Write an essay of about 300 words on 'The Importance of Discipline in Life'.

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: define discipline as self-control and following rules willingly.
- In student life: time management, regular study, healthy habits.
- In public life and work: punctuality, integrity, teamwork; examples from the armed forces and sports.
- Discipline vs rigidity: it should come from understanding, not fear.
- Conclusion: discipline turns ability into achievement for both individuals and nations.

</details>

**4.** Write an essay of about 300 words on: 'Technology is a good servant but a bad master.'

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Introduction: explain the saying — technology helps when humans control its use, and harms when it begins to control their time, choices and minds.
- As a servant: online services and digital payments save time; telemedicine and weather alerts reach villages; farm machinery and drip irrigation raise efficiency.
- As a master: smartphone addiction, shrinking attention spans, cyber fraud, fake news, loss of privacy; dependence that weakens basic skills (calculation, memory, direction).
- Social side: the digital divide between rural and urban, young and old; families sitting together but each on a screen.
- Way forward: digital literacy, screen-time discipline, strong data-protection rules, using technology for learning and work rather than mere entertainment.
- Conclusion: the tool is neutral; the user's judgement decides whether it serves or rules — end with a balanced line.
- Plan: 5 paragraphs (intro 50, three body paragraphs of 60–70, conclusion 50); one concrete example per paragraph.

</details>

**5.** Write an essay of about 300 words on: 'Freedom without responsibility is a danger to democracy.'

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Introduction: democracy guarantees freedoms (speech, movement, association, vote), but every right carries a matching duty; Article 51A lists citizens' fundamental duties.
- Freedom of speech vs hate speech, rumours and fake news on social media that can incite violence.
- Right to protest vs blocking roads, damaging public property and harming fellow citizens' rights.
- Right to vote vs selling votes or voting on caste and religious lines, which weakens accountability.
- Responsibility of institutions too: free media must be fair; leaders must respect dissent; the state must not misuse 'responsibility' as an excuse to curb liberty.
- Way forward: civic education in schools, fact-checking habits, peaceful and lawful protest, respect for opposing views.
- Conclusion: freedom and responsibility are two wheels of the same cart; one without the other overturns democracy.

</details>

**6.** Write an essay of about 300 words on: 'Climate change and the farmer.'

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Introduction: farming depends directly on weather, so the farmer is the first to feel climate change — uneven rain, heatwaves, floods and new pests.
- Effects: heat at grain-filling time can reduce wheat yields; erratic monsoon and floods (as in Punjab in 2025) destroy standing crops; pest attacks on cotton; falling groundwater adds to stress.
- Economic side: rising costs, crop loss, debt; small and marginal farmers have the least cushion.
- Adaptation: heat-tolerant and short-duration varieties, crop diversification, direct seeding of rice, micro-irrigation, weather advisories on mobile phones, crop insurance that pays on time.
- Mitigation by farmers: avoiding stubble burning, efficient fertiliser use, agroforestry; they can be part of the solution, not only victims.
- Role of government and research: farm universities, extension services, assured procurement for alternative crops.
- Conclusion: protecting the farmer from climate risks is protecting national food security — end on a practical, hopeful note.

</details>

**7.** Write an essay of about 300 words on: 'Health is wealth: the challenge of lifestyle diseases.'

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Introduction: lifestyle diseases — diabetes, high blood pressure, heart disease, obesity — are now common even among the young; health is the foundation of every other kind of wealth.
- Causes: sedentary work, fast food high in sugar and fat, lack of sleep, stress, tobacco and alcohol, too much screen time and too little physical activity.
- Costs: long treatment and medicine bills, loss of working years, burden on families and on public health services.
- Prevention is cheaper than cure: regular exercise, traditional balanced diet, yoga and sports, periodic check-ups for sugar and blood pressure.
- Role of society and state: playgrounds and parks, school physical education, food labelling, screening camps at health centres, awareness campaigns.
- Personal discipline: small daily habits (walking, less sugar, enough sleep) matter more than occasional resolutions.
- Conclusion: a healthy citizen is a productive citizen; investing in prevention is the best wealth a nation can build.

</details>
