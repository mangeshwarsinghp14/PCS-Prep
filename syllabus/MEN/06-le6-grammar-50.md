## Short notes

- Grammar topics usually asked at 10+2 level: parts of speech, tenses, subject-verb agreement, articles, prepositions, active-passive voice, direct-indirect speech, transformation of sentences, clauses, modals, conjunctions, question tags, error correction, idioms and phrases, one-word substitution, synonyms-antonyms, homophones, phrasal verbs, punctuation.
- Tenses: present perfect is not used with a past time adverb: 'I saw him yesterday' (not 'have seen'). Use 'since' with a point of time, 'for' with a period: since 2020, for six years.
- Subject-verb agreement: 'Each of the students has a book'; 'The committee has submitted its report' (acting as one unit); 'Bread and butter is my breakfast' (one idea).
- Articles: 'an' before a vowel sound (an hour, an MLA), 'a' before a consonant sound (a university, a one-rupee coin); 'the' with unique things, rivers and superlatives (the Sutlej, the best).
- Prepositions: comply with, abide by, superior to, prefer tea to coffee, senior to, different from, divide among (many) / between (two), angry with a person / at a thing.
- Active-passive: object becomes subject, verb becomes be + past participle. 'The DC inaugurated the camp' = 'The camp was inaugurated by the DC.' Imperative: 'Close the door' = 'Let the door be closed.'
- Direct-indirect: shift tense back one step if the reporting verb is past (is to was, will to would, has to had); change pronouns and time words (now to then, today to that day, tomorrow to the next day).
- Example: He said, 'I am going to Amritsar tomorrow.' = He said that he was going to Amritsar the next day. Universal truths keep present tense.
- Transformation: simple-compound-complex. 'Being ill, he stayed home' (simple) = 'He was ill, so he stayed home' (compound) = 'As he was ill, he stayed home' (complex).
- Degrees of comparison: 'Mount Everest is the highest peak' = 'No other peak is as high as Mount Everest' = 'Mount Everest is higher than any other peak.'
- Question tags: positive statement takes negative tag and vice versa: 'You are coming, aren't you?' 'I am right, aren't I?' 'Let's go, shall we?'
- Modals: must (obligation/strong inference), should (advice/duty), may (permission/possibility), might (weaker possibility), could (past ability/polite request).
- Idioms: 'a bolt from the blue' (sudden shock), 'to bell the cat' (to take a risk for others), 'to burn the midnight oil' (to study late), 'a white elephant' (costly but useless).
- One-word substitution: one who cannot be corrected = incorrigible; a speech made without preparation = extempore; government by officials = bureaucracy; one who knows everything = omniscient.
- Homophones and confusables: principal/principle, stationary/stationery, complement/compliment, affect (verb)/effect (noun), lose/loose.
- Phrasal verbs: call off (cancel), put off (postpone), look into (investigate), give up (abandon), carry out (execute), bring up (raise).
- Punctuation: apostrophe for possession (the officer's file, the officers' files), not for plurals; 'its' (possessive) vs 'it's' (it is).
- Common errors: 'one of my friend' (friends), 'discuss about' (discuss), 'return back' (return), 'cousin brother' (cousin), 'more better' (better).

## Punjab angle

- Typical Indian-English errors examiners look for are common in Punjab too: 'cousin brother', 'prepone' in formal writing, 'discuss about', and missing articles.
- Practise transformation and narration with Punjab-themed sentences (e.g. 'The Deputy Commissioner said that the camp would be held in Bathinda') to combine revision with GS.

## How PPSC asks it

Section B (50 marks) is the most scoring part: short items on correction, voice, narration, transformation, idioms, one-word substitutes and usage. Accuracy is everything; learn rules with one model example each and practise daily drills.

## Deep dive

### Subject–verb agreement — extended rule list

- Two subjects joined by 'and' take a plural verb, unless they name one person/idea: 'The secretary and treasurer is absent' (one person).
- 'Either…or', 'neither…nor', 'not only…but also': the verb agrees with the nearer subject — 'Neither the officer nor the clerks were present.'
- 'As well as', 'along with', 'together with', 'in addition to' do not change the subject: 'The DC, along with his staff, was present.'
- 'Each', 'every', 'either', 'neither', 'everyone', 'somebody' take singular verbs: 'Every boy and girl was given a book.'
- 'The number of' + plural noun → singular verb; 'A number of' → plural verb: 'A number of farmers have protested.'
- Collective nouns: singular when acting as a unit (The jury has decided), plural when members act individually (The jury are divided).
- Nouns plural in form but singular in sense take singular verbs: news, mathematics, economics (as a subject), measles; 'The news is good.'
- Always plural: scissors, trousers, cattle, police, people: 'The police have arrested two men.'
- Amounts as a single quantity take singular verbs: 'Ten thousand rupees is a large sum'; 'Five kilometres is a long walk.'
- Titles of books and names of countries take singular verbs: 'The United States has…'; 'Great Expectations is a novel.'
- 'One of the + plural noun' → singular verb ('One of my friends is a doctor'), but 'one of those who + plural verb' ('He is one of those who work hard').

### Tense traps examiners use

- No future tense in clauses of time/condition: 'I will call you when he comes' (not 'will come'); 'If it rains, we will stay.'
- Past perfect for the earlier of two past actions: 'The train had left before we reached the station.'
- Stative verbs (know, believe, love, own, contain) are rarely continuous: 'I know him' (not 'am knowing'); 'I have a car' (not 'am having a car').
- 'Since' needs the present perfect (or perfect continuous) in the main clause: 'He has lived here since 2015.'
- 'It is time' / 'It is high time' + past simple: 'It is high time we acted.'
- 'Would rather' + past for another person: 'I would rather you stayed.'
- Sequence of tenses: a past main verb takes past in the subordinate clause: 'He said that he was tired' — except universal truths ('The teacher said that the earth revolves around the sun').
- 'Hardly/scarcely…when' and 'no sooner…than' take past perfect with inversion: 'No sooner had he arrived than it began to rain.'
- Unreal conditions: 'If I were the DC, I would…' (were for all persons); third conditional: 'If he had studied, he would have passed.'
- 'Used to' (past habit) vs 'be used to' + -ing (accustomed): 'I am used to working late.'

### Voice — beyond the basic pattern

- Modals: 'You must obey the rules' → 'The rules must be obeyed (by you).'
- Continuous: 'They are repairing the road' → 'The road is being repaired.' (perfect continuous tenses are not normally changed)
- Questions: 'Who wrote this letter?' → 'By whom was this letter written?'; 'Did he sign the file?' → 'Was the file signed by him?'
- Two objects: 'The officer gave me a certificate' → 'I was given a certificate' / 'A certificate was given to me.'
- Imperative: 'Shut the gate' → 'Let the gate be shut'; requests: 'Please help me' → 'You are requested to help me.'
- Reporting verbs: 'People say that he is honest' → 'It is said that he is honest' / 'He is said to be honest.'
- Prepositional verbs keep the preposition: 'They laughed at him' → 'He was laughed at.'
- Intransitive verbs have no passive: 'He sleeps' cannot be changed.
- Agent omitted when obvious or unknown: 'The bridge was built in 1960.'

### Narration — question, command and exclamation

- Yes/no question: He said to me, 'Are you ready?' → He asked me if/whether I was ready.
- Wh-question: She said, 'Where do you live?' → She asked where I lived (statement word order, no question mark).
- Command: The officer said to the peon, 'Bring the file.' → The officer ordered the peon to bring the file.
- Negative command: 'Don't make a noise,' said the teacher. → The teacher told us not to make a noise / The teacher forbade us to make a noise.
- Request: He said, 'Please lend me your pen.' → He requested me to lend him my pen.
- Exclamation: She said, 'What a beautiful sight!' → She exclaimed that it was a very beautiful sight.
- Wishes/greetings: He said, 'Good morning, sir.' → He wished the officer good morning; 'May you live long!' → She prayed that he might live long.
- Suggestions with 'let's': He said, 'Let's go home.' → He suggested that we should go home / suggested going home.
- Time and place words: now→then, here→there, this→that, today→that day, yesterday→the previous day, tomorrow→the next day, ago→before.

### Sentence transformation patterns

- Too…to ↔ so…that…not: 'He is too weak to walk' → 'He is so weak that he cannot walk.'
- Unless ↔ if…not: 'Unless you work hard, you will fail' → 'If you do not work hard, you will fail.'
- As soon as ↔ no sooner…than: 'As soon as the bell rang, the students left' → 'No sooner had the bell rung than the students left.'
- Affirmative ↔ negative: 'He is always punctual' → 'He is never late'; 'Only Ravi passed' → 'None but Ravi passed.'
- Interrogative ↔ assertive (rhetorical): 'Who does not love his country?' → 'Everyone loves his country.'
- Exclamatory ↔ assertive: 'How kind you are!' → 'You are very kind.'
- Simple ↔ complex: 'I know the place of his birth' → 'I know where he was born'; 'On seeing the police, the thief ran away' → 'When the thief saw the police, he ran away.'
- Complex ↔ compound: 'Though he was poor, he was honest' → 'He was poor but honest' / 'He was poor, yet he was honest.'
- Comparatives: 'Few cities are as old as Amritsar' → 'Amritsar is older than most other cities' → 'Amritsar is one of the oldest cities.'

### Common Indian-English errors (wrong → right)

- discuss about the issue → discuss the issue; return back → return; cope up with → cope with.
- one of my friend → one of my friends; each of the students have → each of the students has.
- I am having two brothers → I have two brothers; he is knowing → he knows.
- despite of → despite / in spite of; senior than → senior to; prefer tea than coffee → prefer tea to coffee.
- I did not went → I did not go; he did not came → he did not come.
- pass out (graduate) → graduate (in British usage 'pass out' means to faint, except for military passing-out parades).
- an university → a university; a hour → an hour; the both → both.
- He has come yesterday → He came yesterday; I am here since morning → I have been here since morning.
- Lay/lie: 'lay' takes an object (lay the file on the table); 'lie' does not (I lie down); past of lie is 'lay', of lay is 'laid'.
- Kindly revert → please reply; the needful → name the action.

### Vocabulary block — idioms, phrasal verbs, one-word substitutes, confusables

- Idioms: a red-letter day (memorable day); at the eleventh hour (at the last moment); to beat about the bush (avoid the point); a wild-goose chase (useless search); to turn over a new leaf (reform); to call a spade a spade (speak plainly); in the same boat (in the same difficulty); to burn one's boats (leave no way back); the lion's share (largest part); to throw cold water on (discourage).
- Phrasal verbs: call off (cancel), put off (postpone), look into (investigate), carry out (execute), give in (yield), break out (start suddenly — war, fire, epidemic), bring up (raise; mention), set up (establish), look down upon (despise), make up for (compensate).
- One-word substitutes: one who knows everything — omniscient; government by officials — bureaucracy; rule by a few — oligarchy; that cannot be read — illegible; that cannot be corrected — incorrigible; a person who is a loyal supporter of his country — patriot; one who abstains from alcohol — teetotaller; a life history written by oneself — autobiography; that which cannot be avoided — inevitable; a speech made without preparation — extempore.
- Confusables: principal (head, main) / principle (rule); stationary (not moving) / stationery (paper items); complement (completes) / compliment (praise); affect (verb) / effect (noun; verb = bring about); council (body) / counsel (advice).
- More confusables: eminent (famous) / imminent (about to happen); adverse (unfavourable) / averse (unwilling); continual (repeated) / continuous (unbroken); loose (not tight) / lose (misplace); elicit (draw out) / illicit (illegal).
- Learn each in a sentence of your own; marks come from correct usage, not from memorised meanings alone.

### Punctuation and capital letters

- Comma before a coordinating conjunction joining two independent clauses: 'The rain stopped, and the farmers returned to the fields.'
- No comma between a subject and its verb ('The officers who came late, were warned' is wrong).
- Commas around non-defining clauses only: 'My brother, who lives in Patiala, is a doctor' (one brother); no commas for defining clauses: 'Students who study regularly pass easily.'
- Semicolon joins closely related independent clauses: 'The file was ready; the officer signed it.' Colon introduces a list or explanation.
- Apostrophe: it's = it is; its = possessive; the farmers' union (plural possessive); children's books.
- Capitals for proper nouns, titles before names (Deputy Commissioner Kaur), days, months, languages, religions — not for seasons (winter) or directions unless part of a name (south Punjab vs South Africa).
- Inverted commas for direct speech; full stop inside the closing quotation mark in standard usage.

### Section B map — published topic list vs the 2021 paper

- Commonly published PPSC English syllabus for Section B (50 marks): correcting sentences 5, active-passive 5, direct-indirect 5, tenses 5, joining sentences with conjunctions 5, prepositions 5, punctuating a paragraph 10, synonyms-antonyms 5, correct verb forms 5.
- What the PCS-2020 Mains paper (held 2021) actually set: one-word substitution 5; same word as noun and verb 5; synonyms AND antonyms 5; idioms in sentences 5; narration 5 (including indirect to direct); voice 5; 'do as directed' 5; punctuate a dialogue paragraph 5; correct ten sentences 10.
- Lesson: the paper follows the broad list but adds vocabulary items (one-word, idioms, noun/verb use), so prepare both grammar rules and a vocabulary bank.
- Every item is 5 marks of five sub-parts (the correction item had ten sentences for 10); there are no options — each answer is written in full, so a half-correct sentence earns nothing.
- Sentences in the paper are general-English, not Punjab-specific (shirt, guitar, wedding, spectacles), at roughly Class 12 to graduate level; words like 'fecund', 'reticent', 'benediction' show the vocabulary level.
- Time plan: 45–50 minutes; write the full converted or corrected sentence each time, never only the changed word.
- 'Do as directed' covered noun-to-verb change (make an analysis → analyse), removing gender bias (A citizen … his → Citizens … their), complex-to-simple, combining with a connective and question tags — keep a short drill for each.

### Articles and determiners — rules that decide the blank

- Sound, not spelling: an hour, an honest officer, an MLA, an SDM, an FIR; a university, a European, a one-rupee coin, a useful scheme.
- First mention 'a/an', later mention 'the': 'A farmer came to the office. The farmer wanted a copy of his fard.'
- 'The' with unique things and superlatives (the sun, the best district), rivers, seas and ranges (the Ravi, the Arabian Sea, the Shivaliks), plural country names (the Netherlands, the USA), newspapers (The Tribune), and adjectives used as class nouns (the poor, the elderly).
- No article: proper names of persons, cities, lakes and single peaks (Ludhiana, Dal Lake, Mount Everest), languages (Punjabi is…), meals (after lunch), games (plays hockey), and abstract or uncountable nouns used generally (Honesty is the best policy; Water is precious).
- Institutions used for their purpose take no article: go to school, in hospital (as a patient), to court (as a party); with 'the' they mean the building: 'He went to the school to meet the principal.'
- 'Few' (hardly any) vs 'a few' (some); 'little' vs 'a little': 'A few officers attended' is positive, 'Few officers attended' is negative.
- 'Each' (one by one) and 'every' (all, as a group) take singular nouns and verbs; 'either/neither' are for two only; 'much' with uncountables, 'many' with countables (much money, many rupees).
- Trap list: 'the both' → both; 'a news' → a piece of news; 'an useful' → a useful; 'the nature' (meaning the natural world) → nature.

### Prepositions — high-frequency fixed pairs

- Time: at 5 pm / at night; on Monday / on 15 August; in May / in 2026 / in the morning; since + point of time, for + length of time; by (not later than) vs until (up to): 'Submit it by Friday'; 'The office stays open until 5.'
- Place: at the gate / at Ludhiana bus stand (a point), in Punjab / in the room (inside an area), on the table / on the wall (surface); into for movement inside ('walked into the hall').
- Verbs: comply with, abide by, insist on, object to, apply for (a post) / apply to (a person), consist of, deprive of, refrain from, prevent from, dispose of, part with.
- Adjectives: afraid of, aware of, capable of, fond of, proud of; good at, angry with (a person) / at (a thing), married to, similar to, different from, responsible to (a person) / for (a duty).
- Nouns: access to, cure for, desire for, means of, need for, respect for, solution to (a problem), key to (success).
- Legal and office phrases: accused of / charged with, in accordance with, in charge of, on behalf of, with reference to, in lieu of, by order of.
- Words that need no preposition: discuss (not discuss about), enter (the room), reach (Amritsar), order (a file), resemble (his father), attack (the post), request (the DC) — adding one is a standard 'error spotting' item.
- Between (two) / among (more than two); beside (next to) / besides (in addition to); in time (early enough) / on time (punctual).

### Joining sentences, noun–verb use and 'do as directed'

- Addition: and, both … and, not only … but also, as well as — 'He is honest. He is hardworking.' → 'He is not only honest but also hardworking.'
- Contrast: but, yet, although/though (no 'but' after it), in spite of / despite + noun or -ing: 'Although it rained, the camp went on' = 'Despite the rain, the camp went on.'
- Cause and result: because, as, since, so, therefore, so … that: 'He was ill. He could not attend.' → 'He was so ill that he could not attend.'
- Condition: if, unless (= if not; never add 'not' after unless), provided that, in case.
- Relative pronouns: who (persons), which (things), whose (possession), where (place): 'This is the village. My grandfather was born here.' → 'This is the village where my grandfather was born.'
- Participle (simple sentence): 'He finished the file. He went home.' → 'Having finished the file, he went home.' Keep the same subject for both actions, or the participle 'dangles'.
- Noun and verb use (2021 item): write two separate sentences and label them — 'Counter (n): Pay at the counter. Counter (v): She countered his argument with data.' Other pairs: record, present, conduct, object, permit (stress shifts: REcord n / reCORD v).
- Gender-neutral rewriting: change to plural (Citizens … their taxes) or remove the pronoun (A citizen pays taxes promptly); avoid 'his/her' repeated many times.
- Question tags: affirmative → negative tag and vice versa; 'I am …, aren't I?'; 'Let's …, shall we?'; imperatives take 'will you?'; 'should' keeps 'shouldn't you?'; 'nobody/never' sentences are negative, so the tag is positive ('Nobody came, did they?').

### Reverse narration and punctuation of a paragraph

- PPSC also asks indirect → direct (2021): restore the reporting verb 'said (to)', put the actual words in inverted commas, move tense one step forward and restore pronouns and time words: 'The gardener told his assistant to water the plants properly.' → The gardener said to his assistant, "Water the plants properly."
- Advice/order verbs in indirect speech (advised, ordered, requested, forbade) become imperatives in direct speech; 'asked if/whether' becomes a yes/no question with a question mark.
- Mixed direct speech (question + statement + exclamation) is reported part by part with fresh verbs: 'asked … and added that …', 'exclaimed that …', 'warned them that …'.
- Punctuating a paragraph: capital letter at every sentence start, for 'I', names, days and titles; full stop or question mark at every sentence end — count your sentences and your end marks, they must match.
- Direct speech: comma before the opening inverted comma ('Atima said, "…"'), capital letter for the first spoken word, the end mark inside the closing inverted comma, and a new paragraph for each new speaker.
- Commas after introductory words (Well, However, Yes,), around non-defining clauses (Atima, who was not involved, said …), between items in a list, and before a direct address (Come here, Ravi.).
- Apostrophes: contractions (didn't, it's) and possession (the officer's file, the farmers' union); no apostrophe in possessive 'its' or 'yours'.
- Exclamation mark only for real exclamations ('What a relief!'), never doubled; semicolon to join two closely related complete sentences.

### How marks are lost in the English grammar section

- Writing only the corrected word ('has') instead of the full corrected sentence; examiners mark the sentence.
- Correcting one error but creating another, e.g. fixing the verb and dropping the article; read the new sentence once more aloud in your head.
- Changing the meaning while changing voice or narration (tense jump, wrong pronoun); voice and narration must preserve tense and meaning exactly.
- Missing the reporting-verb change: 'said to' must become asked/ordered/requested/advised according to the sentence type.
- Forcing a passive on intransitive verbs ('He sleeps' has no passive) or adding 'by them/by people' when the agent is unknown.
- For synonyms/antonyms, giving a word of another part of speech (abridge → 'short' instead of 'shorten'); match verb with verb, adjective with adjective.
- For idioms, a sentence that does not show the meaning ('He kicked the bucket.') — add context: 'The old landlord kicked the bucket last winter, and his sons divided the land.'
- Careless capitals and spelling in the answer itself (recieve, seperate, untill, goverment) — each slip can cost the sub-part.
- Fix routine: write the question label, the full answer sentence, and underline the changed part; keep 5 minutes to check verb agreement and articles in every answer.

## Practice MCQs (40)

**1.** Spot the error: 'The number of applicants have increased this year.'

(a) 'applicants' should be 'applicant'  
(b) No error  
(c) 'have' should be 'has'  
(d) 'number' should be 'numbers'

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) 'have' should be 'has'** — 'The number of' + plural noun is a single figure, so it takes a singular verb: 'The number of applicants has increased.' Do not confuse it with 'A number of applicants have applied', where 'a number of' means 'many' and takes a plural verb.

</details>

**2.** Spot the error: 'He is senior than me in service.'

(a) 'senior' should be 'seniority'  
(b) 'in' should be 'at'  
(c) 'than' should be 'to'  
(d) 'me' should be 'mine'

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) 'than' should be 'to'** — Latin comparatives ending in -or (senior, junior, superior, inferior, prior) take 'to', not 'than': 'He is senior to me.' 'Than' is used only with ordinary -er comparatives (older than me).

</details>

**3.** Spot the error: 'She has been working in this office since five years.'

(a) 'since' should be 'for'  
(b) No error  
(c) 'working' should be 'worked'  
(d) 'has been' should be 'is'

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) 'since' should be 'for'** — Use 'for' with a length of time (for five years, for two months) and 'since' with a starting point (since 2021, since Monday). The present perfect continuous 'has been working' is itself correct.

</details>

**4.** Spot the error: 'He did not went to the office yesterday.'

(a) 'went' should be 'go'  
(b) 'the office' should be 'office'  
(c) 'did not' should be 'does not'  
(d) 'yesterday' should be 'last day'

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) 'went' should be 'go'** — After the auxiliary 'did' (and 'does', 'do') the main verb is always in its base form: did not go, did not come, did not write. The past tense is already shown by 'did', so 'went' doubles it.

</details>

**5.** Spot the error: 'The furnitures in the new SDM office are imported.'

(a) 'in the new SDM office' should be 'of the new SDM office'  
(b) 'imported' should be 'being imported'  
(c) 'furnitures … are' should be 'furniture … is'  
(d) No error

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) 'furnitures … are' should be 'furniture … is'** — Furniture is an uncountable noun: it has no plural form and takes a singular verb. Other common uncountables that Indian learners pluralise wrongly: information, luggage, advice, equipment, scenery.

</details>

**6.** In which sentence is the article used correctly?

(a) He is a MLA from Patiala.  
(b) She bought an one-way ticket.  
(c) He is an MLA from Patiala.  
(d) It was an unique decision.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) He is an MLA from Patiala.** — Articles follow the first sound, not the first letter. 'MLA' begins with the vowel sound 'em', so 'an MLA'. 'One' begins with a 'w' sound and 'unique' with a 'yoo' sound, so they take 'a': a one-way ticket, a unique decision.

</details>

**7.** Fill in the blank: '___ Sutlej rises near Lake Rakshastal in Tibet.'

(a) No article  
(b) The  
(c) An  
(d) A

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) The** — Names of rivers, seas, oceans and mountain ranges take 'the' (the Sutlej, the Beas, the Himalayas), while names of single mountains, lakes and cities do not (Mount Everest, Lake Rakshastal, Ludhiana).

</details>

**8.** Choose the correct sentence.

(a) Rich should help the poor.  
(b) The rich should help the poor.  
(c) A rich should help the poor.  
(d) The rich should help poor.

<details><summary>Answer</summary>
