## Short notes

- Paper context: English (10+2 standard), 100 marks; Section A has comprehension, precis, letter, essay and translation (10 each); Section B is grammar (50).
- English and Punjabi papers count in the PPSC Mains total (1350 written + 150 interview = 1500); a minimum of 25% in each paper is also required.
- Step 1: read the questions first so you know what to look for.
- Step 2: read the passage once for the central idea, then a second time marking the lines that answer each question.
- Answer in complete sentences and in your own words; lifting whole sentences from the passage loses marks.
- Match length to marks: a 2-mark answer needs 2-3 focused sentences, not a paragraph.
- Keep the tense and point of view of the question: 'Why does the author...' needs a reason, introduced with 'because' or 'as'.
- Inference questions: state what the passage implies and point to the clue ('The author suggests... as shown by...').
- Vocabulary questions: give the meaning as used in the passage and, if asked, a short sentence of your own using the word.
- Title questions: short (3-6 words), covering the main idea, not a side detail; avoid full sentences.
- Do not add outside knowledge or personal opinion unless the question asks for your view.
- Common traps: confusing the author's view with a view the author is reporting, and answering only half of a two-part question.
- Number answers exactly as the question paper does, and leave a line between answers for neatness.
- Practise with editorial paragraphs: summarise the central idea in one line after every reading.
- Spend about 15 minutes; comprehension is the most scoring Section A item if answers are precise.

## Punjab angle

## Deep dive

### Question types and how to recognise them

- Factual/local ('According to the passage, what…', 'Name two…') — answer is stated in one or two lines; locate, then paraphrase.
- Reason ('Why does the author…', 'What led to…') — look for signal words in the passage: because, since, as a result, therefore, due to.
- Inference ('What can you infer…', 'What does the author imply…') — the answer is not stated; combine two clues and say 'This suggests that…'.
- Vocabulary in context ('Explain the meaning of… as used in the passage') — give the contextual meaning, not the dictionary's first meaning.
- Tone/attitude ('What is the author's attitude towards…') — name the tone with one adjective and justify with a phrase from the passage.
- Central idea / main theme — one or two sentences covering the whole passage, not the first paragraph only.
- Title — 3–6 words, a noun phrase that captures the theme (e.g. 'Water: A Shared Responsibility').
- Purpose/structure ('Why does the author give the example of…') — say what the example proves in the argument.

### Answer templates

- Reason: 'The author argues that X happens because Y; he adds that Z makes it worse.'
- Inference: 'Although not stated directly, the passage suggests that… since it mentions… and…'.
- Vocabulary: '“Frugal” here means careful with money; the author uses it to praise farmers who avoid debt.'
- Tone: 'The tone is critical but constructive: the author points out failures (“…”) yet ends with practical suggestions.'
- Central idea: 'The passage holds that…; it supports this by… and concludes that….'
- Two-part question: answer each part in a separate sentence and keep the parts in the order asked.

### Tone and attitude word bank

- Positive: appreciative, optimistic, admiring, celebratory, encouraging, reverent.
- Negative: critical, sarcastic, ironic, cynical, scornful, pessimistic, alarmist.
- Neutral: objective, analytical, informative, factual, detached, explanatory.
- Emotional: nostalgic, sentimental, anguished, indignant, sympathetic.
- Didactic or moralising — when the passage lectures the reader on values.
- Pick the adjective that fits the whole passage; 'critical' is not 'cynical' — cynical doubts all good motives, critical judges with reasons.

### Reading technique for dense administrative or editorial passages

- First read: underline the topic sentence of every paragraph (usually first or last line).
- Mark contrast words — however, but, yet, on the other hand — the author's real view usually follows them.
- Circle examples and numbers; they support points but are rarely the central idea.
- Note who is speaking: the author's view vs a view the author reports ('Critics claim…').
- Map the passage in the margin with 2–3 word labels per paragraph (cause → effect → solution).
- Re-read only the relevant lines for each question; do not re-read the whole passage every time.

### Errors that cost marks

- Lifting full sentences from the passage instead of paraphrasing.
- Answering from general knowledge ('Punjab has 23 districts') when the passage says nothing of it.
- Writing 'Yes'/'No' without a reason.
- Confusing the author's view with a quoted opponent's view.
- Over-long answers that bury the point; under-length answers for higher-mark questions.
- Spelling mistakes in words copied from the passage itself — check them.

### Practice plan (6 weeks)

- Weeks 1–2: NIOS 302 'Reading with Understanding' lessons — do every in-text question in writing.
- Weeks 3–4: one newspaper editorial a day; write the central idea in one sentence and frame three questions yourself.
- Weeks 5–6: timed 15-minute passages (PSEB Class 12 workbook or previous PPSC papers); compare answers with the passage line by line.
- Keep a vocabulary notebook: word, sentence from the passage, meaning in context, your own sentence.
- Read one long-form article a week (Yojana, Down To Earth) to build stamina for 500-word passages.
- Re-attempt the passages you scored lowest on after two weeks.

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- (a) Costs: families sell land or take heavy loans, houses lie locked, and elderly parents are left alone.
- (b) Benefits: remittances support households and returning migrants bring back new skills.
- (c) It means opportunities at home should be good enough that staying is a positive decision, not something people settle for because they could not go abroad.
- (d) Remittances: money sent home by people working in another country.

- (a) Citizens are often anxious, and patient listening can resolve problems faster than long correspondence.
- (b) Applying rules mechanically, without understanding, creates distrust among people.
- (c) Explaining decisions, even unwelcome ones, shows people they were heard and maintains trust.

**4.** Read the passage and answer the questions (10 marks):
'When the Right to Information Act came into force in 2005, it changed the relationship between the citizen and the state in a quiet but deep way. For the first time, an ordinary villager could ask a government office, in writing, why his ration card had not been issued or how the money for the village road had been spent, and the office was bound to reply within a fixed time. Information that had once been treated as the private property of officials became, at least in law, the property of the people. Many scams came to light because a few determined citizens kept asking questions. Yet the law is only as strong as the people who use it. Information commissions in several states have long lists of pending appeals, some officials reply with half-truths or delay until the matter loses importance, and a few activists who asked uncomfortable questions have faced threats. There is also a quieter problem: most citizens still do not know how to frame an application or where to send it. Transparency, therefore, cannot be handed down by an Act alone. It needs officers who see disclosure as part of their duty rather than a burden, and citizens who are willing to ask. An office that publishes its information on its own leaves little room for suspicion and saves everyone time.'
(a) How did the RTI Act change the citizen's position? (b) Why does the author say 'the law is only as strong as the people who use it'? (c) What obstacles to the Act are mentioned? (d) What does the author suggest officers should do? (e) Give the meaning of 'disclosure' as used here and a suitable title.

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- (a) It gave an ordinary citizen the legal right to ask a government office for information and get a reply within a fixed time; official information became, in law, the people's property.
- (b) Because the Act works only when citizens actually use it and officials honour it — scams were exposed only where determined people kept asking questions.
- (c) Obstacles: long queues of pending appeals before information commissions; evasive or delayed replies; threats to activists; and citizens' lack of knowledge about how and where to apply.
- (d) Officers should treat disclosure as a duty, not a burden, and publish information on their own (suo motu), which removes suspicion and saves time.
- (e) 'Disclosure' here means making information public or revealing it to citizens.
- Title: 'Right to Information: A Law That Needs Users' or 'Transparency Beyond the Act'.
- Technique: answer (c) as a list in one or two sentences; do not add outside facts such as section numbers of the Act.

</details>

**5.** Read the passage and answer the questions (10 marks):
'On a June afternoon, the difference between a tree-lined lane and a bare concrete road can be felt on the skin. Paved surfaces, glass buildings and the heat thrown out by air-conditioners and vehicles make cities several degrees warmer than the countryside around them, a pattern scientists call the urban heat island. The poor suffer most: a street vendor or a construction worker cannot escape into a cooled room, and a tin-roofed house turns into an oven by noon. As heatwaves grow longer, hospitals record more cases of heatstroke, and the demand for electricity rises just when power supply is most strained. The remedies are not mysterious. Trees with wide canopies along roads, small parks in every ward, light-coloured roofs that reflect sunlight, and ponds and wetlands that cool the air can lower local temperatures noticeably. Cities can also change working hours for outdoor labour during the hottest part of the day and open cool shelters for those without them. What is lacking is not knowledge but priority. A flyover is visible and can be inaugurated; a shady street grows slowly and wins no ceremony. Unless town planners begin to measure their success by how liveable a city is in summer, our cities will keep getting richer and hotter at the same time.'
(a) What is meant by the 'urban heat island'? (b) Why do the poor suffer most from urban heat? (c) Name any three remedies suggested by the author. (d) What does the author mean by 'not knowledge but priority'? (e) Give an antonym of 'liveable' and a title for the passage.

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- (a) It is the pattern in which a city becomes several degrees warmer than the surrounding countryside because of paved surfaces, glass buildings and heat from air-conditioners and vehicles.
- (b) Because people like street vendors and construction workers must work outdoors and cannot escape into cooled rooms, and tin-roofed homes become extremely hot by noon.
- (c) Any three: wide-canopy trees along roads; small parks in every ward; light-coloured reflective roofs; ponds and wetlands; changed working hours for outdoor labour; cool shelters.
- (d) The solutions are already known; what is missing is the will to give them importance, because visible projects such as flyovers bring credit while shady streets grow slowly and win no ceremony.
- (e) Antonym of 'liveable': unliveable (or uninhabitable).
- Title: 'Cooling Our Cities' or 'The Urban Heat Island'.
- Write each answer in your own sentence; for (d) explain the contrast the author draws (flyover vs shady street).

**6.** Read the passage and answer the questions (10 marks):
'In many small towns of north India, the coaching centre has become more important than the school. **7.** Read the passage and answer the questions (10 marks):
'The cooperative dairy is one of the most successful stories of rural India. Instead of selling milk to a private trader at whatever price he offered, small producers in a village join together, collect their milk at a common centre, test its quality and sell it through a larger union that processes and markets it under a common brand. Because the profit returns to the members, even a family with two buffaloes gets a fair price and a regular income every ten or fifteen days. Women, who do most of the work of caring for animals, often gain their first bank accounts and a voice in village decisions through these societies. The model has its weaknesses. Where elections to the society are not held on time, or where a few influential members control its affairs, the spirit of cooperation turns into a new form of dependence. Payments sometimes get delayed when the union faces losses, and adulteration by a few members can damage the reputation of all. The answer is not to abandon the model but to keep it honest: regular audits, transparent milk-testing, timely elections and training of members in their rights. A cooperative is strong only when every member feels that it truly belongs to him or her.'
(a) How does a village dairy cooperative work? (b) What benefits does it bring to small producers and to women? (c) What weaknesses of the model does the author point out? (d) How can the model be kept honest? (e) Explain the last sentence of the passage and give a title.

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- (a) Small producers pool their milk at a common village centre, where it is tested for quality, and a larger union processes and sells it under a common brand; profits return to the members.
- (b) Small producers get a fair price and regular payments every ten or fifteen days; women gain bank accounts and a voice in village decisions.
- (c) Weaknesses: delayed elections, control by a few influential members, delayed payments when the union faces losses, and adulteration by some members that harms everyone's reputation.
- (d) Regular audits, transparent milk-testing, timely elections, and training members in their rights.
- (e) A cooperative succeeds only when members feel a sense of ownership; if they feel it belongs to a few, cooperation becomes dependence.
