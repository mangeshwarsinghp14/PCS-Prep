# Precis (10)

**Paper 2 — English, 10+2 standard** › English · Subject: Language papers (Punjabi & English) · Priority: Medium

> Official syllabus line: Passage to be summarised to one-third.

[Study this in the app](https://mangeshwarsinghp14.github.io/PCS-Prep/#node/le2) · [Index](../README.md)

## Short notes

- A precis is a shortened, faithful version of a passage in about one-third of its words, with a suitable title.
- Step 1: count the words in the passage (count one line and multiply by the number of lines) and fix the target (one-third).
- Step 2: read twice; underline the central idea and supporting points; ignore examples, repetitions, quotations and figures of speech.
- Step 3: write a rough draft in your own words, then trim to the target length; final length should be within a few words of the target.
- Write in third person and in reported form; keep the author's view, not yours.
- Usually written in past tense when reporting a speech; for general arguments, keep the passage's tense consistently.
- Combine related points into one sentence; replace long phrases with single words (in spite of the fact that = although).
- Do not add new ideas, comments, conclusions or headings beyond the title.
- Title: short, apt, reflecting the central theme (often 2-5 words).
- Write the final precis as one coherent paragraph; mention the word count at the end if the paper asks for it.
- Common mistakes: copying sentences, exceeding the limit by a wide margin, missing the main idea, and keeping examples.
- Useful substitutions: 'a large number of' = many; 'at this point of time' = now; 'with a view to' = to; 'is of the opinion that' = believes.
- Precis tests understanding plus expression: marks go for content coverage, brevity and language.
- Practice: daily, reduce one editorial paragraph of about 150 words to 50.

## Punjab angle

- Precis skill is directly used in office work: noting and summarising files, which PCS officers do daily.
- Practise on paragraphs about Punjab topics (water, farm debt, drug abuse, industry) to also revise GS content.

## How PPSC asks it

Expect a passage of about 150-300 words to be reduced to one-third with a title. Examiners reward coverage of all main ideas, own words, correct length and a fitting title.

## Deep dive

### Seven-step method with timings (about 20 minutes)

- 1. Count: words in 3 lines ÷ 3 × number of lines; target = one-third, rounded (240 → 80).
- 2. Read twice: first for the theme, second to number each main point in the margin (usually 4–7 points).
- 3. Strike out examples, statistics, anecdotes, quotations, repetitions and rhetorical questions.
- 4. Write a point list in your own words (not sentences from the passage).
- 5. Draft the precis from the list as one paragraph, in the third person and reported form.
- 6. Trim to target: merge sentences, replace phrases with single words, drop adjectives that add no meaning.
- 7. Add the title, fair-copy, count, and write the word count if asked; strike through the rough work.

### Reporting a speech or first-person passage

- Change first person to third: 'We must act now' → 'The speaker urged that immediate action was needed.'
- Use reporting verbs that carry tone: argued, urged, warned, regretted, assured, emphasised, appealed, observed.
- Shift tenses back when reporting a speech: 'is' → 'was', 'will' → 'would' (unless stating a permanent truth).
- Convert questions in the passage into statements: 'Can we ignore our rivers?' → 'He stressed that rivers could not be ignored.'
- Convert exclamations into plain statements of feeling: 'What a waste!' → 'He deplored the waste.'
- Begin with the speaker only if the passage identifies one; otherwise start with the idea itself.

### Compression table — phrase → word

- in spite of the fact that → although; owing to the fact that → because; in the event that → if.
- at the present moment in time → now; in the near future → soon; on a regular basis → regularly.
- a large number of → many; the vast majority of → most; each and every → every.
- make a decision → decide; give consideration to → consider; is of the opinion that → believes.
- people who cannot read or write → illiterate people; a person who looks only after his own interest → selfish/self-seeking.
- a government by the people → democracy; a place where records are kept → archive; work done without payment → voluntary work.
- Lists → a class noun: 'wheat, rice, maize and millet' → 'cereals'; 'radio, television and newspapers' → 'the media'.
- Two parallel sentences → one with 'and', 'but' or 'while'; cause-effect pairs → one sentence with 'so' or 'because'.

### Worked example (own passage)

- Passage (about 90 words): 'Every winter, smoke from burning paddy straw spreads over Punjab and the neighbouring states. Farmers say that the gap between harvesting paddy and sowing wheat is too short, and that machines to manage straw are costly. Yet the smoke harms their own families first, causing coughs and breathing problems, and burning also kills useful organisms in the soil. If machines are shared through co-operatives, if straw is bought by power plants and industries, and if farmers are paid fairly for their effort, burning can be reduced quickly.'
- Main points: (i) straw-burning smoke every winter; (ii) farmers cite short sowing window and costly machines; (iii) harms health and soil; (iv) shared machines, industrial use and fair payment can reduce it.
- Precis (about 30 words): 'Farmers burn paddy straw because sowing time is short and machines are costly, though the smoke harms their health and soil. Shared machines, industrial use and fair incentives can curb it.'
- Title: 'Ending Stubble Burning'.
- What was dropped: 'neighbouring states', 'coughs and breathing problems' and the three 'if' clauses — their ideas survive in general words ('health', 'shared machines, industrial use and fair incentives').
- Check: all four points present; no example lost that carried an idea; length ≈ one-third; third person; no new idea.

### Title formulas

- Theme + angle: 'Water Conservation: Everyone's Duty'.
- Problem/solution pair: 'Drug Abuse and Its Remedies'.
- Value statement: 'The Dignity of Labour'.
- Avoid full sentences, questions and clever puns that hide the theme.
- If the passage builds to a conclusion, the title usually names the conclusion, not the opening example.
- Test: the title should fit this passage and no other passage on the same paper.

### How marks are lost

- Missing the central idea while keeping minor details — the heaviest penalty.
- Copying sentences or long phrases verbatim from the original.
- Adding your own comment, conclusion or moral ('Thus we see that…' followed by a new idea).
- Bullet-point or two-paragraph precis — it must be one paragraph.
- Length far off the target (more than ±10%); not giving a title.
- Grammar slips from over-compression: missing articles, wrong tense sequence, dangling participles.

**4.** Make a precis of the following passage in about one-third of its length and suggest a suitable title (8+2 = 10 marks):
'A public library is one of the few places in a town where a person is welcome without being asked to buy anything. A student who cannot afford costly books, a retired clerk who wants to read the morning papers, a young woman preparing for a competitive examination and a child discovering stories for the first time can all sit under the same roof. In this sense the library is a quiet engine of equality. It gives the poor the same access to knowledge that the rich buy for themselves. Yet in many districts the public library has become a forgotten building. Its shelves hold outdated books, its reading hall has broken fans, and its hours suit the staff rather than the readers. Some people argue that the internet has made libraries unnecessary. This is only partly true. Information on a phone is scattered, often unreliable, and comes with constant distractions, while a library offers selected books, a calm space and the company of others who are also trying to learn. A modern library can also offer computers, internet access and digital books to those who have none at home. Reviving libraries does not require huge budgets. Longer opening hours, a small yearly grant for new books, clean furniture and a librarian who enjoys helping readers can transform a dusty room into the busiest place in town. A society that invests in libraries is investing in the minds of its citizens.' (241 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Target: 241 ÷ 3 ≈ 80 words (within about five words either way).
- Main points: library as a free, equal space; gives the poor access to knowledge; neglect of district libraries; internet is not a full substitute; low-cost revival steps.
- Drop: the list of users (student, retired clerk, young woman, child) — compress into 'everyone'; drop details like broken fans.
- Model precis (about 77 words): 'A public library welcomes everyone free and gives the poor the knowledge the rich can buy, making it a quiet engine of equality. Many district libraries, however, are neglected, with old books, poor facilities and unsuitable hours. The internet cannot replace them: online information is scattered, unreliable and distracting, whereas libraries offer selected books, calm space and digital access. Longer hours, regular grants, better furniture and helpful librarians can revive them as an investment in citizens' minds.'
- Title: 'Libraries: Engines of Equality'.
- Check: one paragraph, third person, no new ideas or opinions, word count written at the end.

</details>

**5.** Make a precis of the following passage in about one-third of its length and suggest a suitable title (8+2 = 10 marks):
'Every day, large quantities of cooked food are thrown away at weddings, hostels, hotels and even in our own kitchens, while families a few streets away go to bed hungry. The waste does not end with the food itself. The water used to grow the grain, the diesel used to transport it, the gas used to cook it and the labour of the farmer are all wasted with it. When the leftover food rots in landfills, it releases gases that add to global warming. Much of this waste comes from habits rather than necessity. Hosts prepare far more dishes than guests can eat because a lavish table is seen as a sign of status; buyers pick up more vegetables than they need because they are cheap that day; and in hostels, students take more on their plates than they finish. Some cities have shown that the problem can be reduced. Volunteer groups collect surplus food from functions and distribute it to shelters, and some marriage halls now encourage hosts to limit the number of dishes. At home, simple steps such as planning meals, storing food properly and using leftovers creatively make a real difference. In a state that takes pride in feeding the nation, wasting food should be seen not merely as careless but as wrong. Respect for food is respect for the farmer and for the hungry.' (228 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Target: 228 ÷ 3 ≈ 76 words (within about five words either way).
- Main points: food wasted while others go hungry; hidden waste of water, fuel and labour plus climate effect; causes are habits; examples of reduction; moral conclusion.
- Drop: the separate examples of hosts, buyers and students — compress into 'lavish menus, over-buying and overfilled plates'.
- Model precis (about 74 words): 'Large amounts of cooked food are wasted at functions, hostels and homes while nearby families go hungry; along with the food, the water, fuel and farm labour behind it are wasted, and rotting food adds to global warming. Most waste arises from habits such as lavish menus, over-buying and overfilled plates. Surplus-food collection, limits on dishes and better planning at home show that it can be reduced. Wasting food disrespects both farmer and hungry.'
- Title: 'Respect for Food'.
- Check: one paragraph, third person, no new ideas or opinions, word count written at the end.

**6.** Make a precis of the following passage in about one-third of its length and suggest a suitable title (8+2 = 10 marks):
'Friends, I have been asked to speak to you on the day you begin your careers as public servants, and I wish to say only a few simple things. You will soon discover that the files on your table are not merely papers; each one carries the hopes of a person who may have travelled many miles and spent money he could not spare in order to reach your office. Do not let him go back without knowing what will happen to his request. If the answer is no, tell him so clearly and explain why. Uncertainty hurts people more than a refusal. Second, remember that the rules were made to serve the public, not to protect you from it. Use them with understanding, and where a rule produces an absurd result, report it so that it can be changed. Third, guard your integrity in small matters, because that is where it is first lost. An officer who accepts a small favour today will find it hard to refuse a large one tomorrow. Finally, keep learning. The problems of our state — water, jobs, drugs, the environment — will not be solved by yesterday's methods. Read, listen to people in the field and do not be afraid to say that you were wrong. If you do these things, you will earn something no promotion can give you: the trust of the people you serve.' (234 words)

- Target: 234 ÷ 3 ≈ 78 words (within about five words either way).

**7.** Make a precis of the following passage in about one-third of its length and suggest a suitable title (8+2 = 10 marks):
'Volunteering is often seen as something done by people who have spare time and money, but in reality it is one of the most powerful ways in which a community looks after itself. Blood donation camps, cleanliness drives, tutoring of poor children and care of the elderly all depend largely on ordinary citizens who give a few hours of their week. In Punjab, the tradition of seva has long made such service a part of daily life rather than an occasional act of charity. Volunteering also changes the volunteer. A young person who spends a weekend helping at a village health camp learns patience, teamwork and a respect for people whose lives are harder than his own. These lessons cannot be taught in a classroom. However, enthusiasm alone is not enough. Volunteers who are not trained can sometimes do more harm than good, especially in rescue work or health care. Good intentions must be supported by coordination with the administration, basic training and honest record-keeping so that help reaches where it is most needed. A state that channels the energy of its volunteers wisely gains a second line of defence in every crisis.' (221 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Target: 221 ÷ 3 ≈ 74 words (within about five words either way).
