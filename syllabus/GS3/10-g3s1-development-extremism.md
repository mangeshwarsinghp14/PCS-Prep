## Short notes

- Core argument: uneven development, exclusion and poor governance create grievances that extremist groups exploit; extremism in turn blocks development, creating a vicious cycle.
- Left Wing Extremism (LWE) began with the Naxalbari uprising (1967, West Bengal); CPI (Maoist) formed in 2004 by merger of PWG and MCC; banned under UAPA.
- LWE belt ('Red Corridor') overlaps with tribal, forested, mineral-rich and poorly governed areas of central and eastern India.
- Development roots: land alienation, displacement by mining and dams without fair rehabilitation, denial of forest rights, exploitation by moneylenders and contractors, absence of state services.
- Planning Commission Expert Group (2008) on 'Development Challenges in Extremist Affected Areas' (chaired by D. Bandyopadhyay) linked LWE to land and forest rights failures.
- Legal safeguards: Fifth Schedule, PESA 1996 (gram sabha powers in Scheduled Areas), Forest Rights Act 2006, Samatha judgment (1997) on tribal land transfers, LARR Act 2013 on fair compensation.
- Nandini Sundar v State of Chhattisgarh (2011): Supreme Court held arming tribal youth as Special Police Officers (Salwa Judum) unconstitutional.
- Government approach: National Policy and Action Plan (2015) combining security and development; SAMADHAN strategy (2017); Union government set 31 March 2026 as the deadline to end LWE.
- Development measures in LWE areas: Road Requirement Plan, mobile towers, Special Central Assistance, Eklavya Model Residential Schools, Aspirational Districts Programme (2018), skill centres, banking outreach.
- Number of LWE-affected districts has fallen sharply over the past decade as roads, camps and welfare delivery expanded.
- North-East insurgencies: ethnic identity, isolation, underdevelopment and porous borders; peace accords (e.g. Bodo Accord 2020) combine autonomy and development packages.
- Jammu and Kashmir: political alienation and cross-border sponsorship are primary drivers, though unemployment and governance deficits aid recruitment.
- Counter-view: development alone does not end extremism - ideology, external support, and organisational strength of armed groups matter; development projects can themselves trigger conflict if imposed.
- Extremism damages development: attacks on roads, schools, telecom towers; extortion; flight of investment and officials.
- Way forward: rights-based development, effective PESA/FRA implementation, local recruitment in police, surrender-rehabilitation policies, credible grievance redress, good governance at the last mile.

## Punjab angle

- Punjab militancy (1980s-early 1990s) had political and religious roots, but contemporary analysis also points to rural educated unemployment and Green Revolution-induced disparities as enabling factors.
- Today's risks: unemployed or drug-affected youth targeted by gangster networks and foreign-based radical handlers via social media.
- Incidents such as the 2023 Ajnala police station episode showed how radical mobilisation can exploit grievances.
- Development responses relevant to Punjab: jobs and skilling for rural youth, de-addiction, border area development, sports and community engagement.

## How PPSC asks it

Questions ask whether underdevelopment causes extremism; balanced answers show the two-way link, cite PESA/FRA, committees and government strategy, and avoid a purely security-centric or purely development-centric view.

## Deep dive

### Left Wing Extremism — timeline (year — event)

- 1967 — Naxalbari peasant uprising in Darjeeling district (Charu Majumdar, Kanu Sanyal, Jangal Santhal).
- 1969 — CPI (Marxist–Leninist) formed; 1971 — joint Army–police 'Operation Steeplechase' crushes the first wave; Charu Majumdar dies in custody (1972).
- 1980s — People's War Group (Andhra Pradesh) and Maoist Communist Centre (Bihar) revive the movement in forest and tribal areas.
- 2004 — PWG and MCC merge to form the CPI (Maoist), creating the 'Red Corridor' from Bihar–Jharkhand to Andhra–Chhattisgarh.
- 2005–06 — Salwa Judum in Chhattisgarh; the Prime Minister calls Naxalism the 'single biggest internal security challenge' (2006).
- 2009–10 — CPI (Maoist) banned under UAPA (2009); Operation Green Hunt; Dantewada ambush kills 76 CRPF personnel (April 2010).
- 2011 and 2013 — Nandini Sundar case ends the SPO/Salwa Judum model (2011); Jhiram Ghati attack kills senior Chhattisgarh Congress leaders (May 2013).
- 2015–17 — National Policy and Action Plan (2015) and SAMADHAN doctrine (2017) combine security and development.
- 2024–25 — Union government sets 31 March 2026 to end LWE; CPI (Maoist) general secretary Nambala Keshava Rao ('Basavaraju') killed in Narayanpur–Abujhmarh (May 2025).
- October 2025 — LWE-affected districts cut from 18 to 11, 'most affected' from 6 to 3 (Bijapur, Sukma, Narayanpur); 1,639 cadres surrendered and over 300 killed in 2025 to that date.
- 30 March 2026 — Union Home Minister tells the Lok Sabha that India has become 'Naxal-free' and the Maoist central structure is almost dismantled.

### Why development deficits feed extremism — theory and evidence

- Relative deprivation (Ted Gurr): violence grows when people see a gap between what they have and what they believe they deserve.
- Grievance vs greed (Collier–Hoeffler): rebellion is also driven by opportunity — forests, minerals, extortion and weak state presence.
- Planning Commission Expert Group (D. Bandyopadhyay, 2008): land alienation, displacement, forest-rights denial and poor governance explain LWE spread.
- LWE districts overlapped with Fifth Schedule tribal areas, high poverty, low literacy and mineral wealth — 'rich land, poor people'.
- Displacement by dams and mines without fair resettlement (before LARR 2013) created a pool of aggrieved people.
- Weak implementation of PESA (1996) and the Forest Rights Act (2006) — high rejection of individual claims — undermined trust in the state.
- Absent services (schools, health, roads, banks) left a vacuum that parallel 'Janatana Sarkars' filled.
- Counter-evidence: many poor districts never saw insurgency; ideology, leadership and terrain also matter.

### The security-plus-development toolkit

- SAMADHAN: Smart leadership, Aggressive strategy, Motivation and training, Actionable intelligence, Dashboard-based KPIs, Harnessing technology, Action plan for each theatre, No access to financing.
- Security: fortified police stations and forward camps, CoBRA and Bastariya Battalion (local tribal youth in the CRPF), drones and better intelligence.
- Finance: Security Related Expenditure scheme and Special Central Assistance for the most-affected districts; NIA and ED target extortion and funding networks.
- Roads: Road Requirement Plan and the Road Connectivity Project for LWE Areas (2016); mobile towers saturated with 4G.
- Services: Eklavya Model Residential Schools, ITIs and skill centres, bank branches and post offices, Aspirational Districts Programme (2018).
- State schemes: Chhattisgarh's 'Niyad Nellanar' (2024) brings a basket of welfare services to villages near new security camps.
- Rehabilitation: surrender-cum-rehabilitation policies with cash, housing, vocational training; surrendered cadres used for intelligence.
- Rights: PESA rules in states, community forest rights, consent of gram sabhas for projects.

### After 'Naxal-free' — consolidating peace

- Hold-and-build: keep camps as service hubs, not just security posts; convert them into schools, clinics and markets.
- Avoid new grievances: mining leases and forest diversion need gram sabha consent and fair benefit-sharing (e.g. District Mineral Foundation funds).
- Rehabilitate and reintegrate surrendered cadres; watch for splinter groups and urban support networks.
- Strengthen local police and administration so that CAPFs can withdraw gradually.
- Monitor outcomes: land titles, FRA claims settled, school attendance, nutrition — not just violence statistics.
- Historical lesson: the 1972 defeat was followed by a revival a decade later when grievances stayed unresolved.

### Other theatres of the development–extremism link

- North-East: insurgencies rooted in identity and isolation; peace accords combine autonomy, money and development — Bodo (2020), Karbi Anglong (2021), ULFA pro-talks faction (2023), NLFT/ATTF in Tripura (2024).
- AFSPA has been withdrawn from large parts of Assam, Nagaland and Manipur since 2022 as violence fell.
- Manipur's ethnic conflict (from May 2023) shows how land, identity and resource competition can turn violent; President's Rule was imposed in February 2025.
- Jammu and Kashmir: cross-border sponsorship is primary; development (tourism, infrastructure) after August 2019 aimed to cut local recruitment; the Pahalgam attack (April 2025) targeted tourism itself.
- Punjab 1980s: political and religious triggers (Anandpur Sahib Resolution 1973, Operation Blue Star 1984) combined with educated rural unemployment and uneven Green Revolution gains; militancy ended by about 1993.
- Punjab today: drug abuse, unemployment and emigration pressures among youth are exploited by gangster–radical networks with handlers abroad.

### Debates for Mains

- 'Development first' vs 'security first': the experience since 2015 suggests sequencing — clear, hold, then build.
- Can development itself cause conflict? Large mining and dam projects without consent have triggered resistance.
- Human rights concerns: encounters, arrests of activists (e.g. Bhima Koregaon case) and civilian casualties must be addressed to keep legitimacy.
- Federal coordination: police is a State subject, but CAPFs, funds and intelligence come from the Centre.
- Measuring success: fewer incidents vs better governance; a ceasefire of violence is not the same as justice for tribal communities.
- Lesson for Punjab: prevention through jobs, de-addiction and community policing is cheaper than counter-insurgency.

### Answer angles

- Define extremism and show the two-way link (deficits → grievances → extremism → blocked development).
- Use LWE as the main case with 2025–26 data (11 districts, 3 most affected, 30 March 2026 declaration).
- Cite Bandyopadhyay (2008), PESA, FRA and SAMADHAN by name.
- Add North-East or Punjab as a second example.
- Balance security gains with rights and governance concerns.
- End with a consolidation plan: rights-based development, local police, rehabilitation, outcome monitoring.

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: Punjab's history of militancy and its causes.
