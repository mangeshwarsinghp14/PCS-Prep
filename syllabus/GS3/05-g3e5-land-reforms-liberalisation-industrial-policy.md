## Short notes

- Land reform goals: remove exploitation, give land to the tiller, raise productivity, reduce rural inequality.
- Components: abolition of intermediaries (zamindari, jagirdari), tenancy reform (rent regulation, security of tenure, ownership rights), ceiling on holdings with redistribution of surplus, consolidation of holdings, updating land records.
- Zamindari abolition was the most successful component, largely completed in the 1950s; First Amendment (1951) created the Ninth Schedule to protect land laws from judicial challenge.
- Tenancy reform was weak: oral leases, eviction before laws took effect ('voluntary surrenders'); exceptions include Kerala and West Bengal (Operation Barga, 1978).
- Ceiling laws suffered from benami and fictitious transfers, high ceilings and exemptions; redistributed surplus land was limited.
- Bhoodan movement (Vinoba Bhave, 1951, Pochampalli, Telangana) sought voluntary land gifts.
- Recent approaches: Model Agricultural Land Leasing Act 2016 (T. Haque committee), DILRMP (2008) for digitised records, SVAMITVA (2020) property cards for rural habitations, unique land parcel ID (ULPIN).
- 1991 reforms (LPG): triggered by balance of payments crisis; New Industrial Policy (July 1991), rupee devaluation, trade liberalisation, FDI opening, financial sector reforms (Narasimham Committee).
- Effects of liberalisation: higher GDP growth, services/IT boom, rise in forex reserves, poverty decline, consumer choice, global integration.
- Concerns: jobless growth, rising inequality, agrarian distress, stagnant manufacturing share, regional divergence, informalisation.
- Industrial Policy Resolutions: 1948 (mixed economy), 1956 (Schedule A reserved for public sector, Schedule B mixed, Schedule C private) - basis of the 'licence-permit raj'.
- 1991 policy: abolished licensing except a few sectors, cut public sector reservation (now only atomic energy and railway operations), MRTP restrictions removed, automatic FDI approval.
- Later reforms: Competition Act 2002 replaced MRTP; FEMA 1999 replaced FERA; IBC 2016; GST 2017; corporate tax cut 2019; four Labour Codes consolidating 29 central laws.
- Make in India (2014), National Manufacturing Policy (2011, target manufacturing at 25% of GDP), PLI schemes (2020) across 14 sectors, Startup India (2016), revised MSME definitions (2020).
- Effects on industrial growth: capacity expansion and competitiveness in autos, pharma, steel; but manufacturing share in GDP has remained in the mid-teens and employment growth in factories modest.
- Way forward: land and labour market reforms with safeguards, logistics cost reduction (PM Gati Shakti), MSME credit, cluster development, R&D.

## Punjab angle

- Punjab was a pioneer in consolidation of holdings under the East Punjab Holdings (Consolidation and Prevention of Fragmentation) Act 1948, which aided the Green Revolution.
- Tenancy and ceiling laws: Punjab Security of Land Tenures Act 1953, PEPSU Tenancy and Agricultural Lands Act 1955, Punjab Land Reforms Act 1972.
- Punjab has extensive informal leasing; reverse tenancy (small owners leasing out to bigger farmers) is common.
- Liberalisation-era industrial lag: tax incentives to neighbouring hill states (2003 package) and landlocked location drew industry away from Punjab.
- Punjab has digitised land records (online jamabandi/fard) to reduce disputes.

## Deep dive

### Land reforms — legal and policy milestones (year — step)

- 1949 — Congress Agrarian Reforms Committee (J.C. Kumarappa) recommends abolition of intermediaries, tenancy security and ceilings.
- 1951 — First Amendment adds Articles 31A and 31B and the Ninth Schedule to shield land laws from court challenges.
- 1955 and 1964 — Fourth and Seventeenth Amendments widen 'estate' and protect ceiling laws (with market-value compensation for land under personal cultivation within the ceiling).
- 1972 — National guidelines on ceilings for a family of five: about 10–18 acres of double-cropped irrigated land, 27 acres of single-cropped land, 54 acres of dry land.
- 1969 and 1978 — Kerala's Land Reforms (Amendment) Act 1969 and West Bengal's Operation Barga (1978) — the most effective tenancy reforms.
- 2007 — I.R. Coelho judgment: laws added to the Ninth Schedule after 24 April 1973 can be tested against the basic structure.
- 2008–16 — National Land Records Modernisation Programme (2008) recast as DILRMP (2016), a fully central scheme for digitised records and registration.
- 2013 — Right to Fair Compensation and Transparency in Land Acquisition, Rehabilitation and Resettlement Act (LARR) replaces the 1894 Act.
- 2016 — NITI Aayog's Model Agricultural Land Leasing Act (T. Haque) to legalise tenancy and protect owners.
- 2020–25 — SVAMITVA property cards for rural inhabited land (April 2020); ULPIN or 'Bhu-Aadhaar' — a 14-digit land parcel ID.

### Assessment of land reforms

- Intermediary abolition: largely complete in the 1950s; it brought about 2 crore tenants into direct relation with the state.
- Tenancy reform: weakest component — concealed and oral tenancy grew; many states now ban or restrict leasing, pushing it underground.
- Ceilings: high limits, family splits, benami transfers and long litigation left little surplus land; much distributed land was poor quality.
- Consolidation of holdings: successful in Punjab, Haryana and western UP — a pre-condition of the Green Revolution.
- Land records: studies of court data (e.g. by DAKSH) suggest about two-thirds of civil cases involve land or property; digitised, conclusive titles are the current reform frontier.
- Bhoodan and Gramdan collected land, but much of it was unfit for cultivation or never distributed.
- New agenda: legal leasing, land titling (conclusive titles), digital maps, women's land rights and fair acquisition for industry.

### Punjab land reform specifics

- East Punjab Holdings (Consolidation and Prevention of Fragmentation) Act 1948 — Punjab completed consolidation earlier than any other state.
- Punjab Security of Land Tenures Act 1953 and PEPSU Tenancy and Agricultural Lands Act 1955 gave tenants protection and purchase rights.
- Punjab Land Reforms Act 1972 sets the permissible area in 'standard acres' (adjusted for land quality and irrigation).
- Punjab Village Common Lands (Regulation) Act 1961 governs shamlat land; one-third of panchayat land auctions are reserved for Scheduled Castes.
- Average operational holding in Punjab (about 3.6 ha, Agriculture Census 2015-16) is over three times the national average (1.08 ha).
- Reverse tenancy: small owners lease out land to larger farmers; leasing is widespread but mostly informal.
- Punjab's Land Pooling Policy 2025 for urban development was withdrawn in August 2025 after farmer opposition — a lesson in consent-based acquisition.
- Online jamabandi and fard reduce corruption at patwari level; mutation delays remain a common grievance.

### Industrial policy after 1991 — the second wave

- National Manufacturing Policy 2011: manufacturing to 25% of GDP and 100 million jobs, with National Investment and Manufacturing Zones.
- Make in India (September 2014); Startup India (January 2016); Business Reform Action Plan ranks states on ease of doing business.
- India's World Bank Doing Business rank rose from 142 (2015) to 63 (2020) before the report was discontinued.
- Jan Vishwas Act 2023 decriminalised minor offences in dozens of central laws to ease compliance.
- PLI schemes in 14 sectors (2020-21), PM MITRA textile parks (seven sites, 2023), 12 new industrial smart cities on corridors (approved 2024).
- National Manufacturing Mission announced in Budget 2025-26; Budget 2026-27 added an SME Growth Fund (₹10,000 crore), modernisation of 200 legacy clusters, three chemical parks and ISM 2.0.
- Factor-market reforms: Labour Codes in force from 21 November 2025; land reform remains with the states.

### Effects of liberalisation on industry — evidence

- Manufacturing share of GDP has stayed around 15–17% for three decades — no East Asian-style surge.
- Under the new GDP series (base 2022-23), manufacturing grew at double digits in 2023-24 and 2025-26.
- Electronics: mobile phone production and exports rose sharply after PLI; smartphones became one of India's largest export items.
- Corporate balance sheets improved after IBC (2016), bank clean-up and the 2019 tax cut; private investment revival has been uneven.
- Competition raised quality and productivity in autos, pharma, steel and telecom; but many small firms remain informal and low-productivity.
- Employment: organised manufacturing created jobs slowly; capital-intensive growth and contract labour are common.
- Regional effect: industry concentrated in coastal and western states with ports and better logistics.

### Debates for Mains

- 'Premature deindustrialisation' (Dani Rodrik): developing countries' manufacturing peaks earlier and lower than in the past.
- Labour regulation: Besley and Burgess (2004) linked pro-worker amendments to lower manufacturing output; others argue firm size is limited more by credit, land and infrastructure.
- PLI critique: subsidies for assembly with limited domestic value addition (debated by Raghuram Rajan and others) vs supporters citing scale and export gains.
- Land acquisition vs farmer rights: LARR's consent and social-impact clauses are seen as protective by some and slow by others.
- Services-led vs manufacturing-led growth: AI and automation may limit factory jobs, so labour-intensive sectors (textiles, food, footwear) need special focus.
- China+1 opportunity depends on logistics costs, tariffs and policy stability.

### Punjab industry — policy and constraints

- Industrial and Business Development Policy 2026 (cabinet approval 7 March 2026): 24 sector policies, 20 incentive options, eligibility period extended to 10–15 years.
- Nine priority sectors get an extra 25% incentive: food processing, sports goods, textiles, agro-waste processing, auto components, electronics/semiconductors, IT, EVs, defence/aerospace — also all units in border and Kandi districts.
- Specific subsidies: ₹20 crore for zero-liquid-discharge systems and ₹7.5 crore for paddy-straw boilers.
- Constraints: landlocked location, distance from ports, border insecurity, high state debt limiting incentives, the 2003 hill-state package that drew units away.
- Strengths: MSME clusters (Ludhiana hosiery and auto parts, Jalandhar sports goods, Mandi Gobindgarh steel), skilled workforce, Eastern DFC starting near Ludhiana.
- Land for industry is scarce and costly in a fertile, densely farmed state — making consent-based pooling and brownfield use important.

### Answer angles

- For land reforms: give component-wise success/failure with state examples (Kerala, West Bengal, Punjab consolidation).
- Link land records digitisation to credit access, dispute reduction and leasing reform.
- For industrial policy: show the arc 1948 → 1956 → 1991 → 2014 → 2020 (PLI) → 2025 (Labour Codes).
- Use evidence (manufacturing share, export data) rather than slogans.
- Add Punjab's 2026 policy and constraints for a state-specific conclusion.
- Trap: the Ninth Schedule protection is not absolute after I.R. Coelho (2007) — do not write that land laws are immune from review.

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** Land reforms in India achieved only partial success. Examine the reasons and assess the relevance of land reforms today. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: objectives and components.
- Successes: zamindari abolition, consolidation in Punjab/Haryana, Operation Barga, Kerala.
- Failures: tenancy and ceiling evasion, poor records, political will, judicial delays.
- Today: land leasing reform, digitised records, land titling, women's land rights, land for industry.
- Conclusion: secure land rights as foundation for credit, investment and productivity.

</details>

**2.** Evaluate the impact of the 1991 economic reforms on Indian industry. (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: BoP crisis and New Industrial Policy 1991.
- Positives: competition, technology, FDI, exports, growth in autos/pharma/IT.
- Negatives: stagnant manufacturing share, deindustrialisation fears, MSME stress, jobless growth.
- Later corrective steps: Make in India, PLI, GST, IBC.
- Conclusion: reforms need second-generation factor market reforms.

</details>

**3.** Trace the changes in India's industrial policy since 1956 and analyse their effects on industrial growth. What lessons do they hold for industrial revival in Punjab? (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- 1956 resolution: public sector commanding heights, licensing.
- 1970s-80s: MRTP, partial delicensing.
- 1991: delicensing, FDI, disinvestment.
- 2014 onwards: Make in India, PLI, ease of doing business, logistics.
- Effects: growth spurts, sectoral shifts, regional concentration.
- Punjab lessons: MSME cluster upgrading, logistics, single-window clearance, stable power tariffs, skilled labour.
