## Short notes

- Internal security challenges arise from interplay of external state actors, state actors' failures, and non-state actors (terrorists, insurgents, organised crime, radical groups).
- External state actors: hostile states sponsor proxy war, cross-border terrorism, infiltration, drugs and fake currency, and influence operations; Pakistan's ISI has long been accused of this in J&K and Punjab.
- External non-state actors: terror groups (LeT, JeM), diaspora-based separatist outfits (e.g. Sikhs for Justice, banned under UAPA in 2019), transnational criminal syndicates.
- Internal non-state actors: insurgent groups, Maoists, communal and radical organisations, gangsters, cyber criminals.
- State actors' role: weak policing, poor intelligence coordination, political patronage or alienation can worsen threats; good governance prevents them.
- Communication network challenges: encrypted messaging, dark web, VoIP, crypto-funding, drones, satellite phones used for coordination and propaganda.
- Social media risks: radicalisation and recruitment, fake news and deepfakes, communal incitement, mob violence fuelled by rumours (e.g. 2018 child-lifting rumours), foreign influence operations.
- Media's positive role: awareness, accountability, countering propaganda, crisis communication; negative role: sensationalism, live coverage aiding terrorists (26/11), trial by media.
- After 26/11, broadcast rules were tightened to restrict live coverage of anti-terror operations.
- Legal framework: IT Act 2000 (Section 69A blocking; Section 79 intermediary safe harbour), IT Rules 2021 (due diligence, grievance officers, traceability for significant messaging intermediaries), Telecommunications Act 2023, DPDP Act 2023.
- Shreya Singhal v Union of India (2015) struck down Section 66A of the IT Act as violating free speech; Anuradha Bhasin v Union of India (2020) required internet shutdowns to be necessary and proportionate.
- Institutions: I4C under MHA, CERT-In, NIA, IB, state cyber cells; Sahyog portal (2024) for faster notices to intermediaries.
- Balancing act: security vs free speech and privacy (Puttaswamy 2017 - privacy a fundamental right).
- Way forward: platform accountability, fact-checking, digital literacy, counter-narratives, AI-based detection of deepfakes, international cooperation (MLATs), media self-regulation.

## Punjab angle

- Pakistan-based handlers use drones to drop drugs and weapons across the Punjab border; BSF jurisdiction in Punjab was extended from 15 km to 50 km from the border in 2021.
- Gangster-terror nexus with handlers based abroad; Punjab Police set up the Anti-Gangster Task Force (2022).
- Social media has been used for radical propaganda and gang recruitment glorifying violence among youth.
- Punjab's 'Yudh Nashian Virudh' anti-drug campaign (2025) targets the narcotics networks that also fund crime.
- Pathankot airbase (2016) and Dinanagar (2015) attacks show cross-border terror risks to the state.

## Deep dive

### Actor map — who threatens internal security (with examples)

- External state actors: Pakistan's military-intelligence establishment (proxy war in J&K, drones and narcotics into Punjab, fake currency); China (cyber intrusions, influence operations, LAC pressure).
- External non-state actors: Lashkar-e-Taiba and its front The Resistance Front (designated a Foreign Terrorist Organisation by the US in July 2025), Jaish-e-Mohammed, Babbar Khalsa International, Khalistan Zindabad Force.
- Diaspora-based separatists: Sikhs for Justice (banned under UAPA in 2019; ban extended in 2024); several Canada- and US-based handlers designated as individual terrorists (e.g. Arsh Dalla 2023, Goldy Brar 2024).
- Transnational gangs: the Lawrence Bishnoi network was listed as a terrorist entity by Canada (September 2025); gangs supply shooters and logistics to terror handlers.
- Internal non-state actors: Maoist remnants, NE insurgent factions, radical preachers, organised crime, cyber-fraud syndicates.
- State actors' role: police capacity gaps, poor inter-agency coordination, political patronage of criminals, or alienation caused by heavy-handed action can enlarge threats.
- Hybrid threat: the same networks run drugs, arms, extortion, radical propaganda and terror — making crime and terror hard to separate.

### Recent case studies (2025–26)

- Pahalgam (22 April 2025): 26 civilians, mostly tourists, killed in Baisaran meadow; India placed the Indus Waters Treaty in abeyance and closed the Attari land route.
- Operation Sindoor (7 May 2025): precision strikes on terror infrastructure in Pakistan and PoK; a four-day conflict with drones and missiles ended on 10 May.
- Information war during Sindoor: fake videos, recycled clips and deepfakes from both sides; PIB Fact Check and platform takedowns were used heavily.
- Red Fort car blast (10 November 2025): NIA probe uncovered a 'white-collar' terror module linked to doctors and a Faridabad cache — showing radicalisation of educated professionals through online networks.
- Punjab grenade and IED attacks on police posts (2024–25) were traced to foreign-based BKI handlers recruiting local youth via social media for small payments.
- India–Canada row (2023–24) over the Nijjar killing strained diplomacy; ties were partly normalised in 2025 with restored high commissioners.

### Communication networks — vulnerabilities

- Encrypted messaging (end-to-end), VPNs and virtual numbers hide handlers abroad; voice-over-internet calls avoid lawful interception.
- Dark web marketplaces and crypto-assets move drugs, weapons and money; hawala bridges crypto and cash.
- Drones serve as both delivery and reconnaissance tools on the Punjab and Jammu borders.
- SIM-box and spoofed international calls used in fraud and hoaxes; Sanchar Saathi portal (2023) and CEIR block stolen phones and fraudulent connections.
- Telecommunications Act 2023: lawful interception, biometric SIM verification, suspension in emergencies and satellite spectrum by administrative allocation.
- National Security Directive on the Telecom Sector (2021): operators may buy equipment only from 'trusted sources'.
- Critical information infrastructure (power, banking, telecom) is a target of state-backed hackers — overlapping with cyber security (g3s3).

### Media and social media — roles and rules

- Positive roles: early warning, public information during crises, exposing failures, countering propaganda, mobilising relief.
- Negative roles: radicalisation and recruitment, rumours triggering lynchings, communal polarisation, live coverage that helps attackers (26/11), 'trial by media'.
- IT Act 2000: Section 69A (blocking for sovereignty, security, public order), Section 79 (intermediary safe harbour subject to due diligence).
- IT Rules 2021: grievance officers, takedown timelines, first-originator traceability for significant messaging platforms (challenged in courts); the 2023 government 'fact check unit' amendment was struck down by the Bombay High Court (2024).
- Sahyog portal (2024) lets agencies send takedown notices to platforms quickly; the Karnataka High Court dismissed X Corp's challenge to it (2025).
- Courts set limits: Shreya Singhal (2015) struck down Section 66A; Anuradha Bhasin (2020) required internet shutdowns to be necessary, proportionate and periodically reviewed.
- New laws: DPDP Act 2023 with Rules notified in November 2025 (phased over 12–18 months); Promotion and Regulation of Online Gaming Act 2025 bans real-money gaming, which had been a laundering channel.
- AI and deepfakes: IT Rules amendments notified in February 2026 (after an October 2025 draft) require labelling of AI-generated (synthetic) content and faster takedowns; fact-checking and media literacy are the soft defences.

### Punjab — actors and media in action

- BSF seized 272 Pakistani drones, over 367 kg of heroin and about 200 weapons on the 553 km Punjab border in 2025 (to late November).
- Punjab Police 'Baaz Akh' anti-drone systems (from August 2025; nine planned from Pathankot to Fazilka) act as a second line behind the BSF.
- 'Yudh Nashian Virudh' (from March 2025): by its 500th day (July 2026) the state reported about 73,000 alleged drug peddlers arrested.
- Anti-Gangster Task Force (April 2022) targets gangster–terror networks; many shooters are recruited online by handlers in Canada, the US, Europe and Pakistan.
- Gun-glorifying songs and social-media posts were restricted by state orders (2022) to curb the gun culture used for gang recruitment.
- BSF's jurisdiction in Punjab was extended from 15 km to 50 km in October 2021 — a Centre–state friction point on policing powers.
- Border districts faced drone and missile incidents and blackouts during the May 2025 conflict — civil defence and community alert systems mattered.

### Way forward and debates

- Security vs liberty: blocking and shutdowns must meet the proportionality test; transparency reports on takedowns build trust.
- Platform accountability: faster response to lawful orders, local grievance handling and AI-based detection of terror content.
- Counter-radicalisation: de-radicalisation programmes, community leaders, sports and jobs for at-risk youth.
- Diplomatic tools: extradition, MLATs, Interpol notices and FATF pressure on terror financing.
- Media self-regulation (NBDSA) plus clear crisis-reporting protocols.
- Intelligence fusion: NATGRID, Multi-Agency Centre and state-level fusion centres; police modernisation.

### Answer angles

- Classify actors (external state, external non-state, internal non-state, state failures) and give one recent example each.
- Separate communication-network challenges (technology) from media/social-media challenges (content).
- Quote laws and judgments precisely: IT Act 69A/79, IT Rules 2021, Shreya Singhal, Anuradha Bhasin, Telecom Act 2023, DPDP Rules 2025.
- Use Punjab's drone–drug–gangster nexus as the state case with 2025 BSF figures.
- Balance security needs with free speech and privacy (Puttaswamy, 2017).
- Conclude with a whole-of-society approach.
