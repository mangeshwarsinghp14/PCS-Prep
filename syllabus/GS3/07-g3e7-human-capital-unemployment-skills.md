## Short notes

- Human capital = stock of skills, knowledge and health embodied in people (Schultz, Becker); drives productivity, innovation and inclusive growth.
- Investments: education, health, nutrition, on-the-job training, migration; measured via HDI (UNDP) and the World Bank Human Capital Index.
- Types of unemployment: structural, frictional, cyclical, seasonal (agriculture), disguised (surplus labour on farms), educated unemployment, technological.
- Measurement in India: Usual Status (principal plus subsidiary), Current Weekly Status, Current Daily Status; PLFS by NSO since 2017-18 replaced the 5-yearly NSSO employment rounds.
- Key ratios: Labour Force Participation Rate, Worker Population Ratio, Unemployment Rate = unemployed / labour force x 100.
- PLFS 2023-24 (usual status, 15+): unemployment rate about 3.2%, LFPR about 60%, female LFPR about 42% - rising sharply from 2017-18, largely self-employment in rural areas.
- Since 2025, PLFS also releases monthly and quarterly estimates (Current Weekly Status) for rural and urban areas.
- Nature of India's problem: underemployment and low-quality jobs more than open unemployment; around 90% of workers in informal employment; youth and educated unemployment higher than average.
- Employment trends: agriculture still employs the largest share (over two-fifths of workers); rising self-employment and construction; slow growth of regular salaried and manufacturing jobs.
- Skill development: Skill India Mission (2015), National Policy on Skill Development and Entrepreneurship (2015), PMKVY, NSDC (2008), ITIs, apprenticeship schemes, NEP 2020's integration of vocational education.
- Only a small share of India's workforce has formal vocational training, far lower than East Asian economies.
- Demographic dividend: working-age (15-64) share rising; median age under 30; window expected to last till around the 2050s before ageing sets in, with states ageing at different speeds.
- Dividend is not automatic: needs health, education quality, female workforce participation and job creation; otherwise risk of 'demographic disaster'.
- Labour reforms: four Labour Codes (wages, industrial relations, social security, occupational safety) consolidating 29 central laws; e-Shram portal for unorganised workers.
- Recent initiatives: PM Internship Scheme (announced Budget 2024-25), employment-linked incentives, Mudra loans, Startup India, PM Vishwakarma for artisans.
- Way forward: labour-intensive manufacturing, care and green jobs, women's safety and childcare, industry-linked skilling, apprenticeship scale-up, better labour market data.

## Punjab angle

- Large-scale youth emigration (study visas to Canada, UK, Australia) reflects aspirations and job gaps; it drains human capital but brings remittances.
- Unemployment in Punjab has generally been above the national average in PLFS surveys, especially among educated youth.
- Substance abuse among youth erodes human capital; health and rehabilitation are linked to employability.
- State initiatives: Punjab Skill Development Mission, Schools of Eminence (2022), government recruitment drives, job fairs (rozgar melas).
- Low female workforce participation and disguised unemployment on small farms are key structural issues.

## How PPSC asks it

Questions probe nature of unemployment in India, quality of jobs, demographic dividend and skilling; examiners reward PLFS-based data, clear typology and realistic solutions with Punjab examples (emigration, drugs, farm-dependence).

## Deep dive

### Labour-market data you must quote (with year)

- PLFS Annual Report 2025 (January–December 2025, usual status, 15+): LFPR 59.3%, WPR 57.4%, unemployment rate 3.1%.
- Gender: male LFPR 79.1% vs female 40.0%; urban female LFPR only 27.7% while urban female unemployment is 6.4%.
- Youth (15–29) unemployment 9.9% overall and 13.6% in urban areas — the core 'educated unemployment' problem.
- Status of work (2025): self-employed 56.2%, regular wage/salaried 23.6%, casual 20.2%; agriculture still 43.0% of workers.
- Monthly PLFS (current weekly status, new design from 2025): June 2026 unemployment 5.5%, LFPR 54.4%, female LFPR 32.7%.
- Economic Survey 2025-26: about 56.2 crore people (15+) were employed in July–September 2025, 8.7 lakh more than the previous quarter.
- Skills gap: only 4.9% of youth (15–29) have formal vocational/technical training and 21.2% informal training (PLFS 2023-24, cited in Economic Survey 2025-26).
- PLFS 2025 figures are not strictly comparable with earlier years because the sample was redesigned from January 2025.

### Types and measurement of unemployment — deeper notes

- Usual Status (365-day reference, principal + subsidiary) gives a low 'chronic' rate; Current Weekly Status (7-day) captures short-term joblessness and is higher; Current Daily Status gives person-days.
- Disguised unemployment: marginal productivity near zero (e.g. extra family members on small farms) — removing them does not reduce output.
- Underemployment: working fewer hours or below skill level; the main Indian problem rather than open unemployment.
- Structural unemployment arises from skill–job mismatch (e.g. graduates vs available jobs); frictional from job search; cyclical from slowdowns; technological from automation and AI.
- Seasonal unemployment is high in rain-fed agriculture; VB-G RAM G allows states a 60-day no-work window in peak farm seasons to keep farm labour available.
- 'Discouraged workers' (who stop searching) lower the LFPR and can make the unemployment rate look better than reality.
- Other data sources: EPFO payroll (formal jobs proxy), e-Shram registrations, ASUSE/QBUSE for unincorporated enterprises, Time Use Survey.

### Demographic dividend — timing and conditions

- India's working-age share is expected to peak around 2030, when nearly 65% of the population will be aged 15–59 (Economic Survey 2025-26).
- States are at different stages: southern states and Punjab are ageing earlier; Bihar, UP and MP will supply most new workers into the 2040s.
- The dividend needs four conditions: jobs, skills, health/nutrition and women's participation — otherwise it becomes a 'demographic burden'.
- Time Use Survey 2024: 75% of men vs 25% of women aged 15–59 took part in employment activities on a given day; 41% of women did caregiving vs 21.4% of men (about 140 vs 74 minutes a day).
- Care economy (childcare, eldercare) is both a barrier to women's work and a source of new jobs — Budget 2026-27 plans training for 1.5 lakh geriatric caregivers.
- Census 2027 will provide the first new population base since 2011, affecting projections and targeting.

### Human capital — health and education links

- Human capital = health + education + skills; the World Bank Human Capital Index and UNDP HDI both show India below its income peers on learning and nutrition.
- Learning poverty (foundational literacy/numeracy gaps) and stunting (NFHS-5: 35.5%) reduce future productivity.
- NEP 2020 integrates vocational education from Class 6, targets 50% GER in higher education by 2035 and multiple entry–exit.
- Budget 2026-27: Samagra Shiksha ₹42,100 crore; AVGC content labs in 15,000 schools and 500 colleges; girls' STEM hostel in every district; Divyang Kaushal Yojana for persons with disabilities.
- Economic Survey 2025-26 chapter 'Getting skilling right' stresses employer-led training, apprenticeships and school-level vocational exposure.
- Return-to-schooling and returns-to-skills are higher where training is linked to jobs (apprenticeship models of Germany and Switzerland).

### Policy responses — employment and skills

- Skill India Mission (2015), PMKVY (short-term training and recognition of prior learning), NSDC (2008), Skill India Digital Hub, ITI upgradation scheme.
- Apprenticeships: National Apprenticeship Promotion Scheme and NATS (for graduates/diploma holders).
- PM Internship Scheme (announced Budget 2024-25): internships in top companies with a monthly stipend for youth.
- PM Viksit Bharat Rozgar Yojana (from 1 August 2025; about ₹99,446 crore): incentives for first-time employees and employers creating new formal jobs; ₹20,083 crore in 2026-27.
- Labour Codes in force (21 November 2025): universal minimum wage floor, social security for gig and platform workers, easier compliance; e-Shram portal for unorganised workers.
- VB-G RAM G Act 2025: 125 days of rural work, focusing on water security, rural infrastructure, livelihood assets and climate resilience.
- Self-employment support: PM Mudra Yojana, Stand-Up India, PM SVANidhi, PM Vishwakarma (2023), PMEGP.

### Punjab — jobs, skills and youth

- PLFS quarterly data (current weekly status): Punjab had the highest unemployment rate among the states listed in April–June 2025 (8.8%) and stayed well above average in July–September 2025 (7.4%), against all-India rates of 5.4% and 5.2%.
- Youth emigration via study visas (Canada, UK, Australia) drains skilled workers but brings remittances; tighter visa rules abroad since 2024 are pushing youth back into local labour markets.
- Drug abuse among youth erodes health and employability — human capital and security issues overlap.
- Responses: Punjab Skill Development Mission, Schools of Eminence, Sikhiya Kranti 2.0 (₹3,500 crore in 2026-27), government recruitment drives.
- Industrial and Business Development Policy 2026 offers employment-linked incentives and targets labour-intensive sectors (textiles, sports goods, food processing).
- Low urban female LFPR and disguised unemployment on small farms are structural; agro-processing and services can absorb this labour.

### Answer angles

- Start with a data line (PLFS 2025), define the concept, then analyse nature (underemployment, informality, gender, youth).
- Explain why low headline unemployment co-exists with distress (self-employment, low wages, discouraged workers).
- Link demographic dividend to the 2030 peak and to state-level differences.
- Cover supply side (skills, health) and demand side (labour-intensive manufacturing, services, care economy).
- Use Punjab's high youth unemployment and emigration as the state case.
- Avoid comparing PLFS 2025 directly with earlier rounds without noting the sample redesign.

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** India's unemployment problem is more about the quality of jobs than the lack of jobs. Discuss with reference to recent trends in employment. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: low open unemployment rate in PLFS vs widespread underemployment.
- Trends: high informality, self-employment, agriculture share, rising female LFPR mostly unpaid/self-employed.
- Quality concerns: low wages, no social security, disguised unemployment, educated youth unemployment.
- Causes: slow manufacturing, skills mismatch, MSME constraints.
- Measures: formalisation, labour codes, labour-intensive sectors, skilling, e-Shram.
- Conclusion: decent work (SDG 8).

</details>

**2.** 'Demographic dividend can turn into a demographic burden without investment in human capital.' Examine. (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Define demographic dividend and window.
- Conditions: health, education quality, jobs, women's participation.
- Risks: unemployable youth, social unrest, migration, inter-state differences.
- Policy: NEP 2020, Skill India, health missions, care economy.
- Conclusion.

</details>

**3.** Analyse the causes of educated youth unemployment in Punjab and its linkage with emigration and substance abuse. Suggest a skill-based strategy for the state. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: Punjab's above-average unemployment and youth emigration.
- Causes: farm dependence, slow industry, mismatch of degrees and jobs, aspiration gap, government job preference.
- Linkages: emigration via IELTS route, debt; idleness and drugs.
- Strategy: industry-linked ITIs, apprenticeships in MSME clusters, agro-processing and services skills, overseas-employment skilling with safe migration, entrepreneurship support.
- Conclusion: turning youth bulge into dividend.

</details>
