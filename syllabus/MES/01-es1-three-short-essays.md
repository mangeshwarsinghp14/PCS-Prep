# Three short essays

**Paper 3 — Essay** › Essay · Subject: Essay · Priority: High

> Official syllabus line: Three short essays in English or Punjabi on specified topics; choice of subjects given. Keep closely to the subject, arrange ideas in an orderly fashion, write concisely. Credit for content value, effective and exact expression.

## Short notes

- Paper: Essay, 150 marks, three short essays (about 50 marks each) from a choice of topics; may be written in English or Punjabi.
- Official marking cues: keep closely to the subject, arrange ideas in an orderly fashion, write concisely; credit for content value and effective, exact expression.
- Follow the word limit printed on the paper for each essay; with three essays in three hours, budget about 55 minutes each (10 planning, 40 writing, 5 revising).
- Choosing topics: pick the one where you can list 10 or more relevant points in two minutes, have Punjab and India examples, and can be balanced. Avoid a topic only because it sounds familiar.
- Topic types: socio-economic (water, migration, inclusive growth), governance/ethics (integrity, citizen service), science-technology (AI, digital India), philosophical/quote-based, and Punjab-specific.
- Decode the topic first: underline key words, define them, and note what the topic asks (cause, impact, solution, evaluation, or a value judgement).
- Structure: introduction (anecdote, fact or definition plus thesis line), body in 3-5 themed sections, counter-view with rebuttal, way forward, conclusion linking back to the opening.
- Use dimensions to generate content: social, economic, political, environmental, ethical, technological, legal, international, and individual vs state.
- Each body paragraph: topic sentence, explanation, concrete example (Punjab/India/world), and a link to the thesis.
- Balance: show both sides; examiners penalise one-sided rants and extreme positions.
- Evidence: use only facts you are sure of with years (e.g. Census 2011; Punjab Budget 2026-27 outlay Rs 2,60,437 crore); a general statement is better than a wrong number.
- Quotations: one short, accurate line from a well-known figure is enough; never invent attributions.
- Language: short, clear sentences; avoid jargon, slang and overlong paragraphs; sub-headings are optional and acceptable if used sparingly.
- Conclusion: forward-looking and optimistic yet realistic; tie to constitutional values or the Sikh Gurus' ideals of seva, equality and honest labour where relevant.
- Common mistakes: drifting off-topic, listing points without argument, repeating ideas, ignoring the second half of a two-part topic, and illegible writing.
- Practice routine: write one full essay a week under time, plus a 10-minute brainstorm on a new topic every day.

## How PPSC asks it

Topics range from Punjab-specific issues to abstract and quote-based themes. Examiners reward relevance to the exact topic, orderly structure, multi-dimensional and balanced content, sound examples and precise, clean language.

## Deep dive

### Five topic types and how to handle each

- Philosophical/quote topic ('The unexamined life…', 'Means are as important as ends'): explain the idea in plain words, test it at individual, social, national and global levels, admit its limits, and end with a balanced verdict.
- Problem topic ('Drug abuse in Punjab'): causes → effects → what has been tried → what works better → way forward; keep one paragraph on root causes, not just symptoms.
- Policy/evaluation topic ('Is free electricity good economics?'): state objectives, benefits, costs and unintended effects, evidence, alternatives, and a reasoned judgement.
- Comparison/tension topic ('Development versus environment'): reject the false choice, show trade-offs and examples where both were achieved, propose principles for balance.
- Punjab-identity topic ('Punjab: from Green Revolution to green transition'): history → present data → challenges → opportunities → a vision; use verified state facts and years.
- First 5 minutes: write the topic, underline each key word, write what the examiner expects in one line, and reject topics where you cannot list 10 relevant points.

### Brainstorm grids (use two together)

- PESTLE-E: political, economic, social, technological, legal, environmental, ethical.
- Levels: individual → family → community/village → district/state → nation → world.
- Time: past (how we got here), present (data, debate), future (trends, risks, vision).
- Stakeholders: citizens, farmers, youth, women, industry, government, civil society, media, courts, international actors.
- Values: liberty, equality, fraternity, justice, dignity, sustainability, compassion (seva), honest labour (kirat).
- Quick test: if your grid gives fewer than 12 usable points, the topic is wrong for you.

- Punjab finances 2026-27 (budget): outstanding liabilities about 45% of GSDP; committed spending on salaries, pensions and interest about 72% of revenue receipts; power subsidy Rs 15,550 crore (PRS).
- A study for the 16th Finance Commission found 75% of Punjab's highest-spending households receive free electricity — second highest among states studied (cited by PRS, 2026).
- NITI Aayog Fiscal Health Index 2025: Punjab ranked last among 18 major states.
- Transparency International CPI 2025: India 91st of 182 countries (February 2026).
- Global anchors: UN SDGs — 17 goals adopted in 2015 for 2030; Paris Agreement (2015) aims to keep warming well below 2°C; India's net-zero target is 2070 (announced at COP26, 2021).
- Constitutional anchors: Article 21 (life and personal liberty), Article 39(b)–(c) (distribution of resources), Article 51A (fundamental duties, including protecting the environment).

### Ideas and thinkers you can cite accurately

- Guru Nanak's three principles — kirat karo (honest labour), naam japo (remember God), vand chhako (share) — for essays on work, ethics and welfare.
- Gandhi's 'talisman' (1947 note): recall the face of the poorest person you have seen and ask whether your step will help him — a test for any policy essay.
- B.R. Ambedkar's closing speech in the Constituent Assembly (25 November 1949): political democracy must be made a social democracy; warned against entering a 'life of contradictions'.
- Jawaharlal Nehru's 'Tryst with Destiny' speech (14–15 August 1947) — ending poverty, ignorance, disease and inequality of opportunity.
- Amartya Sen, 'Development as Freedom' (1999): development is the expansion of people's real freedoms and capabilities, not income alone.
- John Rawls, 'A Theory of Justice' (1971): the 'veil of ignorance' — design rules without knowing your own place in society.
- Kautilya's Arthashastra: the king's happiness lies in the happiness of his subjects — for governance essays.
- Use one or two of these per essay, paraphrased; misattributed quotations (several famous lines credited to Gandhi or Einstein are not theirs) cost credibility.

### Three model outlines (headings you would use)

- 'Technology is a good servant but a bad master': Introduction (smartphone in a Punjab village) → Technology as servant: health, farming, governance → As master: addiction, surveillance, job loss, misinformation → Who decides? individual habits, regulation, design ethics → Conclusion: human purpose must lead technology.
- 'Water is Punjab's real wealth': Introduction (rivers that named the land) → From canals to tubewells: history → Present crisis: groundwater, paddy, free power, floods of 2025 → Inter-state and international dimensions → Solutions: diversification, pricing, recharge, community → Conclusion: a water-secure Punjab vision.
- 'Integrity is doing the right thing when no one is watching': Meaning → Personal level (exams, work) → Institutional level (public office, business) → Systems that support integrity (RTI, technology, leadership) → Limits and dilemmas → Conclusion: character plus institutions.
- Each outline gives 5–6 sections; at about 50 marks per essay, aim for 10–12 paragraphs only if the printed word limit allows — always follow the limit printed on the paper.
- Write the outline in the margin or on the first page and strike it through neatly before starting.
- Keep one Punjab example, one India example and one world example in every essay.

### Language and presentation craft

- Paragraph length 80–120 words; one idea per paragraph; topic sentence first.
- Vary sentence length: mostly 15–20 words, an occasional short sentence for emphasis.
- Prefer concrete nouns and verbs ('farmers sold land to pay agents') to abstractions ('socio-economic distress').
- Use 3–5 sub-headings only if they help navigation; never number paragraphs like a report.
- Transitions between sections (Having seen the causes, we now turn to…) show orderly arrangement — a stated marking criterion.
- Leave 5 minutes per essay to read through: fix agreement, articles, spelling of names, and add one missing example if space allows.

### Self-evaluation rubric (score your practice essays out of 10)

- Relevance (2): every paragraph answers the exact topic, including both halves of a two-part topic.
- Structure (2): clear introduction with thesis, logical sections, linked conclusion.
- Content depth (2): multiple dimensions, causes not just symptoms, counter-view addressed.
- Evidence (2): accurate facts with years, apt examples from Punjab, India and the world.
- Expression (2): correct grammar, precise words, no padding, legible.
- Track scores weekly; repeat any topic type where you score below 6.

### How the PPSC essay paper is marked

- Paper size: 150 marks, three essays of 50 marks each; the 2025 cycle paper (held 2026) printed an 800-word limit per essay and the 2020 and 2018 papers did the same, so treat 800 words as the working target unless the paper says otherwise.
- Choice rules change: the 2018 and 2020 papers said 'attempt any three' from six topics; the 2025 cycle paper had three parts (A, B, C) of four topics each and required at least one essay from each part. Read the instructions before choosing.
- Language: English or Punjabi; the 2025 cycle paper required all three essays in the same language. Do not switch language between essays.
- Official criteria (PPSC scheme): keep closely to the subject, arrange ideas in an orderly fashion, write concisely; credit is given for content value and for effective and exact expression. Each criterion maps to a habit: relevance → decode the topic; order → outline first; concision → no padding; exactness → precise words and correct facts.
- The essay counts fully in merit: it is 150 of the 1,350 written marks (Punjabi 100, English 100, Essay 150, GS I–IV 250 each), and the written total plus the 150-mark interview decides rank.
- A practical (unofficial) way to judge your own script: about one-third for content and argument, one-quarter for structure and coherence, one-quarter for language and expression, and the rest for relevance and balance of the conclusion — use it only for self-assessment.
- What pulls marks down most often: a GS-style answer with headings and bullet lists instead of connected prose, a topic only half answered (e.g. 'empowerment' discussed but 'surveillance' ignored), factual errors in figures, and an abrupt ending when time runs out.
- Quote-type topics (Part B of 2025) are judged on depth of thought: the examiner wants the idea explained, tested at several levels and qualified — not a collection of quotations.
- Punjab-specific topics (e.g. the 2025 essay on Punjab's fiscal health) reward state data with year and source; one accurate figure is worth more than five rounded guesses.
- Punjabi-medium writers are judged on correct Gurmukhi spelling (shudh shabad-jor), punctuation and idiom as well as content; keep technical terms in English in brackets only where needed.
- Presentation is not a stated criterion, but an examiner reading hundreds of scripts rewards legible handwriting, clear paragraph breaks and a neat outline that is struck through.

### Three-hour time plan for three essays

- 0–10 min: read every topic in all parts; mark each 'strong / possible / avoid'; for the two finalists in each part, jot five points in 60 seconds — the topic that yields more points wins.
- Order of writing: start with your strongest topic to bank marks and confidence; leave the abstract or quote topic for second when your mind is warmed up; finish with the topic that has the clearest data (it is easier to write when tired).
- Per essay (about 55 min each): 7 min brainstorm on the dimensions grid, 5 min outline with paragraph order and the Punjab data point you will use, 38 min writing, 5 min reading through.
- Writing speed check: 800 words in 38 minutes needs about 21 words a minute; time yourself at home and, if you write slower, cut the brainstorm to 5 minutes rather than the revision.
- Space check: count the average words per line in your handwriting (usually 9–12) and work out how many lines make 800 words, so you can stop at the right length without counting words in the hall.
- Hard-stop rule: when 55 minutes are over, write a two- or three-sentence conclusion and move on; an unfinished third essay costs far more than a slightly shorter second one.
- Keep a 5-minute buffer at the end for headings of outlines to be struck through, missing words, spelling of names and a last check that each essay has a title or the topic number written correctly.
- If you fall behind: shorten the counter-view to two sentences and merge two body dimensions, but never drop the conclusion or the Punjab example.
- Practise the full 3-hour paper at least four times before Mains, under exam conditions with a clock; individual essays alone do not train the stamina needed for the third essay.

### A reusable nine-block structure (800 words)

- Block 1 — Hook (60–80 words): a short scene, a striking fact with year, or a paradox drawn from Punjab or India; avoid starting with a dictionary definition or a long quotation.
- Block 2 — Decode and thesis (60–80 words): explain the key words in plain language, show what the topic is really asking, and end with one sentence stating your position.
- Blocks 3–5 — Three dimensions (110–130 words each): pick the three richest from social, economic, political, ethical, environmental, technological; each block = claim → reasoning → one concrete example → link to thesis.
- Block 6 — Punjab lens (90–110 words): one paragraph that applies the topic to Punjab with at least one figure, year and source (e.g. Census 2011, CGWB 2024, Economic Survey 2025-26).
- Block 7 — Counter-view (70–90 words): state the strongest opposing argument fairly, then answer it; this block shows 'balance of judgement'.
- Block 8 — Way forward (90–110 words): four or five concrete steps across actors — citizens, community, state, Centre, market — not vague wishes.
- Block 9 — Conclusion (60–70 words): return to the hook image, restate the thesis in fresh words and end on a constitutional value or an ideal of seva, equality or honest labour.
- For a quote/abstract topic, replace Blocks 3–5 with 'levels' — individual, society/institutions, nation/world — and keep Block 6 as a Punjab illustration.
- For a policy topic ('Is X sustainable?'), use objectives → achievements → costs and unintended effects → evidence → alternatives in Blocks 3–7, and give a clear verdict in Block 9.
- Transitions that show order: 'Beyond the economy, the question is also one of…', 'Yet this is only half the picture…', 'What, then, should be done?' — one per block is enough.
- Total ≈ 800 words in 9–11 paragraphs; adjust block lengths but keep the sequence so you never have to think about structure in the hall.

## Mains practice questions (21)

**1.** Essay: 'Punjab's water crisis: from abundance to scarcity.' Discuss the causes and suggest a sustainable path for the state.

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: the name Punjab ('five waters') versus today's falling water tables; thesis: the crisis is man-made and therefore reversible.
- Causes: paddy-wheat cycle since the Green Revolution, free farm power removing the cost of pumping, MSP-assured procurement favouring paddy, urban wastage and polluted rivers.
- Impacts: deeper and costlier tubewells, rising farm debt, quality problems in groundwater, threat to food security and drinking water.
- Inter-state and national angle: river-water sharing disputes, Indus Waters Treaty in abeyance since April 2025 and debate over using eastern-river water.
- Steps taken: Punjab Preservation of Subsoil Water Act, 2009 (delayed transplanting), promotion of direct seeding of rice, crop diversification efforts.
- Way forward: maize, pulses, cotton and horticulture with assured markets; drip/sprinkler irrigation; metered or capped farm power with compensation; rainwater harvesting; treated wastewater reuse; village water budgets.
- Conclusion: water as a shared trust; combine policy incentives with community ownership.

</details>

**2.** Essay: 'Migration of youth from Punjab: aspiration or distress?'

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: Punjab's long tradition of migration (soldiers, settlers in Canada, UK and beyond); today's study-visa wave; thesis: it is both, and policy must make staying a real choice.
- Aspiration: global exposure, better incomes, remittances, diaspora networks, soft power of Punjabi culture.
- Distress: limited good jobs, stagnant farm incomes, drug problem, perceived lack of opportunity, social pressure.
- Costs: land sold or loans taken, fraud by illegal agents, ageing parents alone, brain drain, demographic imbalance in villages.
- Governance response: regulating travel agents, skill and employment programmes, attracting industry and start-ups.
- Way forward: quality higher education, agro-processing and services jobs, safe and legal migration information, engaging the diaspora for investment.
- Conclusion: migration is not the problem; migration without choice is.

</details>

**3.** Essay: 'Seva is the soul of public service.'

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: define seva as selfless service without expectation, rooted in the Sikh tradition (langar, kar seva) and other Indian traditions; thesis: administration works best when authority is used as service.
- Values: integrity, empathy, impartiality, dedication to public service; link to Constitution's ideals of justice and equality.
- Examples: community langars during disasters such as the 2025 floods, officers who simplified services for citizens, volunteers in health camps.
- Challenges: red tape, corruption, apathy, political pressure, overload of work.
- Institutional supports: citizen charters, right to services laws, grievance redress, e-governance, training in ethics.
- Counter-view: service needs systems and accountability, not only goodwill; balance motivation with rules.
- Conclusion: an officer who sees the citizen as the purpose of the job turns rules into results.

</details>

**4.** Essay: 'Debt, despair and dignity: rethinking the economics of the Punjab farmer.'

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Hook: a Malwa smallholder whose wheat and paddy fetch MSP, yet who borrows from the arhtiya for diesel, seed and a daughter's wedding — a farm that is profitable on paper but a household in debt.
- Dimensions: economic (stagnant yields, rising input and machinery costs, shrinking holdings), ecological (wheat–paddy cycle draining aquifers), social (wedding and status spending, emigration costs) and institutional (informal credit, assured procurement for only two crops).
- Punjab data: agriculture is about 23% of state GSVA but contracted about 2.5% in 2025-26 after the floods (Economic Survey 2025-26); groundwater extraction is about 157% with 115 of 153 units over-exploited (CGWB, 2024).
- Dignity: distress shows up as farmer suicides, land sales and debt among tenant farmers and landless labourers, who are often Dalit — Scheduled Castes are 31.9% of Punjab's people (Census 2011) but own little land.
- Counter-view: free power, MSP and loan waivers already protect Punjab's farmers, whose incomes are above the national average — but these mostly help larger farmers and lock the state into water-intensive paddy.
- Way forward: assured procurement or price-deficiency payments for maize, pulses and cotton; institutional credit and debt mediation; crop insurance; FPOs and agro-processing in rural clusters; direct benefit transfer in place of untargeted free power.
- Conclusion: return to the smallholder — dignity comes from a viable farm and a fair market, not from periodic rescue packages.

</details>

**5.** Essay: 'Rivers gave Punjab its name; the 2025 floods asked whether Punjab has learnt to live with them.'

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Hook: the land of five waters watched dhussi bundhs give way along the Sutlej, Beas and Ravi in August–September 2025 — the same state that worries daily about falling water tables.
- Scale: on 2 September 2025 all 23 districts were declared flood-affected; about 1,400 villages and 3.5 lakh people were hit and crops on over 4 lakh acres submerged; agriculture shrank about 2.5% in 2025-26 (Economic Survey 2025-26).
- Causes: extreme rain in the Himachal and Jammu & Kashmir catchments, high releases from Bhakra, Pong and Ranjit Sagar, encroachment and illegal sand mining in floodplains, weak embankments and choked drains.
- Dimensions: governance (early warning, BBMB–state coordination), economic (crop, livestock and house losses, fresh debt), social (displacement, disease, children's schooling) and federal (central relief of Rs 1,600 crore announced in September 2025).
- Counter-view: floods are natural events and higher embankments or bigger dams will solve them — but purely structural fixes push risk downstream and give a false sense of safety.
- Way forward: floodplain zoning and removal of riverbed encroachments, pre-monsoon embankment audits, real-time reservoir data shared with districts, village disaster plans and volunteers, crop and house insurance, restoration of wetlands and natural drainage.
- Use Punjab's strengths: gurdwara langars, diaspora donations and youth volunteers that carried relief in 2025 — channel them into planned preparedness.
- Conclusion: a land named after rivers must give its rivers room; living with water means planning for both scarcity and excess.

</details>

**6.** Essay: 'A generation at risk: the war on drugs must be won in homes and schools, not only at the border.'

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Hook: a cricket kit gathering dust in a village home, its owner in a de-addiction centre — the human face behind seizure statistics.
- Supply side: cross-border trafficking by drones; the BSF reported seizing 272 drones and over 367 kg of heroin on the 553 km Punjab border in 2025 (to late November); the state's 'Yudh Nashian Virudh' drive began in March 2025.
- Demand side: joblessness, peer pressure, easy money, emigration stress and family breakdown; the Punjab Opioid Dependence Survey (2015) estimated about 2.3 lakh opioid-dependent people.
- Health approach: addiction as a disease — outpatient opioid-assisted treatment clinics (from 2017), rehabilitation and aftercare; stigma keeps families silent until it is too late.
- Home and school: early warning signs, counsellors, life-skills education, sports and mentoring; panchayats and gurdwara committees as community watch without vigilantism.
- Counter-view: only harsh policing works — but arrests without treatment refill jails and do not cut demand; the NDPS Act itself offers immunity to addicts who volunteer for treatment (Section 64A).
- Way forward: a three-track policy — choke supply, cut demand, reduce harm — with hotspot mapping, skilling and local jobs, playgrounds, and reintegration of recovered youth.
- Conclusion: the state can guard the border; only families, teachers and communities can guard a child's mind.

</details>

**7.** Essay: 'Empty courtyards and ageing parents: the hidden social cost of emigration from rural Punjab.'

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Hook: locked kothis in a Doaba village, their gates bearing the names of Canadian cities, while an elderly couple inside waits for a weekly video call.
- Context: from the old Doaba 'NRI belt' to the post-2010 study-visa boom across Malwa and Majha; IELTS centres in small towns; Canada's 2024 cap on study permits; the first US deportation flight landed at Amritsar on 5 February 2025.
- Social costs: elders without care, land sold or mortgaged to pay fees and agents, risky 'dunki' routes, abandoned brides in NRI marriages (the Law Commission's 287th Report, 2024, urged compulsory registration), villages losing young leaders.
- Economic side: remittances build houses, schools and gurdwaras — India received about US$129 billion in 2024 (World Bank) — but much goes into consumption and land rather than productive local enterprise.
- Demographic angle: Punjab's fertility is already low (TFR 1.6, NFHS-5, 2019-21), so emigration of the young accelerates ageing.
- Counter-view: migration is a legitimate aspiration and a right; diaspora skills and capital can return — 'brain circulation' rather than 'brain drain'.
- Way forward: strict licensing of travel agents under the Punjab Travel Professionals (Regulation) Act, 2014; safe legal pathways and pre-departure training; elder day-care and tele-health; matching grants for NRI-funded village works; local jobs so that leaving is a choice, not a compulsion.
- Conclusion: a Punjab that sends its children abroad must also care for those who remain — prosperity measured in remittances alone is hollow.

</details>

**8.** Essay: 'Mother tongue, global tongue: securing the future of Punjabi in a connected world.'

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Hook: a grandchild in Brampton who understands Punjabi but cannot read the Gurmukhi on her grandfather's letters.
- Heritage: Punjabi has been in the Eighth Schedule since 1950; Gurmukhi was standardised under Guru Angad Dev; a literature running from Baba Farid and Guru Nanak to Waris Shah, Amrita Pritam and Shiv Kumar Batalvi.
