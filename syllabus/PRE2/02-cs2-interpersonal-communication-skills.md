# Interpersonal & communication skills

> Official syllabus line: Interpersonal skills including communication skills.

## Short notes

- Communication process: sender encodes a message, sends it through a channel, receiver decodes it and gives feedback; 'noise' is anything that distorts it.
- Barriers: physical (noise, distance), semantic (jargon, language), psychological (anger, prejudice), organisational (too many levels), cultural.
- 7 Cs of effective communication: clear, concise, concrete, correct, coherent, complete, courteous.
- Active listening: full attention, no interrupting, paraphrase to confirm, ask open questions, note non-verbal cues.
- Non-verbal communication (tone, posture, eye contact, gestures) often carries the emotional message; mismatch with words reduces trust.
- Assertive style states needs firmly and respectfully; aggressive ignores others' rights; passive suppresses one's own. Exams reward assertive.
- Emotional intelligence (Daniel Goleman): self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy and social skills.
- Empathy = understanding another person's feelings from their point of view; sympathy = feeling sorry for them. Officers are expected to show empathy.
- Johari window: Open (known to self and others), Blind (known to others, not self), Hidden (known to self, not others), Unknown (known to neither). Feedback shrinks the Blind area.
- Conflict styles (Thomas-Kilmann): competing, collaborating, compromising, avoiding, accommodating. Collaborating seeks a win-win solution.
- Situational questions: choose the option that solves the problem, follows rules, respects people and does not pass the buck.
- Reject extremes: ignoring the issue, immediate harsh punishment, giving in to pressure, or escalating before trying to resolve at your level.
- Best answers usually combine listening, verifying facts, acting within the law, and communicating the decision with reasons.
- Feedback should be specific, timely, about behaviour not personality, and given in private when critical.
- Team skills: delegate with clear goals, credit the team publicly, correct individuals privately, keep commitments.
- Downward, upward, horizontal and diagonal are directions of formal communication; the 'grapevine' is informal communication.

## Punjab angle

- Field officers in Punjab often handle farmer protests, dharnas and village disputes, so situational questions on calm, lawful dialogue are directly relevant.
- Bilingual communication (Punjabi with the public, English in files) is a practical interpersonal skill for PCS officers.

## How PPSC asks it

Questions are mostly situational: a citizen, colleague or crowd scenario with four responses. Pick the response that is lawful, empathetic, solution-oriented and proportionate; also learn basic terms (Johari window, barriers, 7 Cs, EI components).

## Deep dive

### What PPSC asks — pattern

- 2021 paper: three concept-based items — the communication style of someone who opens with 'I may be wrong, but...' (passive or passive-aggressive styles), a win-win negotiator who listens before bargaining, and an 'evaluative' listening style.
- 2025 paper: no separately identifiable interpersonal items — the topic can vanish or reappear, so prepare the core concepts in a few hours rather than weeks.
- UPSC CSAT 2011-2013 carried 'decision-making' situational items; PPSC's syllabus wording ('interpersonal skills including communication skills') allows both concept and situation questions.
- Expect vocabulary from organisational behaviour: communication styles, listening types, negotiation types, barriers, feedback, conflict styles, group development, transactional analysis.
- Marks value: even 2-4 questions are worth 5-10 marks — easy marks if the terms are known.
- Scoring rule for situations: choose the option that is lawful, empathetic, proportionate and solves the problem at your level.

### Concept tables — styles, listening, negotiation

- Communication styles: passive (avoids expressing needs, over-apologises), aggressive (dominates, blames, ignores others' rights), passive-aggressive (appears to agree but resists indirectly — sarcasm, delay, sulking), assertive (states needs and limits clearly while respecting others; uses 'I' statements).
- Listening types: active or reflective (attends, paraphrases, asks clarifying questions), empathetic (understands feelings), evaluative or critical (judges the merit of arguments), informational (to learn facts), appreciative (for enjoyment), selective (hears only what fits expectations), pseudo-listening (pretends to listen).
- Negotiation types: distributive (fixed pie, win-lose bargaining over one issue such as price) vs integrative (win-win, expands value by trading across several issues).
- Principled negotiation (Fisher and Ury, 'Getting to Yes', 1981): separate the people from the problem, focus on interests not positions, invent options for mutual gain, insist on objective criteria.
- BATNA — best alternative to a negotiated agreement — sets your walk-away power; the reservation point is the worst deal you will still accept; ZOPA is the zone where both parties' reservation points overlap.
- Conflict-handling (Thomas-Kilmann) is about assertiveness vs cooperativeness: competing (high-low), accommodating (low-high), avoiding (low-low), collaborating (high-high), compromising (middle).
- Group development (Bruce Tuckman, 1965; 'adjourning' added 1977): forming, storming, norming, performing, adjourning.

### Non-verbal and analytical models

- Non-verbal channels: kinesics (body movement, gestures, posture), oculesics (eye contact), paralanguage (tone, pitch, pauses), proxemics (use of space), haptics (touch), chronemics (use of time — punctuality signals respect), appearance and artefacts.
- Edward T. Hall's proxemic zones: intimate (up to about 45 cm), personal (about 45 cm-1.2 m), social (about 1.2-3.6 m, formal and official dealings), public (beyond about 3.6 m, speeches).
- Albert Mehrabian's 7-38-55 finding (words, tone, body language) applies only to messages about feelings when words and tone conflict — it does not mean words carry only 7% of all meaning.
- Transactional analysis (Eric Berne): ego states Parent (critical or nurturing), Adult (rational, factual), Child (free or adapted); complementary transactions keep communication flowing, crossed transactions cause conflict; 'I'm OK - You're OK' (Thomas Harris) is the healthy life position.
- Johari window and Goleman's emotional-intelligence components are already in your short notes — revise them with these models.
- Feedback models: SBI (Situation-Behaviour-Impact) for specific, non-personal feedback; the 'sandwich' (positive-corrective-positive) is popular but can dilute the message.
- Barriers to remember with examples: semantic (jargon, language mismatch), perceptual (stereotypes), emotional (anger, fear), physical (noise, distance), organisational (hierarchy, too many levels), cultural (gestures and norms differ).

### Answering situational (decision-making) items — a rubric

- Step 1 — Identify the stakeholder whose interest is primary (citizen in distress, public safety, rule of law).
- Step 2 — Remove options that are illegal, unethical, or escalate force first.
- Step 3 — Remove options that pass the buck ('tell him to go to another office') or ignore the problem ('wait and see').
- Step 4 — Remove unauthorised promises or favours (promising compensation on the spot, bending rules for a relative).
- Step 5 — Of the remaining, prefer the one that combines listening, fact-checking and acting within your powers, with communication of reasons.
- Language cues: options with 'immediately punish', 'ignore', 'refuse to meet' are usually wrong; options with 'listen', 'verify', 'explain', 'within rules' are usually right.
- Time: these items are quick (30-45 seconds) — do them in the first pass with language questions.

### Common traps

- Passive vs passive-aggressive: passive gives in openly; passive-aggressive agrees on the surface but resists indirectly.
- Evaluative listening judges arguments; empathetic listening focuses on feelings — PPSC 2021 tested this distinction.
- Compromising (each side gives up something) is not the same as collaborating (both sides fully satisfied).
- BATNA is not your bottom price; it is your best option outside the deal.
- Sympathy (feeling sorry) is not empathy (understanding from the other's perspective).
- Grapevine (informal channel) is not always harmful — it is fast and often accurate, but can spread rumours.

## Practice MCQs (20)

**1.** An elderly villager comes to your office upset that his pension has stopped. He speaks angrily and loudly. What should you do first?

(a) Ask him to come back when he is calm  
(b) Warn him that misbehaviour in office is an offence  
(c) Calm him, listen to his full grievance and check his record  
(d) Tell him pensions are handled by another branch

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Calm him, listen to his full grievance and check his record** — Effective interpersonal handling starts with de-escalation and active listening, followed by fact-checking. Sending him away ignores a genuine grievance; redirecting without help passes the buck; a warning escalates conflict. The option “Calm him, listen to his full grievance and…” addresses both his emotion and the problem.

</details>

**2.** Your subordinate repeatedly submits reports late. Which response reflects good feedback practice?

(a) Criticise him in the weekly staff meeting as an example to others  
(b) Immediately issue a written warning without discussion  
(c) Meet privately, point to specific delays, ask the reasons and agree on a timeline  
(d) Quietly do his work yourself to avoid friction

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Meet privately, point to specific delays, ask the reasons and agree on a timeline** — Good feedback is private, specific, about behaviour and two-way, and ends in an agreed plan. Public criticism humiliates; doing his work avoids the problem; an instant warning skips finding the cause. The option “Meet privately, point to specific delays, …” meets all feedback principles.

</details>

**3.** In the Johari window, the area that is known to others but not known to oneself is called:

(a) Unknown area  
(b) Blind area  
(c) Hidden area  
(d) Open area

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Blind area** — The Johari window has four panes. Open: known to self and others. Hidden: known to self only. Unknown: known to neither. Blind: others see it but you do not, and it shrinks when you seek feedback.

</details>

**4.** A group of farmers blocks a road near the tehsil office demanding compensation for crop loss. As the officer on the spot, the most appropriate first step is to:

(a) Promise full compensation on the spot to end the blockade  
(b) Order the police to clear the road by force at once  
(c) Meet their leaders, hear the demand and explain the process  
(d) Ignore the blockade until they tire and leave

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Meet their leaders, hear the demand and explain the process** — The balanced response opens dialogue, gives accurate information and keeps the issue within lawful process. Force first is disproportionate; ignoring lets the public suffer and the protest grow; an unauthorised promise is beyond your power and damages credibility later.

</details>

**5.** Which of the following is NOT one of the five components of emotional intelligence described by Daniel Goleman?

(a) Intelligence quotient  
(b) Social skills  
(c) Empathy  
(d) Self-regulation

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) Intelligence quotient** — Goleman's five components are self-awareness, self-regulation, motivation, empathy and social skills. IQ measures cognitive ability and is a separate construct; EI was proposed precisely as distinct from IQ.

</details>

**6.** Two of your team members disagree strongly on how to run a survey and the work has stalled. Which conflict-handling style is most likely to give a lasting solution?

(a) Avoiding: let them sort it out over time  
(b) Competing: decide yourself and order both to comply without discussion  
(c) Collaborating: bring both together to build a plan using the best of each idea  
(d) Accommodating: accept whichever member is louder to keep peace

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Collaborating: bring both together to build a plan using the best of each idea** — Collaborating addresses both parties' concerns and produces a win-win plan with buy-in, so it lasts. Avoiding delays work; competing may be needed in emergencies but breeds resentment; accommodating rewards pressure rather than merit.

</details>

**7.** At a review meeting, a section officer says 'Fine, do it your way,' but later deliberately delays the work and grumbles about the decision to colleagues. His communication style is best described as:

(a) Passive-aggressive  
(b) Passive  
(c) Aggressive  
(d) Assertive

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) Passive-aggressive** — Working: on the surface he agrees (looks passive), but he resists indirectly through delay and complaints — that hidden resistance is the mark of passive-aggressive behaviour. A passive person would comply without resisting; an aggressive person would openly attack the decision; an assertive person would state his disagreement clearly and respectfully in the meeting.

</details>

**8.** In Fisher and Ury's negotiation framework, BATNA refers to:

(a) The lowest price you are prepared to accept  
(b) The agenda both sides agree before talks begin  
(c) Best alternative to a negotiated agreement  
(d) A binding award given by an outside arbitrator

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Best alternative to a negotiated agreement** — BATNA = Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement. It is your fallback outside the deal (for example, another buyer or a court case) and it decides your bargaining power. The lowest acceptable price is the reservation point, which is derived from the BATNA but is not the same thing; agendas and arbitration awards are unrelated.

</details>

**9.** Consider the following statements:  
1. In integrative negotiation, parties treat the total value as fixed and focus on claiming the largest possible share of it.  
2. In distributive negotiation, one party's gain is broadly the other party's loss.  
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) 2 only** — Statement 1 is wrong: treating the pie as fixed and claiming a share describes distributive bargaining; integrative (win-win) negotiation enlarges the total value by trading on issues the parties value differently. Statement 2 is correct: in distributive (win-lose) bargaining, such as haggling over a single price, one side's gain is broadly the other's loss.

</details>

**10.** A newly formed district task force on drug abuse spends its second week in heated arguments over roles and priorities. According to Tuckman's model, the team is in the:

(a) Norming stage  
(b) Storming stage  
(c) Performing stage  
(d) Forming stage

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Storming stage** — Tuckman's sequence: forming (polite orientation), storming (conflict over roles, leadership and goals), norming (agreed rules and cohesion), performing (focused work), adjourning (closure). Open conflict over roles after initial formation is the storming stage.

</details>

**11.** Consider the following statements: 1. Edward T. Hall described 'social distance' as roughly 1.2 to 3.6 metres, typical of formal and official interactions. 2. Mehrabian's 7-38-55 rule shows that in all communication words carry only 7% of the meaning. Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) 1 only** — Statement 1 is correct: Hall's zones are intimate (to about 45 cm), personal (about 45 cm-1.2 m), social (about 1.2-3.6 m) and public (beyond about 3.6 m). Statement 2 is a common misreading: Mehrabian's experiments dealt only with messages about feelings and attitudes when words and tone conflicted; he warned against applying the figures to all communication. So only 1 is correct.

</details>

**12.** Officer: 'What time will the flood-relief report be ready?' Clerk: 'By 4 pm, sir, after the tehsildar's figures arrive.' In Eric Berne's transactional analysis this exchange is a:

(a) Complementary Parent-to-Parent transaction  
(b) Complementary Adult-to-Adult transaction  
(c) Ulterior Child-to-Child transaction  
(d) Crossed Parent-to-Child transaction

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Complementary Adult-to-Adult transaction** — Working: the officer asks a factual question from the Adult ego state and expects an Adult reply; the clerk answers factually from the Adult state. When the response comes from the state addressed and goes back to the state that sent it, the transaction is complementary — here Adult to Adult. A crossed transaction would be a reply such as 'Why are you always rushing me?' (Child or Parent).

</details>

**13.** A woman who speaks only Punjabi is struggling to explain her pension problem to a clerk at a Sewa Kendra who replies only in English. As the supervising officer, what should you do first?

(a) Advise her to file the complaint online herself  
(b) Speak to her in Punjabi and record her grievance  
(c) Ask her to come back with someone who knows English  
(d) Tell the clerk to hand her a printed English form

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Speak to her in Punjabi and record her grievance** — Apply the rubric: the barrier here is semantic (language). The option “Speak to her in Punjabi and record her gri…” removes the barrier, solves the problem at your level and coaches the clerk. The option “Ask her to come back with someone who know…” passes the burden to the citizen; the option “Tell the clerk to hand her a printed Engli…” repeats the same barrier on paper; the option “Advise her to file the complaint online he…” adds a digital barrier for someone already struggling. So the answer is “Speak to her in Punjabi and record her gri…”.

</details>

**14.** During a public hearing, the SDM notes each complaint, repeats it in his own words and asks, 'Have I understood you correctly?' This is an example of:

(a) Pseudo-listening  
(b) Evaluative listening  
(c) Active (reflective) listening  
(d) Selective listening

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(c) Active (reflective) listening** — Paraphrasing and checking understanding are the defining techniques of active (reflective) listening. Selective listening hears only part of the message, evaluative listening judges the merit of arguments, and pseudo-listening only pretends to attend — none involves repeating back to confirm.

</details>

**15.** A naib tehsildar has handled crop-damage surveys (girdawari) for many years, knows the work thoroughly and is keen to take full responsibility for this season's survey. According to Hersey and Blanchard's situational leadership model, which style suits him best?

(a) Selling (coaching)  
(b) Delegating  
(c) Telling (directing)  
(d) Participating (supporting)

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) Delegating** — Step 1: the model matches style to the follower's readiness (ability plus willingness); an officer who is both highly able and willing is at the highest readiness level. Step 2: for him the leader gives low task direction and low relationship support — delegating; telling suits those unable and unsure, selling those still learning, and participating those able but hesitant. Answer: delegating.

</details>

**16.** At a village meeting an officer explains a new scheme using phrases such as 'convergence mode', 'saturation approach' and 'DBT-enabled'. The villagers nod politely, but many later fill the forms wrongly. The main barrier to communication here is:

(a) Organisational barrier  
(b) Emotional barrier  
(c) Physical barrier  
(d) Semantic barrier

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) Semantic barrier** — Step 1: the message failed because the listeners did not share the meaning of the technical words used — a problem of language and meaning. Step 2: there was no noise or distance (physical), no hierarchy or channel problem (organisational) and no anger or fear (emotional). Answer: semantic barrier.

</details>

**17.** Consider the following statements:  
1. Empathetic listening means agreeing with the speaker's opinion so that he feels supported.  
2. A statement such as 'I am worried when the reports come late, because the DC's review gets delayed' is a typical feature of assertive communication.  
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(b) 2 only** — Empathetic listening means understanding the speaker's feelings and point of view, not agreeing with it, so the first statement is wrong. The second is right: an 'I-statement' names one's own feeling, the specific behaviour and its effect without blaming — the hallmark of assertive communication. Answer: 2 only.

</details>

**18.** Rumours that the sub-division office will be shifted to another town spread quickly among the staff through informal chats before any order is issued. Which is the most effective way for the SDM to deal with this grapevine?

(a) Ignore the rumours, since the grapevine carries no real information  
(b) Forbid all informal discussion among staff during office hours  
(c) Find the person who started the rumour and take strict action  
(d) Share accurate information early and use informal talks to correct rumours

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(d) Share accurate information early and use informal talks to correct rumours** — Step 1: the grapevine is fast and cannot be abolished; rumours grow where official information is missing, so the remedy is timely, accurate communication plus using informal networks to correct errors. Step 2: banning talk or punishing the source drives rumours underground, and ignoring them is wrong because the grapevine often carries partly true information. Answer: “Share accurate information early and use i…”.

</details>

**19.** Which of the following feedback statements best follows the Situation–Behaviour–Impact (SBI) model?

(a) In yesterday's flood review your figures did not match the tehsil report, so the DC doubted our data  
(b) You are always careless with numbers, and that reflects badly on the whole office here  
(c) Please try to be far more professional in future meetings, as we have discussed before  
(d) Others in this office are much better at handling data and figures than you are

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) In yesterday's flood review your figures did not match the tehsil report, so the DC doubted our data** — Step 1: SBI feedback names a specific situation (yesterday's flood review), the observable behaviour (figures did not match the tehsil report) and its impact (the DC questioned the data). Step 2: the option “You are always careless with numbers, and …” generalises ('always') and judges character; the option “Please try to be far more professional in …” is vague; the option “Others in this office are much better at h…” compares with others. Answer: “In yesterday's flood review your figures d…”.

</details>

**20.** Consider the following statements about the Thomas–Kilmann conflict modes:  
1. Compromising lies at an intermediate level of both assertiveness and cooperativeness.  
2. Accommodating combines high assertiveness with low cooperativeness.  
Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only  
(b) 2 only  
(c) Both 1 and 2  
(d) Neither 1 nor 2

<details><summary>Answer</summary>

**(a) 1 only** — Compromising — each side gives up something — sits in the middle on both assertiveness and cooperativeness, so the first statement is right. The second is wrong: accommodating is low on assertiveness and high on cooperativeness (yielding to the other side); high assertiveness with low cooperativeness describes competing. Answer: 1 only.

</details>
