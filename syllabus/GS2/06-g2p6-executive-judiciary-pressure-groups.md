## Short notes

- Union executive: President (Art 52–62; elected by electoral college of elected MPs and MLAs), Vice-President, PM and Council of Ministers (Art 74–75), Attorney General (Art 76).
- Art 74(1): President acts on aid and advice (binding after 42nd; 44th allowed one reconsideration). Council of Ministers capped at 15% of Lok Sabha/Assembly strength (91st Amendment 2003; states minimum 12).
- State executive: Governor (Art 153–162), Chief Minister and Council of Ministers (Art 163–164), Advocate General (Art 165); Governor's discretion limited (Nabam Rebia 2016).
- Ministries and departments: Government of India (Allocation of Business) Rules 1961 and Transaction of Business Rules; Cabinet Secretariat (Cabinet Secretary as head of civil services); PMO; attached and subordinate offices.
- Cabinet committees (e.g. CCS, Appointments Committee of the Cabinet) are extra-constitutional but central to decision-making.
- Judiciary: integrated single hierarchy — Supreme Court (Art 124; sanctioned strength 34 including CJI since 2019), High Courts (Art 214), subordinate courts.
- Appointments: collegium system from Second (1993) and Third (1998) Judges cases; NJAC struck down (2015); Memorandum of Procedure still debated.
- Judicial independence: security of tenure, salaries charged on Consolidated Fund, removal only by Parliament (Judges Inquiry Act 1968), contempt powers (Art 129/215).
- Issues: vacancies, pendency, lack of diversity, post-retirement appointments, transparency of collegium, judicial accountability (no Judicial Standards Bill in force).
- Pressure groups: organised groups seeking to influence policy without contesting elections — business (FICCI, CII, ASSOCHAM), labour (INTUC, AITUC, BMS, CITU), farmers (BKU factions, Samyukt Kisan Morcha), professionals (IMA, Bar Councils), students (ABVP, NSUI, SFI).
- Types (Almond and Powell): institutional, associational, non-associational (caste, religion), anomic (spontaneous protests).
- Techniques: lobbying, petitions, media campaigns, litigation (PIL), protests, strikes; electioneering support.
- Positive role: interest articulation, feedback to government, expertise in committee consultations, check on state.
- Negative role: narrow interest capture, disruption, caste/communal mobilisation, unequal access (business vs informal workers), absence of lobbying regulation in India.
- Formal/informal associations: RWAs, cooperatives, self-help groups, religious bodies, caste panchayats/khaps — some enhance participation, some undermine constitutional values.

## Punjab angle

- Samyukt Kisan Morcha and Punjab's farm unions led the 2020–21 protests that ended with repeal of the three farm laws (Nov 2021) — classic pressure group case study.
- SGPC, constituted under the Sikh Gurdwaras Act 1925, is an elected religious body with major social and political influence.
- Arhtiya (commission agent) associations and rice-sheller groups influence procurement policy in Punjab mandis.
- Punjab's executive: Chief Minister and Council of Ministers; Punjab Civil Secretariat at Chandigarh; Governor of Punjab also acts as Administrator of UT Chandigarh.
- Dera organisations and ex-servicemen associations act as informal pressure groups in Punjab politics.

## Deep dive

### Union and state executive — offices and recent changes

- President Droupadi Murmu (since 25 July 2022) — first tribal woman President; Vice-President C.P. Radhakrishnan (elected Sept 2025) after Jagdeep Dhankhar resigned on health grounds (21 July 2025).
- Narendra Modi's third term began in June 2024 with a coalition (NDA) majority; Cabinet Secretary T.V. Somanathan (from Aug 2024); Attorney General R. Venkataramani (from Oct 2022).
- Chief Justice of India Surya Kant — 53rd CJI, sworn in on 24 Nov 2025.
- Art 74(2) — courts cannot inquire into the advice given by ministers; Art 77(3) — Allocation of Business and Transaction of Business Rules (1961).
- Cabinet committees (reconstituted 2019): Appointments, Accommodation, Economic Affairs, Parliamentary Affairs, Political Affairs, Security, Investment and Growth, Employment and Skill Development.
- Shamsher Singh (1974) — President and Governor act on ministerial advice except where the Constitution gives discretion.
- Governor and Bills (Nov 2025 reference opinion): three options under Art 200; no "pocket veto"; courts will not set timelines but can examine prolonged unexplained inaction.

### Ministries and departments — structure

- Schedule to the Allocation of Business Rules lists ministries and departments; recent additions: Ministry of AYUSH (2014), Skill Development (2014), Jal Shakti (2019), Department of Military Affairs (2020, headed by the CDS), Ministry of Cooperation (2021).
- Hierarchy: Secretary → Additional Secretary → Joint Secretary → Director/Deputy Secretary → Under Secretary → Section Officer.
- Attached offices (e.g. CPWD, Directorate General of Foreign Trade) give technical guidance; subordinate offices are field units.
- Cabinet Secretariat coordinates; the PMO advises the PM; NITI Aayog (2015) replaced the Planning Commission by executive resolution.
- Lateral entry at Joint Secretary level began in 2018; a 2024 UPSC advertisement for 45 posts was withdrawn after objections about reservation.
- Mission Karmayogi (2020) — competency-based training through the iGOT platform; Capacity Building Commission.
- 2nd ARC 13th Report recommended grouping ministries into about 20–25 coherent clusters.

### Judiciary — appointments and administration

- Judges cases: First (S.P. Gupta, 1981 — executive primacy), Second (1993 — CJI primacy), Third (1998 — collegium of CJI and four senior-most judges).
- Memorandum of Procedure on appointments is still not finalised after the NJAC verdict (2015).
- Women: Justice B.V. Nagarathna is in line to become the first woman CJI (2027).
- Ad hoc retired judges in High Courts under Art 224A were permitted by the SC (Lok Prahari, 2021) to cut pendency.
- Transparency steps: collegium resolutions online (from 2017); judges' asset declarations on the SC website (2025).
- Justice Yashwant Varma inquiry (2025–26) is the first judge-removal process since Justice Soumitra Sen's (2011) in which an inquiry committee found the charges proved — though the judge resigned before Parliament voted.
- All India Judicial Service (Art 312, needs a Rajya Sabha resolution) remains contested by states and High Courts.

### Pressure groups — theory and Indian examples

- Interest groups articulate demands; pressure groups try to influence policy without seeking office; parties seek office.
- Myron Weiner's The Politics of Scarcity (1962) studied Indian organised interests; the Rudolphs (In Pursuit of Lakshmi, 1987) called them "demand groups".
- Movements that changed law: Chipko (1973), Narmada Bachao Andolan (1985), MKSS and the RTI campaign (1990s → RTI Act 2005), India Against Corruption (2011 → Lokpal Act 2013), Nirbhaya protests (2012 → Criminal Law Amendment 2013).
- Farmers' protest (Nov 2020 – Dec 2021) led to repeal of the three farm laws — a rare reversal of enacted law by mass pressure.
- Ladakh's Leh Apex Body and Kargil Democratic Alliance press for statehood and Sixth Schedule status (agitation of 2025).
- Regulation: India has no lobbying law; FCRA amendments (2020) tightened foreign funding of NGOs.
- Business associations (CII, FICCI, ASSOCHAM) are consulted on budgets; trade unions (BMS largest) shape labour code debates.

### Punjab — executive, judiciary and pressure groups

- Chief Minister Bhagwant Mann (since 16 March 2022); Governor Gulab Chand Kataria (since 31 July 2024), who is also Administrator of Chandigarh.
- Punjab's executive–Governor friction (2022–23) over Assembly sessions, university Bills and the Police (Amendment) Bill ended in Supreme Court rulings.
- Kisan Mazdoor Morcha / KMSC and SKM (non-political) camped at Shambhu and Khanauri borders from Feb 2024; Jagjit Singh Dallewal's fast began 26 Nov 2024; Punjab Police cleared the sites on 19 March 2025.
- SGPC (elected under the Sikh Gurdwaras Act 1925) — last general house elections in 2011; its voter registration for new polls has been slow.
- Akal Takht edicts (e.g. on Sukhbir Badal, 2024) show the political weight of religious institutions.
- Deras (Dera Sacha Sauda, Radha Soami Satsang Beas, Dera Sachkhand Ballan of the Ravidassia community) act as informal vote-influencers.
- Arhtiyas (commission agents), rice millers and employees' unions shape procurement and pay policy; ex-servicemen's bodies are influential given the large veteran population.

### Issues and reforms

- Executive dominance over legislature (ordinances, Money Bills) and over appointments to independent bodies.
- Governor neutrality: Sarkaria and Punchhi suggested eminent non-political persons and consultation with the CM.
- Police reforms (Prakash Singh, 2006): State Security Commission, fixed DGP tenure, Police Establishment Board, Police Complaints Authority — compliance remains partial.
- Judicial vacancies and pendency; demand for a National Judicial Infrastructure Authority.
- Pressure groups: good for interest articulation and accountability but risk capture by organised elites; need a transparent lobbying register.
- Civil-service reform: performance appraisal, domain expertise, lateral entry with reservation safeguards.

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** Pressure groups are often called the 'anonymous empire'. Evaluate their role in Indian democracy with reference to the farmers' movement of 2020–21. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: definition and difference from political parties (S.E. Finer's phrase).
- Typology and techniques.

- Positive: voice for producers, policy feedback, consultation deficit exposed.

**2.** Critically analyse the collegium system of judicial appointments. What reforms would ensure both independence and accountability? (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: Art 124, 217 'consultation'; Judges cases.
- Merits: insulates judiciary from executive.
- Criticism: opacity, nepotism allegations, delays, lack of diversity, executive sitting on files.
- NJAC 2015 judgment; MoP stalemate.
- Reforms: secretariat, published criteria, time-bound processing, broader consultation, diversity goals.
- Conclusion: 'independence with transparency'.

</details>

**3.** Discuss the constitutional position of the President of India vis-à-vis the Council of Ministers. (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: nominal head; Art 53, 74.
- Binding advice after 42nd/44th Amendments; reconsideration once.
- Situational discretion: appointment of PM in hung House, pocket veto (Art 111), Art 78 right to information.
- Examples: pocket veto of Indian Post Office (Amendment) Bill 1986.
- Conclusion: constitutional head with moral influence.

</details>
