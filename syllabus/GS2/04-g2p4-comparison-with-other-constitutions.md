## Short notes

- India drew on many constitutions; Ambedkar called it a selective adaptation suited to Indian conditions.
- UK: parliamentary government, rule of law, single citizenship, Speaker, cabinet system, writs, bicameralism, legislative procedure.
- USA: Fundamental Rights, judicial review, independent judiciary, impeachment of President, removal of SC/HC judges, Vice-President as chair of upper house, Preamble idea.
- Ireland: Directive Principles, nomination of members to Rajya Sabha, method of electing the President.
- Canada: federation with strong Centre, residuary powers with Centre, Governor appointed by Centre, advisory jurisdiction of Supreme Court.
- Australia: Concurrent List, freedom of trade and commerce, joint sitting of Houses; Weimar Germany: suspension of FRs during Emergency; USSR: Fundamental Duties, ideals of justice in Preamble.
- France: republic and ideals of liberty, equality, fraternity; South Africa: amendment procedure, election of Rajya Sabha members; Japan: 'procedure established by law' (Art 21).
- UK vs India: UK has an uncodified constitution and parliamentary sovereignty; India has a written constitution, constitutional supremacy and judicial review limited by basic structure.
- US vs India: US presidential system with fixed term and strict separation; India parliamentary with collective responsibility; US has dual citizenship and dual court system, India single citizenship and integrated judiciary.
- US Senate gives equal representation to states; India's Rajya Sabha is population-based, making Indian federalism less 'state-equal'.
- US 'due process of law' vs India's 'procedure established by law'; Maneka Gandhi (1978) read substantive due process into Art 21.
- Amendment: US needs ratification by three-fourths of states (very rigid); India's Art 368 is partly flexible; UK by ordinary Act of Parliament.
- Federalism: US 'indestructible union of indestructible states' (coming together); India 'indestructible union of destructible states' (holding together; Art 3).
- France semi-presidential (directly elected President and PM); Switzerland uses direct democracy (referendum, initiative), absent in India's national scheme.
- Judicial appointments: US by President with Senate confirmation; UK by Judicial Appointments Commission; India by collegium (Second and Third Judges cases).
- Emergency powers: India's Art 352/356/360 are wider than most democracies; Germany's Basic Law includes 'constructive vote of no confidence', often cited for Indian electoral reform debates.

## Punjab angle

- Anandpur Sahib Resolution (1973) and later autonomy demands looked to a looser, US/Swiss-type federation — useful for comparative federalism answers.
- Large Punjabi diasporas in Canada, UK and USA make comparative knowledge of their parliamentary/federal systems relevant (e.g. Punjabi-origin legislators in Canada and UK).
- Punjab abolished its Legislative Council in 1970, illustrating India's flexible bicameralism in states, unlike fixed bicameral US states (except Nebraska).
- Inter-state water disputes (SYL) compared with US interstate compacts and Supreme Court original jurisdiction — a common comparative angle.

## How PPSC asks it

Usually asked as compare-and-contrast (India vs UK/US on parliamentary sovereignty, judicial review, federalism, amendment). Reward goes to structured tables of similarities/differences and a conclusion on suitability for India.

## Deep dive

### Legislatures compared

- USA: Senate 100 (two per state, six-year staggered terms), House 435; Senate confirms appointments and ratifies treaties (two-thirds).
- UK: House of Lords is mainly appointed life peers plus 26 bishops, with the hereditary element being phased out; Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949 limit it to a one-month delay on money Bills and about one year on others.
- India: Rajya Sabha has 12 nominated members, cannot be dissolved, has special powers (Arts 249, 312); Money Bills only 14 days.
- Australia: Senate elected with equal representation (12 per state); deadlock solved by double dissolution (s.57) and joint sitting.
- Germany: Bundesrat members are delegates of Land governments (3–6 votes each, cast as a bloc).
- Canada: Senate appointed by the Governor General on PM's advice, regionally distributed.
- France: Senate indirectly elected; Art 49.3 lets the government pass a Bill without a vote unless a censure motion succeeds (used for pension reform, 2023).

### Executives compared

- USA: President elected via Electoral College for four years; 22nd Amendment two-term limit; veto overridden by two-thirds of both Houses; no collective responsibility.
- UK: PM and Cabinet by convention; the monarch acts on advice; Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 was repealed in 2022, restoring dissolution on PM's request.
- France: semi-presidential; directly elected President (five years) appoints PM; "cohabitation" when they come from rival camps; Art 16 emergency powers.
- Germany: Chancellor elected by the Bundestag; removable only by a "constructive vote of no confidence" (Art 67) naming a successor — cited in Indian reform debates.
- Switzerland: seven-member Federal Council as collective head; presidency rotates yearly.
- India: nominal President, real executive in Council of Ministers collectively responsible to the Lok Sabha (Art 75(3)); Governors appointed by the Centre (Canadian model).

### Judiciary and constitutional review

- USA: Marbury v Madison (1803) created judicial review; nine justices with life tenure; due process (5th and 14th Amendments).
- UK: no strike-down power; Human Rights Act 1998 allows a "declaration of incompatibility"; UK Supreme Court since 2009.
- Germany: Federal Constitutional Court (16 judges, 12-year non-renewable terms, chosen by Bundestag and Bundesrat); citizens can file a constitutional complaint.
- France: Conseil constitutionnel; since 2010 the QPC lets courts refer questions on existing laws.
- South Africa: Constitutional Court enforces socio-economic rights (Grootboom, 2000 — housing).
- India: integrated judiciary, collegium appointments, writs under Arts 32 and 226, PIL, basic structure — among the most powerful courts in the world.

### Federalism and amendment compared

- USA (Art V): two-thirds of Congress plus three-fourths of states — 27 amendments only.
- Australia (s.128): referendum with a double majority (national and a majority of states); the 2023 "Voice" referendum failed.
- Switzerland: mandatory referendum for amendments; popular initiatives with 100,000 signatures.
- Canada: Constitution Act 1982 general formula needs Parliament plus seven provinces with 50% of population; Quebec's distinct status is an example of asymmetry.
- Germany: two-thirds of both Bundestag and Bundesrat; Art 79(3) eternity clause protects federalism and human dignity.
- Stepan: "coming together" federations (US, Switzerland, Australia) vs "holding together" (India, Spain, Belgium); India's Art 3 lets Parliament redraw states, unlike the US.

### Rights, directives and emergencies compared

- Directive Principles came from Ireland's 1937 Constitution (Art 45, non-cognisable); Spain's 1978 Constitution also has "guiding principles".
- US Bill of Rights (1791) mainly negative rights; India adds positive socio-economic rights through Arts 21, 21A and DPSP.
- Germany's Basic Law Art 1 places human dignity first — Indian courts cite it in Art 21 cases.
- Weimar Art 48 emergency powers were misused in the 1930s; India's 44th Amendment added safeguards (written Cabinet advice, Arts 20–21 non-suspendable).
- South Africa's Constitution (1996) has justiciable socio-economic rights and a horizontal application of rights — compare Kaushal Kishor (2023).
- Japan's 1947 Constitution (Art 31) supplied the phrase "procedure established by law"; its Art 9 renounces war, while India only directs the State to promote international peace (Art 51, a DPSP).

### Punjab angle and neighbouring comparisons

- Pakistan's 18th Amendment (2010) abolished the Concurrent List and devolved many subjects; the 7th NFC award gave provinces 57.5% of the divisible pool — a useful contrast for Pakistani Punjab and Indian Punjab.
- Pakistan's 26th Amendment (2024) created constitutional benches; its 27th Amendment (Nov 2025) set up a Federal Constitutional Court — contrasted with India's single Supreme Court.
- Anandpur Sahib Resolution looked to a US/Swiss-style federation with residuary powers to states — compare with Canada's accommodation of Quebec.
- Punjabi diaspora legislators in Canada and the UK make Westminster-style systems familiar; Canada's "notwithstanding clause" (s.33) is a contrast with Indian judicial supremacy.
- Bicameralism: Punjab abolished its Legislative Council (1970); in the US all states except Nebraska are bicameral.
- Water sharing: US interstate compacts and the US Supreme Court's original jurisdiction vs India's Art 262 tribunals (SYL, Ravi–Beas).

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** Compare and contrast the Indian parliamentary system with the British parliamentary model. Why did the framers prefer it over the American presidential system? (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: Westminster model adopted with modifications.
- Similarities: cabinet responsible to lower house, nominal head, PM leadership, Speaker, question hour.
- Differences: written Constitution, constitutional supremacy, judicial review, elected President, federal structure, fundamental rights.
- Reasons over presidential: familiarity under 1919/1935 Acts, responsibility over stability, diversity needs accommodation, avoid executive–legislature deadlock.
- Critiques: instability in coalition era, anti-defection compensations.
- Conclusion: 'responsibility' valued over 'stability' consciously.

</details>

**2.** "India's federalism is unique and cannot be compared to the American model." Discuss. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: holding-together vs coming-together federations.
- US: dual citizenship, dual constitutions, equal Senate representation, states' consent for borders, residuary with states.
- India: single citizenship, single Constitution (except earlier J&K), Art 3, residuary with Centre, emergency provisions, All India Services.
- Common: written constitution, division of powers, independent judiciary.
- Scholars: Wheare 'quasi-federal'; Granville Austin 'cooperative federalism'.
- Conclusion: federalism adapted to unity and diversity.

</details>

**3.** Distinguish between 'procedure established by law' and 'due process of law'. How has the Indian judiciary bridged the gap? (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: Art 21 wording borrowed from Japan; due process from US 5th/14th Amendments.
- Difference: procedural legality vs substantive fairness review.
- A.K. Gopalan (1950) narrow view.
- Maneka Gandhi (1978): just, fair and reasonable; link of Art 14, 19, 21.
- Later expansion: Puttaswamy (privacy, 2017).
- Conclusion: substantive due process through interpretation.

</details>
