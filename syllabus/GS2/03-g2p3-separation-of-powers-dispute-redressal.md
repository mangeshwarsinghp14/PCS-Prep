## Short notes

- Separation of powers is not rigid in India; Ram Jawaya Kapur (1955) held the Constitution does not strictly separate functions but assigns them broadly.
- Checks and balances: judicial review (Art 13, 32, 226), parliamentary control of executive (collective responsibility Art 75(3)), executive's role in judicial appointments, Parliament's power to remove judges (Art 124(4)).
- Art 50 directs separation of judiciary from executive in public services; Art 121/211 bar legislative discussion of judges' conduct; Art 122/212 bar courts from questioning legislative procedure.
- Kesavananda Bharati (1973) and Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain (1975) treated separation of powers as part of basic structure.
- Overlaps: ordinances (Art 123/213) as executive law-making; delegated legislation; tribunals exercising judicial functions within the executive.
- Judicial activism vs overreach: PIL from S.P. Gupta (1981); Vishaka (1997) guidelines; critiques around policy-making by courts (e.g. highway liquor ban 2016, later modified).
- Dispute redressal — courts: Supreme Court (Art 124), High Courts (Art 214), subordinate courts (Art 233–237); Art 131 original jurisdiction for Centre–state disputes; Art 262 bars courts from inter-state water disputes adjudicated by tribunals.
- Tribunals: Art 323A (administrative) and 323B (other matters) via 42nd Amendment; L. Chandra Kumar (1997) restored High Court review; Tribunals Reforms Act 2021 merged several tribunals; Madras Bar Association cases on independence.
- Alternative Dispute Resolution: Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996; Mediation Act 2023 (pre-litigation mediation); Sec 89 CPC; Lok Adalats under Legal Services Authorities Act 1987 (award final, no appeal).
- Specialised forums: Consumer Protection Act 2019 (District/State/National Commissions), Commercial Courts Act 2015, Gram Nyayalayas Act 2008, Family Courts Act 1984, NCLT under IBC 2016.
- Quasi-judicial redressal: Information Commissions, Human Rights Commissions, CAT (1985), Armed Forces Tribunal (AFT Act 2007), service and electricity regulators.
- Inter-state: Inter-State River Water Disputes Act 1956 (amended 2002 for time limits); Inter-State Council; Zonal Councils.
- Pendency: over 5 crore cases across courts (National Judicial Data Grid); causes — judge vacancies, government as largest litigant, adjournments, poor infrastructure.
- Reforms: e-Courts Phase III, virtual hearings, National Litigation Policy (proposed), All India Judicial Service debate, case management, more ADR.
- Legal aid: Art 39A; NALSA and State Legal Services Authorities; free legal services to women, SC/ST, persons in custody, low-income persons.

## Punjab angle

- Punjab and Haryana share a common High Court at Chandigarh — one of India's busiest High Courts.
- Punjab State Legal Services Authority (PLSA) holds National Lok Adalats with district legal services authorities; useful example for ADR.
- SYL dispute: SC's 2016 opinion on Presidential reference struck down the Punjab Termination of Agreements Act 2004; earlier Ravi-Beas Tribunal (1986) — example of Art 262 and Art 143.
- Punjab Assembly–Governor stand-offs (2023) went to the Supreme Court, showing judiciary resolving executive–legislature disputes.
- Armed Forces Tribunal has a regional bench at Chandigarh serving Punjab's large ex-servicemen population.

## How PPSC asks it

Questions test whether India follows 'functional' rather than rigid separation, judicial activism vs overreach, and efficacy of tribunals/ADR in reducing pendency. Cite cases (Ram Jawaya Kapur, L. Chandra Kumar, Madras Bar Assn) and suggest practical reforms.

## Deep dive

### Separation of powers — case table (case — year — principle)

- In re Delhi Laws Act (1951) — delegated legislation allowed, but essential legislative functions cannot be delegated.
- Special Reference No. 1 of 1964 (Keshav Singh) — legislature's privileges are subject to the Constitution and judicial review of fundamental rights.
- Asif Hameed (1989) and Divisional Manager, Aravali Golf Course (2007) — warnings against judicial overreach into policy.
- State of Tamil Nadu v State of Kerala (Mullaperiyar, 2014) — a legislature cannot simply overrule a judgment; it may only remove the basis of it.
- Kalpana Mehta (2018) — parliamentary committee reports may be relied on in court, but not questioned.
- Rojer Mathew (2019) — whether the Speaker's Money Bill certificate is reviewable was referred to a 7-judge bench.
- Krishna Kumar Singh (2017) — re-promulgation of ordinances is a fraud on the Constitution.
- GNCTD v Union (May 2023, 5 judges) — Delhi government controls services except police, public order and land; Parliament then passed the GNCTD (Amendment) Act 2023.
- State of Punjab v Principal Secretary to Governor (Nov 2023) and the Presidential Reference opinion (Nov 2025) — limits of executive discretion over legislation.

### Checks on the judiciary and judicial accountability

- Removal of judges under Arts 124(4)/217 and the Judges (Inquiry) Act 1968: special majority in each House after a three-member inquiry committee finds proved misbehaviour.
- Past attempts: Justice V. Ramaswami (1993, motion failed in Lok Sabha), Justice Soumitra Sen (2011, Rajya Sabha passed; he resigned before the Lok Sabha vote).
- Justice Yashwant Varma (cash found after a fire at his residence, March 2025): motion admitted Aug 2025; three-member inquiry committee (Justice Aravind Kumar of the SC, a High Court Chief Justice and senior advocate B.V. Acharya). Varma resigned in April 2026, ending removal proceedings; the committee's report, laid in the Lok Sabha in August 2026, found all three charges proved.
- In-house procedure (1999) for complaints against judges; collegium resolutions published online since 2017; judges' assets put on the SC website (2025 decision).
- Contempt of Courts Act 1971 (truth as a defence since 2006) — Prashant Bhushan case (2020) revived the debate on criminal contempt.
- Post-retirement appointments and tribunal postings raise questions of independence.

### Centre–state and inter-state dispute mechanisms

- Art 131 original suits: Punjab v Union over the Rural Development Fund (2023), Kerala v Union on borrowing (2023–24), West Bengal v Union on CBI consent (2021).
- Art 262 and the Inter-State River Water Disputes Act 1956: tribunals for Krishna, Godavari, Narmada, Cauvery (final SC verdict 2018; Cauvery Water Management Authority), Mahadayi (award 2018), Vansadhara.
- Ravi–Beas Tribunal (Justice V. Balakrishna Eradi, set up 1986, report 1987) — further references never finalised; SYL litigation continues in the SC.
- The 2019 Inter-State River Water Disputes (Amendment) Bill proposed a single standing tribunal and a Disputes Resolution Committee — it lapsed.
- Art 143 references for complex questions: Berubari (1960), Cauvery (1991), Special Courts (1978), SYL (2016), Governor assent (2025).
- Non-judicial forums: Inter-State Council, Zonal Councils, NITI Governing Council, GST Council (no standing dispute mechanism yet, though Art 279A(11) provides for one).

### Courts, tribunals and ADR — reforms

- Tribunals: Madras Bar Association cases (2020, 2021) struck down short tenures; in Nov 2025 the SC again struck down key provisions of the Tribunals Reforms Act 2021 and asked for a National Tribunals Commission.
- Mediation Act 2023 — pre-litigation mediation, Mediation Council of India, online mediation; Commercial Courts Act requires pre-institution mediation (Sec 12A).
- Arbitration: 2019 amendment created the Arbitration Council of India; India International Arbitration Centre Act 2019.
- Lok Adalats: National Lok Adalats are held across India on fixed dates several times a year; Permanent Lok Adalats (Sec 22B, Legal Services Authorities Act) handle public utility services and can decide on merits.
- e-Courts Phase III (2023–27, about Rs 7,210 crore) — digitisation, virtual courts, e-filing.
- Minimum three years' practice for entry-level civil judge restored by the Supreme Court in May 2025.
- Gram Nyayalayas Act 2008 — mobile courts at the intermediate panchayat level; few states have operationalised them fully.

### Punjab specifics

- Punjab and Haryana High Court: started in 1947 at Shimla as the East Punjab High Court, moved to Chandigarh in 1955, common High Court after 1966 (Art 231).
- Punjab State Legal Services Authority and district authorities hold National Lok Adalats and run legal-aid clinics.
- Punjab Police Act 2007 and Prakash Singh (2006) directives; the 2023 Punjab Police (Amendment) Bill sought a state-run process for DGP selection, bypassing the UPSC panel.
- Water and territory disputes from Punjab test every forum: Art 262 tribunal (Eradi), Art 143 reference (SYL 2016), Art 226 (BBMB 2025 High Court orders), Art 131 suits.
- Executive–legislature disputes (Governor vs Assembly, 2023) were settled by the Supreme Court — an example of judicial arbitration of separation of powers.
- Armed Forces Tribunal regional bench at Chandigarh and the Central Administrative Tribunal Chandigarh bench serve the large service and ex-service population.

### Answer frameworks

- Montesquieu (The Spirit of the Laws, 1748) — strict separation; India follows functional separation with checks and balances.
- Judicial activism vs restraint: PIL successes (Vishaka, MC Mehta, Right to Food) vs overreach (liquor-ban radius, policy timetables).
- Tribunalisation: speed and expertise vs independence and executive control.
- ADR: reduces pendency and cost; concerns over quality, coercion and lack of appeal.
- Way forward: National Tribunals Commission, judicial impact assessment for new laws, All India Judicial Service debate, more judges per million population.
- Evidence to cite: National Judicial Data Grid (pendency by court and age of case) and the India Justice Report rankings of states on police, prisons, judiciary and legal aid.

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** "The Indian Constitution does not recognise the doctrine of separation of powers in its absolute rigidity." Discuss with suitable examples and judgments. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: Montesquieu's doctrine; Indian approach of functional separation with checks.
- Constitutional basis: Art 50, 121–122, 211–212, 361; Ram Jawaya Kapur 1955.
- Overlaps: executive drawn from legislature, ordinances, delegated legislation, tribunals, judicial appointments with executive role.
- Checks: judicial review, impeachment, parliamentary questions, CAG.
- Basic structure: Kesavananda, Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain.
- Tensions: overreach debates, NJAC, executive non-compliance.
- Conclusion: harmony through constitutional morality, not compartmentalisation.

</details>

**2.** Tribunals were created to reduce the burden on courts but have themselves faced criticism. Examine the issues and suggest reforms. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: Art 323A/B, 42nd Amendment, Swaran Singh Committee.
- Achievements: specialised expertise (NGT, CAT, AFT), speed in some areas.
- Issues: vacancies, executive control over appointments/service conditions, tenure, ouster of HC jurisdiction, pendency.
- Judgments: L. Chandra Kumar 1997, Madras Bar Assn series, Rojer Mathew 2019.
- Tribunals Reforms Act 2021 and criticisms.
- Reforms: National Tribunals Commission, uniform service conditions, digitisation.
- Conclusion: independence as the precondition of effectiveness.

</details>

**3.** Assess the role of Lok Adalats and mediation in improving access to justice in India. (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: Art 39A; LSA Act 1987; Mediation Act 2023.
- Strengths: low cost, speed, finality, settlement of motor accident, bank recovery and matrimonial cases.
- Limitations: compromise pressure, compromise quality, lack of trained mediators, limited to compoundable matters.
- Punjab: PLSA National Lok Adalats.
- Way forward: court-annexed mediation centres, awareness, online dispute resolution.
- Conclusion: complements, not substitutes, adjudication.

</details>
