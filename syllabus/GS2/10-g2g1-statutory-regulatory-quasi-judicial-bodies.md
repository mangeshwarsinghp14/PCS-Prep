## Short notes

- Statutory bodies are created by an Act of Parliament/legislature (e.g. NHRC, CVC, Lokpal, NGT); regulatory bodies set and enforce rules for a sector; quasi-judicial bodies adjudicate disputes with court-like powers but are not courts.
- NHRC: Protection of Human Rights Act 1993 (amended 2019); chair a former CJI or SC judge; recommendatory powers; cannot inquire into matters older than one year; limited role over armed forces.
- CVC: statutory under CVC Act 2003 (after Vineet Narain 1997); supervises vigilance and CBI's corruption investigations; advisory, not prosecutorial.
- CBI: under Delhi Special Police Establishment Act 1946; needs state consent (Sec 6) — several states have withdrawn general consent.
- Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act 2013: Lokpal (chair + up to 8 members, half judicial) began functioning in 2019; jurisdiction over PM with safeguards, ministers, MPs, officials; states to set up Lokayuktas.
- Information Commissions: RTI Act 2005; RTI (Amendment) Act 2019 let Centre fix tenure and salaries — criticised as diluting independence.
- Financial regulators: RBI (1934 Act), SEBI (1992 Act), IRDAI (1999), PFRDA (2013 Act); FSDC as coordination forum.
- Sectoral regulators: TRAI (1997), CERC and State ERCs (Electricity Act 2003), CCI (Competition Act 2002), FSSAI (2006 Act), RERA authorities (2016 Act), AERA, NMC (2019, replaced MCI).
- Quasi-judicial: NGT (2010 Act; environmental disputes; principle of polluter pays), consumer commissions (2019 Act), NCLT/NCLAT (Companies Act/IBC), CAT (1985), Armed Forces Tribunal (2007), ITAT, SAT.
- Other statutory commissions: NCW (1990), NCPCR (2005), NCM (1992), National Commission for Safai Karamcharis.
- NITI Aayog (2015) and erstwhile Planning Commission are executive bodies — neither constitutional nor statutory.
- Issues: appointment dominated by executive, post-retirement sinecures for judges/bureaucrats, vacancies, recommendatory powers ignored, overlap between regulators, regulatory capture, weak parliamentary oversight.
- Judicial view: independence of tribunals is part of separation of powers (Madras Bar Association cases); NGT held to have suo motu powers (2021).
- Reform ideas: common law on regulators (Financial Sector Legislative Reforms Commission 2013 proposed draft Indian Financial Code), parliamentary committee on regulators, fixed tenures, transparent selection, regulatory impact assessment.
- 2nd ARC 13th Report recommended rationalising regulators and making them accountable to Parliament.

## Punjab angle

- Punjab State Electricity Regulatory Commission (PSERC) fixes tariffs; free power to farm pumps and 300 free units to households (from July 2022) create a large subsidy bill for PSPCL.
- Punjab Pollution Control Board (under Water Act 1974 and Air Act 1981) and NGT orders on stubble burning and the Buddha Nullah pollution in Ludhiana.
- Punjab State Human Rights Commission, Punjab State Commission for Scheduled Castes and Punjab State Information Commission are key state-level bodies (the Right to Service Commission was abolished in 2018 when the 2011 Act was replaced).
- Real Estate Regulatory Authority, Punjab regulates projects in Mohali/New Chandigarh and Ludhiana belts.

## How PPSC asks it

Examiners ask whether regulatory/quasi-judicial bodies are 'toothless', how to secure their independence, and the difference between statutory and constitutional status. Name the Act and year for each body and offer structural reforms.

## Deep dive

### Key bodies compared (law — who sits — who selects — how removed — what power)

- NHRC — Protection of Human Rights Act 1993 (amended 2006, 2019) — chair a former CJI or former SC judge (2019 change), plus members; chairs of NCW, NCSC, NCST, NCM, NCBC, NCPCR and the Chief Commissioner for Persons with Disabilities sit as ex officio members — chosen by a committee led by the PM (Lok Sabha Speaker, Home Minister, Leaders of Opposition of both Houses, RS Deputy Chairman) — term 3 years after 2019 (was 5) — recommendations only.
- CVC — CVC Act 2003 — Central Vigilance Commissioner plus up to 2 Vigilance Commissioners — selected by PM, Home Minister and Leader of Opposition (LS) — 4 years or age 65 — removed by the President after a Supreme Court inquiry — supervises CBI in Prevention of Corruption Act cases; does not itself prosecute.
- Lokpal — Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act 2013 (in force 16 January 2014) — chair plus up to 8 members; half judicial, and at least half from SC/ST/OBC/minorities/women — selection committee: PM, Speaker, LoP (LS), CJI or a nominated SC judge, and an eminent jurist — 5 years or age 70 — removal on SC inquiry after a petition signed by at least 100 MPs — has its own Inquiry Wing and Prosecution Wing.
- Lokpal chairs: Justice Pinaki Chandra Ghose was the first (March 2019); Justice A.M. Khanwilkar has headed it since March 2024 (official Lokpal website).
- Central Information Commission — RTI Act 2005 — selected by PM, LoP and a Union minister named by the PM — since the RTI (Amendment) Act 2019 the Centre fixes term and salary by rules (RTI Rules 2019 set a 3-year term).
- NGT — National Green Tribunal Act 2010 — chair a retired SC judge or High Court Chief Justice; Principal Bench in Delhi, zonal benches at Bhopal, Pune, Kolkata and Chennai — must apply sustainable development, precautionary and polluter-pays principles (Section 20) — appeal lies straight to the Supreme Court within 90 days.
- CCI — Competition Act 2002 (amended 2023: deal-value threshold of ₹2,000 crore for combinations; settlement and commitment procedures) — appeals go to NCLAT.
- CBI Director — chosen by a committee of PM, LoP (or leader of the largest opposition party) and CJI or nominee (DSPE Act as amended by the Lokpal Act); since the 2021 amendments the tenure is 2 years, extendable year by year up to 5 years. The same 2021 change applies to the ED Director (through the CVC Act).

### Constitutional vs statutory vs executive — the classification trap

- Constitutional bodies: Election Commission (Art 324), UPSC/SPSCs (Art 315), CAG (Art 148), Finance Commission (Art 280), NCSC (Art 338), NCST (Art 338A, 89th Amendment 2003), NCBC (Art 338B, 102nd Amendment 2018), Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities (Art 350B), GST Council (Art 279A).
- NCBC trap: it was statutory under the NCBC Act 1993 and became constitutional only in 2018.
- Statutory bodies: NHRC/SHRCs, NCW (1990 Act), NCM (1992), NCPCR (2005 Act), CVC, CIC/SICs, Lokpal, NGT, NDMA, NIA (NIA Act 2008), the Competition Commission and sector regulators.
- Executive bodies (created by resolution): NITI Aayog (2015), National Security Council, and the CBI (set up by a Home Ministry resolution in 1963, with powers drawn from the DSPE Act 1946). In 2013 the Gauhati High Court called the CBI unconstitutional (Navendra Kumar case); the Supreme Court stayed that ruling.
- The CVC began as an executive body in 1964 on the Santhanam Committee's advice. It became statutory only in 2003, after Vineet Narain (1997) and an ordinance in 1998.
- National Commission for Safai Karamcharis: its 1993 Act lapsed in 2004, so it now continues as a non-statutory body whose tenure is extended by resolution — a frequent MCQ trap.
- Tribunals: Article 323A (administrative tribunals) and 323B (other matters) came in through the 42nd Amendment (1976). CAT itself is created by the Administrative Tribunals Act 1985, so it is a statutory body with a constitutional basis.
- Regulators are 'independent' only by statute; Parliament can change their powers by ordinary law, as the RTI (Amendment) Act 2019 and the Tribunals Reforms Act 2021 showed.

### Quasi-judicial bodies and judicial control — leading cases

- Quasi-judicial means the body decides rights after hearing the parties, must follow natural justice (hear the other side; no one judges their own cause) and must give reasoned orders, which courts can review.
- S.P. Sampath Kumar (1987): tribunals may substitute for High Courts if equally effective. L. Chandra Kumar (1997) reversed course: tribunal decisions remain subject to writ review by a High Court Division Bench under Arts 226/227, because judicial review is part of the basic structure.
- Union of India v R. Gandhi (2010): NCLT/NCLAT upheld, but members must have judicial-equivalent qualifications. Rojer Mathew (2019) struck down the tribunal service rules framed under the Finance Act 2017.
- Madras Bar Association (2020 and 2021): a minimum 5-year tenure, a judicial majority on selection committees, and a National Tribunals Commission were directed. The Tribunals Reforms Act 2021 (which abolished bodies such as the Film Certification Appellate Tribunal and IPAB) re-enacted the struck-down 4-year tenure and was challenged again.
- Consumer Protection Act 2019: three-tier commissions. Since the 2021 rules, District Commissions hear claims up to ₹50 lakh, State Commissions ₹50 lakh–₹2 crore, and the National Commission above ₹2 crore. The Central Consumer Protection Authority is a separate regulator that handles class actions and misleading ads.
- RERA 2016: an Authority plus an Appellate Tribunal in each state. Newtech Promoters (2021) upheld RERA's power to order refunds with interest.
- Electricity Act 2003: State ERCs set tariffs and adjudicate disputes between licensees and generators; appeals go to APTEL and then to the Supreme Court.
- Lok Adalats (Legal Services Authorities Act 1987): an award counts as a civil court decree, and no appeal lies against it. Permanent Lok Adalats handle public utility services.
- NGT suo motu power: Municipal Corporation of Greater Mumbai v Ankita Sinha (2021) held that NGT can act on its own (for example on letters or news reports).

### Regulatory governance — debates to write on

- Independence depends on four things: (1) how members are appointed, (2) security of tenure, (3) financial autonomy (many regulators depend on ministry grants), and (4) post-retirement restrictions.
- Accountability gap: most regulators send annual reports to Parliament, but no standing committee scrutinises them systematically. The FSLRC (2013) proposed a common framework: board structure, public consultation on draft rules, and a regulatory impact assessment.
- 2nd ARC Ethics report (4th, 2007) wanted a constitutional 'Rashtriya Lokayukta' and state Lokayuktas, with the PM kept outside its remit — note the contrast with the 2013 Act, which includes the PM with safeguards.
- Regulatory overlap: RBI and SEBI on hybrid instruments; TRAI and CCI on digital platforms; FSSAI and state food departments on enforcement.
- Regulatory capture and the 'revolving door': retired officials heading the regulators of their former ministries.
- Vacancies weaken bodies. Information Commissions and tribunals have repeatedly run with empty posts, and the Supreme Court in Anjali Bhardwaj (2019) directed timely, transparent appointments to information commissions.
- CBI consent: under Section 6 of the DSPE Act the CBI needs state consent. Several states, including Punjab (November 2020), withdrew general consent, so case-by-case consent is now needed.
- Lokpal performance: most complaints in the early years were dismissed for not following the prescribed format or for lacking jurisdiction (Lokpal annual reports). Use this to argue for simpler access, not for abolition.

### Punjab's statutory and quasi-judicial bodies (beyond the basics)

- Punjab Transparency and Accountability Commission: under the Punjab Transparency and Accountability in Delivery of Public Service Act 2018, it hears second appeals (revisions) over service delivery. It replaced the Punjab Right to Service Commission, which was abolished in July 2018 (state gazette; see g2g3).
- Lokpal Punjab: a state ombudsman under the Punjab Lokpal Act 1996 (pre-dates the national 2013 Act).
- Punjab State Information Commission: a Chief and Information Commissioners under the RTI Act; hears second appeals and imposes Section 20 penalties.
- PSERC (Punjab State Electricity Regulatory Commission): formed in 1999 under the Electricity Regulatory Commissions Act 1998, now under the Electricity Act 2003. It fixes PSPCL tariffs, and the state reimburses the subsidy (₹15,550 crore in the 2026-27 budget, per PRS).
- Punjab Water Regulation and Development Authority: set up under the Punjab Water Resources (Management and Regulation) Act 2020; regulates groundwater extraction and charges for industrial and commercial use.
- Others: Punjab State Human Rights Commission (PHRA 1993), Punjab State Commission for Women, Punjab State Commission for Scheduled Castes, Punjab State Food Commission (NFSA 2013, Section 16), Punjab State Commission for Protection of Child Rights, State Police Complaints Authority (a Prakash Singh 2006 direction), Punjab RERA (2017).
- Punjab Vigilance Bureau is an executive police wing, not a statutory commission. It runs the Anti-Corruption Action Line.

### Answer frames, PYQ patterns and traps

- Typical asks: 'Are regulatory bodies a fourth branch of government?'; 'Tribunalisation — boon or bane for justice delivery?'; 'Is the Lokpal a toothless tiger?'; 'Discuss the role of quasi-judicial bodies in protecting rights'.
- Five-step frame: mandate and legal basis → independence (appointment, tenure, finance) → nature of powers (binding or recommendatory) → performance evidence (pendency, vacancies, compliance) → reforms.
- Reform menu: a common 'Regulatory Reform Act', a parliamentary committee on regulators, a National Tribunals Commission, fixed tenures with no re-appointment, a cooling-off period before post-retirement jobs, outcome audits by the CAG, public consultation on rules.
- NHRC traps: it cannot take up a matter more than one year after the act was committed; for armed forces it can only seek a report from the Centre; it has the powers of a civil court for summoning and evidence.
- Lokpal traps: complaints against the PM need approval of a full bench with a two-thirds majority, the inquiry is held in camera, and international relations, security, public order, atomic energy and space are excluded.
- CIC trap: since 2019 the tenure is set by rules, not by the Act. The State Chief Information Commissioner is chosen by the CM, LoP and a minister.
- Write 'statutory body under the ___ Act, year' every time; examiners reward precision.

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** "Statutory bodies like the NHRC have become toothless tigers." Critically examine and suggest measures to make them effective. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: PHRA 1993; Paris Principles.
- Constraints: recommendatory powers, one-year limitation, armed forces exclusion, dependence on government for staff and funds, appointments.
- Contributions: custodial death guidelines, bonded labour, inquiries.
- Similar issues: SHRCs, NCW, CIC after 2019 amendment.
- Reforms: binding powers for some orders, independent investigative wing, transparent appointments, action-taken reports in legislature.
- Conclusion: independence plus enforcement capacity.

</details>

**2.** Regulatory bodies are essential for a market economy but face challenges of independence and accountability. Discuss with examples. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: shift from state ownership to regulatory state after 1991.
- Examples: SEBI, TRAI, CERC/SERCs, CCI, RBI.
- Independence issues: appointments, government directions, revolving door.
- Accountability issues: no parliamentary oversight framework, overlapping mandates.
- FSLRC and 2nd ARC proposals.
- Punjab: PSERC and power subsidy politics.
- Conclusion: 'arm's length but answerable'.

</details>

**3.** Distinguish between constitutional, statutory and executive bodies with examples. Why does this distinction matter? (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: source of authority.
- Constitutional: ECI, CAG, UPSC, FC.
- Statutory: NHRC, CVC, Lokpal, NGT.
- Executive: NITI Aayog, earlier CBI (now under DSPE Act).
- Significance: removal protection, permanence, independence, amendment difficulty.
- Conclusion: status signals strength of autonomy.

</details>
