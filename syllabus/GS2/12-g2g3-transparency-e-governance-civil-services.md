## Short notes

- Governance: exercise of authority to manage resources; good governance (UNDP/UNESCAP) — participation, rule of law, transparency, responsiveness, consensus orientation, equity, effectiveness, accountability.
- Transparency tools: RTI Act 2005, proactive disclosure (Sec 4), social audits, open data (data.gov.in), e-procurement (GeM, 2016), budget transparency.
- Accountability: political (legislature), administrative (hierarchy, CVC), judicial, social (citizens, media); answerability plus enforceability.
- e-Governance evolution: NIC (1976), National e-Governance Plan (2006) with Mission Mode Projects, Digital India (2015), e-Kranti (NeGP 2.0).
- Interaction types: G2C, G2B, G2G, G2E; models often cited — broadcasting, critical flow, comparative analysis, e-advocacy, interactive-service.
- Successes: Bhoomi land records (Karnataka), Aadhaar-based DBT, UPI, DigiLocker, UMANG, CoWIN, CPGRAMS, PRAGATI (2015) for project monitoring, Common Service Centres.
- Limitations: digital divide (rural, gender, elderly), authentication failures causing exclusion, cybersecurity, data privacy, low process re-engineering ('digitising red tape'), language barriers.
- Legal framework: IT Act 2000, Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023, Aadhaar Act 2016 (Puttaswamy 2017 privacy; Aadhaar judgment 2018).
- Potential: AI-enabled services (Bhashini for languages), India Stack/DPI exported abroad, predictive governance using data analytics, single-window clearances.
- Citizens' Charter: UK origin (1991); India adopted in 1997; DARPG's Sevottam model (2nd ARC 12th Report) links charter, grievance redress and service capability.
- Weakness of charters: not legally enforceable, poorly publicised, no consultation; remedy — Right to Services laws (MP 2010 first; Punjab 2011, replaced in 2018 by the Transparency and Accountability in Delivery of Public Services Act).
- Civil services in democracy: Arts 308–323; Art 311 safeguards; Art 312 All India Services; Sardar Patel's 'steel frame'; values of neutrality, anonymity, integrity, commitment to constitutional values.
- Reforms: Mission Karmayogi (2020, NPCSCB) and iGOT platform for competency-based training; Hota Committee (2004) on administrative reforms; 2nd ARC 10th Report on personnel administration.
- Debates: lateral entry (2018 onwards; 2024 advertisement withdrawn over reservation concerns), politicisation and transfers (T.S.R. Subramanian 2013 — fixed tenure, civil services boards), generalist vs specialist.
- Changing trends: New Public Management (efficiency, outsourcing) to New Public Service and networked governance; PPPs; citizen-centric, outcome-based and data-driven governance; 'minimum government, maximum governance'.
- Changing trends (contd.): social media as accountability tool; behavioural insights (nudges) in policy; regulatory state; cooperative and competitive federalism.

## Punjab angle

- Punjab Transparency and Accountability in Delivery of Public Services Act 2018 (replaced the Right to Service Act 2011 and abolished its Commission) notifies time-bound services with penalties; delivery largely through Sewa Kendras.
- Sewa Kendras provide hundreds of G2C services under one roof; 'Bhagwant Mann Sarkar Tuhade Dwar' (helpline 1076) delivers services at citizens' doorsteps.
- Punjab Land Records Society digitised land records; online 'fard' access reduces patwari discretion.
- Anti-Corruption Action Line (launched March 2022) lets citizens send audio/video evidence of bribe demands.
- Punjab State Information Commission handles RTI second appeals; Punjab's e-governance faces digital divide in border and Kandi areas.

## How PPSC asks it

Frequent themes: e-governance potential vs exclusion, citizens' charters vs right to service, civil service neutrality and reforms (Karmayogi, lateral entry). Strong answers use models/frameworks, examples (Bhoomi, Sewa Kendras) and balance.

## Deep dive

### 2nd ARC (2005–09) report by report — what to quote

- Set up in 2005 under M. Veerappa Moily (later chaired by V. Ramachandran); it gave 15 reports. The 1st ARC (1966) was chaired by Morarji Desai and then K. Hanumanthaiya.
- 1st — Right to Information: Master Key to Good Governance (2006): repeal the Official Secrets Act 1923 and move its essentials into the National Security Act, strengthen Section 4 proactive disclosure, digitise records.
- 2nd — Unlocking Human Capital: Entitlements and Governance (2006): an NREGA case study on panchayat-led planning and social audit.
- 3rd — Crisis Management: From Despair to Hope (2006); 5th — Public Order (2007), which called for separating crime investigation from law-and-order duties and for State Security Commissions.
- 4th — Ethics in Governance (2007): a constitutional Rashtriya Lokayukta, codes of ethics for ministers and civil servants, partial state funding of elections, and confiscation of illegally acquired property.
- 6th — Local Governance (2007); 7th — Capacity Building for Conflict Resolution (2008); 8th — Combating Terrorism (2008), which backed a federal investigation agency (the NIA came in 2008); 9th — Social Capital (2008).
- 10th — Refurbishing of Personnel Administration (2008): mid-career entry, intensive performance reviews at 14 and 20 years of service, a Civil Services Law, and a domain-competency approach.
- 11th — Promoting e-Governance: The SMART Way Forward (2008). SMART = Simple, Moral, Accountable, Responsive, Transparent. Its core advice: re-engineer processes before computerising them, build capacity, and use a unique ID.
- 12th — Citizen Centric Administration (2009) endorsed the Sevottam model; 13th — Organisational Structure of GoI; 14th — Strengthening Financial Management Systems; 15th — State and District Administration (all 2009).

### RTI in practice — sections, case law and recent changes

- Section 4(1)(b): 17 categories of mandatory proactive disclosure. Section 6: application (₹10 fee for central public authorities; free for BPL). Section 7: reply within 30 days, or 48 hours where life or liberty is involved.
- Section 8: exemptions, but Section 8(2) lets public interest override them; Section 11: third-party information; Section 19: first appeal, then second appeal to the Information Commission.
- Section 20: penalty of ₹250 a day, up to ₹25,000, on the PIO. Section 24: intelligence and security organisations are exempt except for allegations of corruption or human-rights violations. Section 22: the Act overrides inconsistent laws.
- Digital Personal Data Protection Act 2023, Section 44(3), rewrote RTI Section 8(1)(j) to exempt personal information outright, removing the old public-interest test. Transparency activists criticise it, and it took effect alongside the DPDP Rules (notified 13 November 2025, when Section 44(3) came into force).
- Case law: CBSE v Aditya Bandopadhyay (2011) — evaluated answer sheets are 'information'; Girish Ramchandra Deshpande (2012) — service records are personal; RBI v Jayantilal Mistry (2015) — RBI cannot hide behind 'fiduciary relationship'; CPIO, Supreme Court v Subhash Chandra Agarwal (2019) — the CJI's office is a public authority.
- Political parties: in 2013 the CIC declared six national parties public authorities under RTI. The parties did not comply, and the issue is pending before the Supreme Court.
- Weak spots: rising pendency and vacancies in commissions, poor record management, threats to RTI users, and rare imposition of penalties. The Whistle Blowers Protection Act 2014 is still not operational (the 2015 amendment bill lapsed).
- History: the Freedom of Information Act 2002 was passed but never notified; the RTI Act came into force on 12 October 2005. Tamil Nadu and Goa (1997) had the first state RTI laws, followed by Rajasthan, Karnataka, Delhi and Maharashtra; MKSS's jan sunwais in Rajasthan drove the movement.

### Citizens' charters to Right to Service — Punjab's verified model

- Punjab Right to Service Act 2011 (Punjab Act 24 of 2011) was repealed by the Punjab Transparency and Accountability in Delivery of Public Service Act 2018 (Punjab Act 16 of 2018). The Governor assented on 12 July 2018 and it was gazetted on 17 July 2018 (state gazette).
- The Punjab Right to Service Commission and its ten commissioners were dispensed with on 18 July 2018. Exam trap: older notes still describe the 2011 Act and its commission as current.
- The 2018 Act makes online receipt of applications mandatory and requires electronic delivery of services within three years (extendable by two). Each authority must publish its list of e-services, reviewed every 1 January.
- Appeals: first appeal to the Appellate Authority within 30 days (decided within 30 days); revision to the Punjab Transparency and Accountability Commission within 60 days. Both can act suo motu.
- Penalty: ₹500 to ₹5,000 on the Designated Officer for failing without sufficient cause; the Commission can impose up to ₹10,000 per case, recover it from salary, and award compensation to the applicant out of the penalty.
- The nodal agency is the Department of Governance Reforms and Public Grievances, which notifies services, time limits and officers.
- Delivery channels: Sewa Kendras (one-stop centres since 2016); doorstep delivery under 'Bhagwant Mann Sarkar Tuhade Dwar' through helpline 1076 (406 services at launch in December 2023, 437 by mid-2026 per state releases); the Connect Punjab and e-Sewa portals.
- Sevottam (DARPG, endorsed by the 2nd ARC 12th Report) has three modules: Citizen's Charter, grievance redress, and service delivery capability. Its seven steps: define services and clients → set standards → build capability → perform → monitor → evaluate → improve.
- National context: Madhya Pradesh's Lok Sewaon ke Pradan ki Guarantee Adhiniyam 2010 was the first right-to-service law; Bihar, Haryana and others followed. India's first charters came in 1997, borrowing the UK's 1991 Citizen's Charter (John Major).

### e-Governance — models, maturity, evidence

- Gartner's four-stage e-government maturity model: presence → interaction → transaction → transformation. Use it to place a service (for example, online fard is at the transaction stage; integrated land titling would be transformation).
- Richard Heeks' 'design–reality gap': most e-government projects in developing countries fail totally or partly because the design assumes processes, skills and data that do not exist on the ground.
- NeGP 2006: Mission Mode Projects (central, state and integrated) on core infrastructure — SWAN (network), State Data Centres and Common Service Centres; Digital India (2015) and e-Kranti followed.
- Digital public infrastructure stack: Aadhaar (Act 2016), UPI (2016), DigiLocker, Account Aggregator, ONDC (2022), Bhashini (language AI, 2022), DIKSHA; India shares these abroad, for example through MOSIP-based digital ID.
- UN E-Government Survey (EGDI): India is in the middle band of ranks, held back by human-capital and telecom-access components more than by online services — useful for a 'digital divide' argument.
- Punjab: Connect Punjab and e-Sewa portals, Punjab Land Records Society's online fard and mutation, e-office in secretariat departments, e-registration of deeds; the Anti-Corruption Action Line takes video and audio complaints.
- Risks to write about: exclusion through authentication failure, language barriers, cyber-security (ransomware attacks on public hospitals), data protection under the DPDP Act 2023, vendor lock-in, and 'digitising red tape' without process re-engineering.
- Best practice: process re-engineering first, an assisted mode always available, offline fallback, grievance redress inside the portal, open APIs, and periodic third-party audit.

### Civil services in a democracy — law, committees, debates

- Constitution: Art 309 (recruitment rules), Art 310 (doctrine of pleasure), Art 311 (no dismissal by an authority subordinate to the appointing one; inquiry before major penalty, with exceptions in Art 311(2)(a)–(c) — Union v Tulsiram Patel, 1985), Art 312 (All India Services by Rajya Sabha resolution).
- Rules: All India Services Act 1951; AIS (Conduct) Rules 1968; AIS (Discipline and Appeal) Rules 1969; CCS (Conduct) Rules 1964. In Punjab: Punjab Civil Services (Punishment and Appeal) Rules 1970 and the Punjab Government Employees (Conduct) Rules.
- T.S.R. Subramanian v Union of India (2013): the Supreme Court asked for a minimum fixed tenure, Civil Services Boards to decide transfers and postings, and written recording of oral instructions from political superiors.
- Committees: Gorwala (1951) and Paul Appleby (1953) on public administration; Kothari (1976) created the prelims–mains exam structure; Satish Chandra (1989) added the essay paper; Hota (2004) on reforms; Baswan (2016) reviewed age and attempts.
- Mission Karmayogi (2020): the National Programme for Civil Services Capacity Building, the Capacity Building Commission (2021), the iGOT Karmayogi platform, role-based competency mapping (FRACing), and annual capacity-building plans for ministries.
- Lateral entry: appointments at Joint Secretary level since 2018. A 2024 UPSC advertisement for 45 posts was withdrawn after objections that it bypassed reservation — use this for a generalist vs specialist debate.
- Neutrality vs commitment: the 1970s call for a 'committed bureaucracy' vs Weberian neutrality. Today's framing is commitment to constitutional values, not to the party in power.
- Changing trends: Old Public Administration → New Public Management (efficiency, outsourcing) → New Public Service (citizens, not customers; Denhardt) → digital-era governance and 'Jan Bhagidari'; Good Governance Day falls on 25 December.

### Answer frames and traps

- Frame for any e-governance question: access (devices, connectivity, language) → process (re-engineering) → people (skills, attitude) → protection (privacy, security) → accountability (grievance, audit).
- Frame for charters vs right to service: a charter is a declaration; a right-to-service law gives an enforceable right, a time limit, an appeal and a penalty; add Punjab's 2018 Act as the state example.
- Trap: Sevottam is a DARPG framework, which the 2nd ARC endorsed; the ARC did not invent it.
- Trap: the Official Secrets Act 1923 is still in force even though the 2nd ARC recommended repealing it.
- Trap: the J&K Right to Information Act 2009 was repealed by the J&K Reorganisation Act 2019; the central RTI Act now applies in J&K and Ladakh.
- Trap: Punjab's appellate body for service delivery is now the 'Punjab Transparency and Accountability Commission', not the 'Right to Service Commission'.
- Use one Punjab example in every answer: Sewa Kendras, 1076 doorstep delivery, online fard, or the Anti-Corruption Action Line.

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** "E-governance is not about 'e' but about 'governance'." Discuss the successes and limitations of e-governance in India. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: e-governance as process re-engineering, not digitisation alone.
- Successes: DBT, UPI, DigiLocker, GeM, CPGRAMS, Bhoomi, Punjab Sewa Kendras.
- Limitations: digital divide, authentication failures, data privacy, cyber risks, legacy processes.
- Frameworks: NeGP, Digital India, DPDP Act 2023.
- Way forward: assisted digital access, grievance escalation, interoperable DPI, AI with safeguards.
- Conclusion: technology as enabler of citizen-centric governance.

</details>

**2.** Citizens' Charters have failed to deliver on their promise. Examine the reasons and evaluate whether Right to Service laws are a better alternative. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: 1997 adoption; UK roots.
- Reasons for failure: unenforceable, generic, no consultation, no grievance link.
- Sevottam as corrective.
- RTS laws: MP 2010, Punjab 2011 — time limits, penalties, appeals.
- Limits of RTS: limited services, weak penalties, capacity gaps.
- Way forward: charter + RTS + grievance redress + feedback loop.
- Conclusion: enforceable entitlements enhance trust.

</details>

**3.** What is the role of civil services in a democracy? How does Mission Karmayogi seek to reorient it? (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: permanent executive, continuity, policy advice, implementation.
- Values: neutrality, integrity, empathy, constitutional commitment.
- Challenges: politicisation, rule-bound culture, skill gaps.
- Karmayogi: rule-to-role, competency framework, iGOT, Capacity Building Commission.
- Conclusion: from 'steel frame' to 'flexible frame'.

</details>
