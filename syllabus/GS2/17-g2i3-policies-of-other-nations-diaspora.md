## Short notes

- US: tariff hikes on Indian goods (August 2025), H-1B fee of $100,000 for new petitions (Sept 2025 proclamation), stricter immigration and deportations — affect exports, IT services and Indian students/workers.
- US remains key partner in defence, technology (iCET/TRUST), critical minerals and Quad; CAATSA risk over Russian arms; sanctions on Iran affected Chabahar and oil imports.
- EU: Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (definitive phase from Jan 2026) affects Indian steel, aluminium exports; EU Deforestation Regulation and data rules (GDPR) shape trade.
- UK, Canada, Australia: tightened student visa and immigration rules (e.g. Canada's study permit caps from 2024) affect Indian students and remittances.
- China: trade deficit, export curbs on rare earths and critical inputs (2025), dumping concerns, BRI in neighbourhood, CPEC through PoK.
- Russia: discounted crude (Russia became India's top crude supplier after 2022), defence supplies, payment mechanism issues under Western sanctions.
- Gulf countries: home to the largest share of Indian migrant workers; labour laws (kafala-type sponsorship), energy supplies, investments (UAE, Saudi Arabia).
- Pakistan and Bangladesh politics affect border security, water and connectivity; Afghanistan policy affects terror threat and Central Asian access.
- Global monetary policy: US Federal Reserve rate changes drive capital flows and rupee volatility.
- Indian diaspora: over 3.5 crore NRIs and PIOs; India is the world's largest remittance recipient (about $129 billion in 2024, World Bank estimate).
- Institutional framework: Pravasi Bharatiya Divas (9 Jan, marking Gandhi's 1915 return), OCI card (2005), L.M. Singhvi High Level Committee on the Indian Diaspora (report 2002), Ministry of Overseas Indian Affairs merged with MEA (2016).
- Diaspora roles: remittances, investments, knowledge transfer, lobbying (e.g. support for India–US civil nuclear deal), soft power through culture and leadership positions abroad.
- Diaspora protection: evacuation operations — Ganga (Ukraine 2022), Kaveri (Sudan 2023), Ajay (Israel 2023), Sindhu (Iran/Israel 2025); eMigrate portal; Indian Community Welfare Fund.
- Challenges: exploitation of low-skilled migrants, illegal migration routes, extremist elements abroad, hate crimes, brain drain, dual-citizenship demands.
- Way forward: migration and mobility partnerships, skill certification, safe legal pathways, diaspora bonds/investment, outreach through missions.

## Punjab angle

- Punjab's diaspora is concentrated in Canada, UK, USA and Australia; remittances support Doaba's rural economy and 'NRI villages'.
- India–Canada ties strained after Canada's 2023 allegations over the killing of Hardeep Singh Nijjar and diplomatic expulsions (2024); partial reset with restoration of high commissioners in 2025.
- Feb 2025 US deportation flights with Indian illegal migrants, many from Punjab, landed in Amritsar — exposed 'donkey route' travel agents; Punjab acted against fraudulent agents.
- Student outmigration via IELTS-driven routes faces new caps in Canada, UK and Australia, affecting Punjab families and coaching economy.
- Punjab has an NRI Affairs Department and a State Commission for NRIs to address property disputes and grievances of overseas Punjabis.

## How PPSC asks it

Usually framed as impact of a specific country's policy (US tariffs/visas, EU CBAM, Canada tensions) on India, or role/protection of the diaspora. Use current facts, sector impact and India's response options; Punjab diaspora examples are valued.

## Deep dive

### Diaspora by the numbers (MEA data as of January 2026; census figures)

- Total overseas Indians: about 3.73 crore (37,280,845) — 1.95 crore Persons of Indian Origin (PIOs) and 1.78 crore Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) (MEA, 'Population of Overseas Indians', data as of January 2026).
- USA: about 60.8 lakh overseas Indians (37.7 lakh PIOs, 23.1 lakh NRIs) — the largest PIO community.
- UAE: about 43.4 lakh, almost all NRIs — the largest NRI population. Saudi Arabia about 27.5 lakh, Kuwait about 10.4 lakh.
- Canada: about 32.5 lakh (18.6 lakh PIOs, 13.9 lakh NRIs); UK: about 12.8 lakh in MEA's latest table (MEA revises country estimates from mission reports, so quote the year).
- Australia about 8.5 lakh, New Zealand about 3 lakh, Italy about 2.5 lakh — all important Punjabi destinations.
- Canada's 2021 Census: about 7.7 lakh people (2.1%) reported Sikhism as their religion, more than double the 2001 share (0.9%); British Columbia has the highest Sikh share of any province (5.9%) (Statistics Canada).
- England and Wales Census 2021: about 5.24 lakh Sikhs (0.9%); Wolverhampton (12.0%) and Sandwell (11.5%) have the highest shares (ONS).
- The US census does not ask about religion, so Sikh numbers there are only estimates (commonly put at around 5 lakh).

### Punjabi diaspora — history in phases (year — event)

- 1850s–1900s — Sikh soldiers and policemen serve across the British Empire (Hong Kong, Malaya, Shanghai, East Africa); some settle there.
- 1904–1908 — Punjabi migrants reach British Columbia and California (lumber mills, railways, farms); Canada's 'continuous journey' regulation (1908) is designed to block them.
- 1913 — the Ghadar Party is founded on the US west coast (Astoria/San Francisco) by Sohan Singh Bhakna, Lala Har Dayal and others, with Kartar Singh Sarabha among its young leaders.
- 1914 — the Komagata Maru (376 passengers) is refused entry at Vancouver; on its return, police fire on passengers at Budge Budge near Calcutta (29 September). Canada's PM Justin Trudeau apologised in the House of Commons in May 2016.
- 1923 — US v Bhagat Singh Thind: the US Supreme Court denies citizenship to Indians as 'not white'; this is reversed by the Luce–Celler Act 1946. Dalip Singh Saund becomes the first Asian-American Congressman (1957).
- 1950s–70s — labour migration to UK foundries and transport (Southall, Birmingham, Wolverhampton); 'twice migrants' come from East Africa after Kenyan Africanisation and Uganda's 1972 expulsion.
- 1967 onwards — Canada's points system opens skilled and family migration (Brampton, Surrey); the 1980s–90s bring asylum flows and diaspora politics around Punjab's militancy period.
- 2010s–2020s — a student-visa boom (IELTS route to Canada, Australia, UK), farm labour to Italy, and irregular 'donkey routes' to the US through Latin America.

### Policies of other countries that hit India and Punjab (2024–26)

- Canada: study-permit caps from January 2024 (about a 35% cut, tightened again for 2025); the Student Direct Stream ended in November 2024; post-graduation work permits restricted. This hurt Punjab's IELTS-coaching economy and families who financed study abroad through loans and land sales.
- Canada–India diplomatic arc: September 2023 allegation over the Nijjar killing → diplomats withdrawn and expelled (2023–24) → Canada's Foreign Interference Commission named India among foreign actors (January 2025) → Modi–Carney meeting at the G7 (June 2025) → high commissioners restored (2025) → Carney's visit to New Delhi (March 2026), with a uranium deal and a target to finish CEPA in 2026.
- USA: deportation flights (from February 2025, many landing at Amritsar) exposed travel-agent networks; a US$100,000 fee on new H-1B petitions (September 2025); tighter student-visa scrutiny. The February 2026 interim trade framework cut the reciprocal tariff on Indian goods to 18%.
- UK: the 2025 immigration white paper shortened the Graduate route and lengthened the path to settlement; at the same time the UK CETA (in force July 2026) eases short-term business mobility and gives posted workers social-security relief through the Double Contribution Convention.
- Australia: annual caps on new international student enrolments from 2025; Italy: exploitation of Sikh farmworkers in Latina (the 2024 death of Satnam Singh exposed illegal labour brokering).
- Gulf: oil prices, sponsor-based (kafala) labour systems and nationalisation of jobs (Saudisation, Emiratisation) shape the welfare of low-skilled migrants, including Punjabis in construction and transport.
- China: export curbs on rare-earth magnets and fertilisers (2025) and FDI screening show how a developing-country rival's policies affect Indian industry, including Punjab's Ludhiana auto-parts and cycle makers.

### India's institutional framework for the diaspora

- Constitution: Article 8 (citizenship for persons of Indian origin living abroad, by registration); Citizenship Act 1955, Sections 7A–7D, on Overseas Citizens of India — the OCI card dates from 2005, and the PIO card was merged into it in 2015. OCI holders cannot vote or hold constitutional or most government posts, and need permission to buy farmland.
- Emigration Act 1983: Protector General of Emigrants; 'Emigration Check Required' (ECR) passports for low-skilled workers going to notified (mostly Gulf) countries; e-Migrate portal (2015).
- Voting: since the 2010 amendment (Section 20A, Representation of the People Act 1950) NRIs can register and vote in person in their home constituency; proxy or postal voting for NRIs has not been enacted.
- Welfare schemes: Indian Community Welfare Fund (2009), Pravasi Bharatiya Bima Yojana (insurance for ECR workers), MADAD consular grievance portal (2015), Pravasi Bharatiya Sahayata Kendras, Know India Programme, Scholarship Programme for Diaspora Children.
- Engagement: Pravasi Bharatiya Divas (since 2003, on 9 January); the Ministry of Overseas Indian Affairs (2004) was merged into MEA in 2016; Pravasi Bharatiya Samman awards.
- Mobility: Migration and Mobility Partnership Agreements with France, Germany, the UK, Japan, Portugal, Italy and others; Pravasi Kaushal Vikas Yojana for pre-departure skilling.
- NRI marriages: the Law Commission's 287th Report (2024) recommended compulsory registration of NRI–Indian marriages; an earlier Registration of Marriage of NRI Bill (2019) lapsed. This matters greatly to abandoned brides in Punjab.

### Punjab's own NRI governance and the 'donkey route' problem

- Department of NRI Affairs, Punjab; Punjab State Commission for NRIs (a statutory body that hears NRI grievances such as property disputes); the NRI Sabha Punjab (Jalandhar) as a semi-official forum; NRI police stations and an NRI wing of Punjab Police.
- Laws against fraud: the Punjab Prevention of Human Smuggling Act 2012 and the Punjab Travel Professionals (Regulation) Act 2014, which licenses travel agents, IELTS centres and ticketing agents through Deputy Commissioners.
- After the February 2025 deportations, Punjab formed special teams and registered FIRs against fraudulent agents. The DC's licensing register and awareness camps are an SDM-level tool.
- Economy and society: remittances and NRI investment built the Doaba 'NRI belt' (Jalandhar, Hoshiarpur, Kapurthala, SBS Nagar) — large houses, gurdwaras, schools and hospitals funded by the diaspora; the flip side is locked houses, elderly parents living alone, and land left with absentee owners.
- Philanthropy: diaspora-funded village infrastructure and flood relief (2023 and 2025); there is potential for matching-grant schemes in which the state matches NRI contributions for village works.
- Politics: Canadian and UK Punjabi politicians (for example Jagmeet Singh, NDP leader 2017–2025; Preet Kaur Gill and Tanmanjeet Singh Dhesi, both elected UK MPs in 2017) give Punjab a voice abroad, but separatist fringes abroad complicate India's diplomacy.

### Answer frames, analysis tools and traps

- Frame for 'effect of policies of developed and developing countries': channel (trade, finance, technology, migration, energy, security) → specific policy → impact on India (sector, region, people) → India's response (negotiate, diversify, retaliate under WTO rules, build domestic capacity).
- Diaspora as an asset: remittances, investment, knowledge networks, lobbying (for example during the India–US civil nuclear deal), cultural soft power. As a liability: extremist fringes, brain drain, the cost of protecting citizens in crises.
- Remittance sources have shifted: RBI surveys show advanced economies (the US above all) now send more than the Gulf, reflecting a more skilled migrant profile.
- Evacuations to cite: Operation Rahat (Yemen 2015), Vande Bharat (2020), Ganga (Ukraine 2022), Kaveri (Sudan 2023), Ajay (Israel 2023), Sindhu (Iran 2025).
- Trap: Pravasi Bharatiya Divas marks Gandhi's return from South Africa on 9 January 1915; the L.M. Singhvi High Level Committee reported in 2002.
- Trap: a PIO is a foreign citizen of Indian origin; an NRI is an Indian citizen living abroad. The OCI is a lifelong visa, not dual citizenship.
- Punjab hook for every answer: student migration, deportations, NRI marriages, NRI property disputes, Doaba remittances.

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** Analyse how recent policies of the United States on trade and immigration affect India's economic and strategic interests. Suggest India's response. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: US as largest export market and strategic partner.
- Trade: 2025 tariffs, sectors hit (textiles, gems, leather, shrimp), MSME impact.
- Immigration: H-1B fee, student visa tightening, deportations.
- Strategic continuity: Quad, defence, technology.
- Response: negotiate bilateral trade deal, diversify markets via FTAs, boost domestic manufacturing, talent retention.
- Conclusion: resilience through diversification and engagement.

</details>

**2.** "The Indian diaspora is both an asset and a responsibility." Discuss, with special reference to the Punjabi diaspora. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: size, spread, remittances.
- Asset: remittances, investment, soft power, political influence abroad.
- Responsibility: evacuations, worker protection, illegal migration, consular support.
- Punjab: Canada/UK presence, remittance economy, extremist fringe and bilateral strain, deportations.
- Policies: OCI, PBD, eMigrate, ICWF, Punjab NRI Commission.
- Way forward: mobility partnerships, regulation of agents, diaspora engagement.
- Conclusion: two-way partnership.

</details>

**3.** What is the EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism? Discuss its implications for India. (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: carbon price on imports of carbon-intensive goods.
- Sectors: steel, aluminium, cement, fertilisers, hydrogen, electricity.
- Implications: export cost, MSME compliance burden, WTO and CBDR concerns.
- India's response: carbon credit trading scheme, green steel, negotiations.
- Conclusion: turn challenge into decarbonisation opportunity.

</details>
