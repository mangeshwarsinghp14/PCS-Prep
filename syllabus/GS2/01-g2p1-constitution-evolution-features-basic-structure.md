## Short notes

- Historical underpinnings: Regulating Act 1773 (first step of parliamentary control over the Company), Pitt's India Act 1784, Charter Acts 1813/1833/1853, GoI Act 1858 (Crown rule).
- Indian Councils Acts 1861, 1892, 1909 (Morley-Minto: communal electorates for Muslims); GoI Act 1919 (dyarchy in provinces, bicameral central legislature).
- GoI Act 1935: provincial autonomy, federal scheme, three lists, Federal Court (1937), RBI; roughly the largest single source of the Constitution's administrative text.
- Nehru Report 1928 and Karachi Resolution 1931 previewed fundamental rights; Cabinet Mission Plan 1946 created the Constituent Assembly (indirectly elected by provincial assemblies).
- Constituent Assembly first met 9 Dec 1946; Objectives Resolution moved by Nehru 13 Dec 1946 (adopted 22 Jan 1947); Drafting Committee chaired by B.R. Ambedkar.
- Constitution adopted 26 Nov 1949 (Constitution Day) and came fully into force 26 Jan 1950; took about 2 years 11 months 18 days.
- Salient features: lengthy written text, blend of rigidity and flexibility, federal system with unitary bias, parliamentary government, single citizenship, independent integrated judiciary, universal adult franchise.
- Fundamental Rights (Part III), DPSP (Part IV, non-justiciable, Art 37), Fundamental Duties (Part IVA, added by 42nd Amendment 1976 on Swaran Singh Committee advice; 11 duties after 86th Amendment 2002).
- Amendment under Art 368: simple majority (outside 368, e.g. Art 2, 3, 4), special majority, and special majority plus ratification by half the states for federal provisions.
- Landmark amendments: 1st (1951, Ninth Schedule), 42nd (1976, 'mini-Constitution'), 44th (1978, restored safeguards; property right moved to Art 300A), 52nd (1985, anti-defection), 61st (1989, voting age 18).
- 73rd/74th (1992, PRIs/ULBs), 86th (2002, Art 21A education), 101st (2016, GST), 103rd (2019, 10% EWS quota, upheld in Janhit Abhiyan 2022), 106th (2023, one-third women's reservation after next delimitation).
- Basic structure evolution: Shankari Prasad (1951) and Sajjan Singh (1965) allowed amendment of FRs; Golaknath (1967) barred it; 24th Amendment (1971) responded.
- Kesavananda Bharati (1973, 13 judges, 7:6): Parliament can amend any part but not the basic structure; Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain (1975) applied it to free and fair elections.
- Minerva Mills (1980): limited amending power and FR-DPSP balance are basic structure; Waman Rao (1981): Ninth Schedule laws after 24 Apr 1973 open to review; I.R. Coelho (2007) confirmed.
- S.R. Bommai (1994): secularism and federalism are basic features; Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record (NJAC case, 2015): 99th Amendment struck down to protect judicial independence.
- Elements commonly listed as basic structure: supremacy of Constitution, rule of law, judicial review, separation of powers, federalism, secularism, democracy, free and fair elections, parliamentary system, welfare state.
- Significant provisions to know: Art 14, 19, 21 (golden triangle per Maneka Gandhi 1978), Art 32 (Ambedkar's 'heart and soul'), Art 356, Art 370 (abrogated 2019, upheld by SC Dec 2023).

## Punjab angle

- Art 25 Explanation I recognises wearing and carrying of kirpan as part of profession of the Sikh religion; Explanation II groups Sikhs with Hindus for temple-entry/reform laws, a long-standing Sikh grievance.
- Two Sikh members of the Constituent Assembly (Hukam Singh and Bhupinder Singh Maan) did not sign the final Constitution in protest over minority safeguards.
- Punjab Reorganisation Act 1966 (under Art 3 and 4) created present Punjab, Haryana and UT Chandigarh and transferred hill areas to Himachal Pradesh.
- 59th Amendment (1988) permitted proclamation of Emergency in Punjab on grounds of internal disturbance; a series of amendments extended President's Rule in Punjab (1987–1992), a key case study on Art 356.
- Anandpur Sahib Resolution (1973) sought a restructured federation with Centre limited to defence, foreign affairs, communications and currency — useful for answers on federal features.

## How PPSC asks it

Questions ask how colonial Acts shaped the Constitution, why basic structure doctrine matters for democracy, or to assess a recent amendment against it. ## Deep dive

### Constitutional evolution — statute by statute (Act — what it added)

- Regulating Act 1773 — Governor-General of Bengal (Warren Hastings) with a council of four; Supreme Court at Calcutta (1774).
- Act of Settlement 1781 — limited the Supreme Court's jurisdiction over revenue matters and officials acting officially.
- Pitt's India Act 1784 — Board of Control: "double government" of Company and Crown.
- Charter Act 1833 — Governor-General of India (Bentinck); centralised legislation; first Law Member (Macaulay).
- Charter Act 1853 — separate legislative wing of the council; open competition for civil services.
- GoI Act 1858 — Crown rule; Secretary of State for India; Viceroy.
- Indian Councils Acts 1861 (portfolio system, ordinances, Indians nominated), 1892 (indirect election, budget discussion), 1909 (separate electorates for Muslims).
- GoI Act 1919 — dyarchy in provinces, bicameral central legislature, direct elections, separate electorates extended to Sikhs, Indian Christians, Anglo-Indians; led to the Public Service Commission (1926).
- GoI Act 1935 — 321 sections, 10 schedules; provincial autonomy (1937), proposed All-India Federation (never formed), dyarchy at the Centre, three lists, Federal Court, RBI.
- Indian Independence Act 1947 — ended paramountcy; the Constituent Assembly became a sovereign body and the dominion legislature.

### Ideas and compromises in the Constituent Assembly

- Due process: B.N. Rau's 1947 meeting with US judge Felix Frankfurter influenced the choice of "procedure established by law" (from Japan) over "due process".
- Language: the Munshi–Ayyangar formula (1949) — Hindi in Devanagari as official language, English to continue for 15 years, international numerals.
- Property: Art 31 compromise (compensation fixed by law) — later the source of decades of litigation and amendments.
- Minorities: separate electorates rejected; reservation kept only for SCs and STs; minority educational rights placed in Arts 29–30.
- Village vs individual: Gandhians wanted village republics; Ambedkar called the village "a sink of localism" — panchayats went into Art 40 (DPSP).
- Emergency provisions criticised by H.V. Kamath and others as a copy of Weimar Art 48.
- Ambedkar's 25 Nov 1949 warnings: give up unconstitutional methods ("grammar of anarchy"), avoid hero-worship (bhakti), turn political democracy into social democracy.
- Ambedkar hoped Art 356 would remain a "dead letter"; it was first used in Punjab on 20 June 1951.

### Basic structure — expanded case table (case — year — holding)

- Sajjan Singh (1965) — majority upheld the 17th Amendment, but Justices Hidayatullah and Mudholkar first doubted unlimited amending power.
- Golaknath (1967, 11 judges, 6:5) — Parliament cannot abridge Fundamental Rights; prospective overruling used.
- Kesavananda Bharati (24 April 1973, 13 judges, 7:6) — amending power cannot destroy the basic structure; Chief Justice Sikri's list included supremacy of the Constitution, republican and democratic government, secularism, separation of powers, federalism.
- Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain (1975) — free and fair elections; Minerva Mills (1980) — limited amending power, FR–DPSP balance, judicial review.
- S.P. Sampath Kumar (1987) — judicial review is basic; L. Chandra Kumar (1997) — High Court review of tribunals cannot be excluded.
- Kihoto Hollohan (1992) — Speaker's decisions under the Tenth Schedule are reviewable.
- M. Nagaraj (2006) — "equality code" principles; I.R. Coelho (2007) — Ninth Schedule laws after 24 April 1973 open to basic-structure review.
- Basic structure tests constitutional amendments, not ordinary laws (State of Karnataka v Union, 1977; Kuldip Nayar, 2006).
- Supreme Court Advocates-on-Record (2015) — judicial independence; Janhit Abhiyan (2022) — EWS quota does not violate it.
- In re Article 370 (11 Dec 2023) — abrogation upheld; Dr Balram Singh (25 Nov 2024) — "socialist" and "secular" in the Preamble upheld.
- Exported: Bangladesh adopted it (Anwar Hossain Chowdhury, 1989); Kenya's High Court and Court of Appeal applied it in the BBI case (2021), though Kenya's Supreme Court (2022) declined to; Germany's Basic Law Art 79(3) has an explicit "eternity clause".

### Significant provisions — the "living document" 2017–2026

- Puttaswamy (2017) privacy; Navtej (2018); Joseph Shine (2018); Sabarimala reference (2019) on essential practices — pending.
- Art 142 "complete justice" was used in Ayodhya (2019) and in State of Tamil Nadu v Governor (April 2025) to deem Bills assented.
- Presidential Reference opinion (20 Nov 2025, 5 judges): courts cannot fix timelines or "deemed assent" for Arts 200/201; a Governor has three options — assent, withhold and return with a message, or reserve for the President; he cannot sit on Bills indefinitely, and prolonged unexplained inaction is open to limited judicial review.
- Sita Soren (2024) — no immunity for legislators taking bribes; ADR (Feb 2024) — electoral bonds struck down.
- State of Punjab v Davinder Singh (2024) — sub-classification of SCs; Property Owners Association (2024) — Art 39(b) read narrowly.
- Mineral Area Development Authority (July 2024, 9 judges, 8:1) — royalty is not a tax; states can tax mineral rights.
- SIR judgment (May 2026) — Arts 324 and 326 read together to uphold roll purification.
- Pending constitutional changes: 129th Bill (ONOE), 130th Bill (removal of detained ministers); the 2026 delimitation amendment was defeated (April 2026).

### Punjab and the constitutional story

- GoI Act 1919 gave Sikhs separate electorates for the first time; Jallianwala Bagh (13 April 1919) and the Rowlatt Act formed the backdrop.
- Under the 1935 Act the Unionist Party (Sikandar Hayat Khan) formed Punjab's provincial government in 1937; Khizar Hayat Tiwana led a coalition in 1946.
- Cabinet Mission (1946) grouped Punjab in Section B with Muslim-majority provinces; Sikh leaders rejected it as ignoring their interests.
- Constitution (SC) Order 1950 limited SC status to Hindus; Sikh SCs were added in 1956 after Sikh demands.
- Art 25 Explanation II grouping Sikhs with Hindus remains a grievance; the NCRWC (2002) suggested rewording it.
- Art 356 was first used anywhere in India in Punjab (20 June 1951); the 1987–92 spell remains the longest.
- Punjab-origin Supreme Court rulings: State of Punjab v Principal Secretary to Governor (2023), Davinder Singh (2024), Gurbaksh Singh Sibbia (1980, anticipatory bail), Golaknath (1967, from Punjab land-ceiling laws).

### Mains answer frameworks

- "Living document": 106 amendments between 1951 and 2023 (US: 27 in over 230 years) plus judicial interpretation (Art 21) and conventions.
- "Federal with a unitary bias": list Arts 3, 249–253, 256–257, 356, single citizenship, integrated judiciary; balance with Bommai, GST Council voting and Finance Commission devolution.
- Basic structure — critique: unelected judges, vagueness, judicial supremacy; defence: protects against majoritarian capture, now copied abroad.
- Use Ambedkar's "constitutional morality" (borrowed from George Grote) — applied in Navtej and NCT Delhi (2018).
- Link historical underpinnings to current debates: 1919 dyarchy → Governor–state friction; 1935 lists → Centre-heavy distribution; 1909/1919 separate electorates → rejection of communal representation.
- Always add Punjab examples: Art 356 history, Punjabi Suba under Art 3, Governor–Assembly disputes (2023).

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** "The Government of India Act, 1935 is the blueprint of the Indian Constitution." Critically examine this statement, highlighting the features in which the Constitution departed from colonial legislation. (250 words)

*Like a real PPSC question: [Mains 2026 · GS2 Q11](../../past-papers/mains_2026_GS2.md#q11)*

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: 1935 Act as the most detailed colonial statute; large share of administrative provisions carried over.
- Borrowed: federal scheme, three lists, Governor's office, Federal Court-to-Supreme Court idea, emergency provisions, public service commissions.
- Departures: sovereignty of the people (Preamble), universal adult franchise instead of limited property franchise, justiciable Fundamental Rights, DPSP.
- Responsible government at the Centre, abolition of separate electorates, single citizenship, judicial review over legislation.
- Indian innovations: Fundamental Duties (later), amendment flexibility, Panchayati Raj vision.
- Critique: 'unitary bias' and Art 356 as colonial residues; Ambedkar's defence that form is borrowed but spirit is new.
- Conclusion: continuity of machinery, transformation of philosophy.

</details>

**2.** Trace the evolution of the basic structure doctrine. Has it strengthened or weakened parliamentary democracy in India? (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: doctrine as judicial limit on amending power under Art 368.
- Chronology: Shankari Prasad 1951, Sajjan Singh 1965, Golaknath 1967, 24th Amendment 1971, Kesavananda 1973.
- Consolidation: Indira Gandhi v Raj Narain 1975, Minerva Mills 1980, Waman Rao 1981, Bommai 1994, Coelho 2007, NJAC 2015.
- Strengthened democracy: protected elections, judicial review, federalism from Emergency-era excesses (39th, 42nd amendments).
- Critique: counter-majoritarian; vague, judge-defined content; tension with parliamentary supremacy (e.g. NJAC debate).
- Way forward: judicial restraint, dialogue between institutions, clearer reasoning on what is 'basic'.
- Conclusion: doctrine acts as a constitutional anchor rather than a veto on reform.

</details>

**3.** Discuss the significance of the 42nd and 44th Constitutional Amendments in shaping the balance between the State and individual liberty in India. (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: Emergency context (1975–77).
- 42nd: 'socialist, secular, integrity' in Preamble; Fundamental Duties; DPSP primacy over FRs (Art 31C expanded); curbs on judicial review; Art 74 advice binding.
- 44th: 'armed rebellion' replaced 'internal disturbance' for Art 352; written Cabinet advice for Emergency; Art 20 and 21 cannot be suspended.
- Property right removed from Part III (Art 300A); restoration of judicial powers.
- Minerva Mills struck parts of 42nd (Art 368(4),(5)).
- Conclusion: lessons in institutional safeguards against executive excess.

</details>
