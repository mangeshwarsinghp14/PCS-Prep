## Short notes

- United Nations (1945; 193 members): six principal organs — General Assembly, Security Council, ECOSOC, Trusteeship Council (suspended 1994), ICJ, Secretariat.
- UNSC: 15 members (5 permanent with veto, 10 elected for two years); India elected eight times, latest 2021–22; India seeks permanent seat with G4; reform discussed in Intergovernmental Negotiations (IGN).
- ICJ (The Hague): 15 judges, nine-year terms; India won provisional measures and merits in Kulbhushan Jadhav case (2019).
- UN Human Rights Council: 47 members elected by UNGA; Universal Periodic Review.
- Specialised agencies: WHO (Geneva; Pandemic Agreement adopted May 2025; US withdrawal notified 2025), ILO (1919; tripartite), FAO, UNESCO, ICAO, IMO, ITU, WIPO.
- Bretton Woods: IMF (quotas, SDR basket of five currencies, surveillance, lending) and World Bank Group (IBRD, IDA, IFC, MIGA, ICSID — India is not an ICSID member).
- WTO (1995; 166 members): consensus-based; Appellate Body non-functional since Dec 2019; issues for India — public stockholding, fisheries subsidies, e-commerce moratorium.
- Development banks: ADB (1966, Manila), AIIB (2016, Beijing; India second-largest shareholder), New Development Bank (2015, Shanghai; first president K.V. Kamath).
- FATF (1989): sets AML/CFT standards; grey list; India's mutual evaluation (2024) placed it in 'regular follow-up', the top category.
- Other bodies: IAEA (Vienna), UNEP (Nairobi), UNFCCC, IPCC, Interpol (Lyon), Commonwealth (56 members), G20 (1999), IEA (India an association country).
- Indian-led bodies: International Solar Alliance, CDRI, Global Biofuels Alliance.
- Critiques: post-1945 power structures, veto paralysis (Ukraine, Gaza), under-representation of Global South in IMF quotas, funding cuts, rising unilateralism.
- India's stance: 'reformed multilateralism', voice of the Global South (Voice of Global South Summits), one of the largest cumulative troop contributors to UN peacekeeping.
- Way forward: UNSC expansion, IMF quota reform, restoring WTO dispute settlement, climate finance commitments, plurilateral coalitions.

## Punjab angle

- WTO agriculture rules matter for Punjab's MSP-based wheat and paddy procurement; India invoked the Bali Peace Clause for rice in 2020.
- FATF standards on terror financing and money laundering are relevant to cross-border narco-terror funding through Punjab's border.
- Multilateral lenders (World Bank, AIIB) have supported canal-based urban water supply projects for Punjab cities such as Amritsar and Ludhiana.
- UNODC reports on Golden Crescent drug trafficking provide context for Punjab's drug crisis.
- FAO and UNEP frameworks on crop residue and air quality inform Punjab's stubble management debates.

## Deep dive

### 2025–26 developments to update your notes

- WTO MC14 (Yaoundé, Cameroon, March 2026): ended without consensus. The moratorium on customs duties on electronic transmissions (in place since 1998) was not extended, and WTO reform and agriculture had no breakthrough; work moved back to Geneva.
- WTO Agreement on Fisheries Subsidies (adopted at MC12, 2022) came into force in September 2025 after two-thirds of members accepted it; the second phase on subsidies that cause overfishing is still under negotiation. India seeks long transition periods for small fishers.
- UN Secretary-General selection 2026: the process opened on 25 November 2025; candidates held interactive dialogues in April and June 2026; Security Council straw polls began on 30 July 2026, and Rebeca Grynspan (Costa Rica, UNCTAD head) led them as of late October 2026 without a formal recommendation. António Guterres's term ends on 31 December 2026. Check the final appointment before the exam.
- WHO: the Pandemic Agreement was adopted by the World Health Assembly in May 2025, with an annex on pathogen access and benefit-sharing still to be negotiated; the US gave notice of withdrawal from WHO in January 2025.
- ICJ advisory opinion on states' climate obligations (July 2025): states have binding duties to prevent significant climate harm. Useful for 'international law and climate justice' answers.
- BRICS: India chaired in 2026 and hosted the 18th summit (New Delhi, September 2026); the New Development Bank and the Contingent Reserve Arrangement remain BRICS's financial institutions.
- India is a candidate for a non-permanent UNSC seat for 2028–29; its last terms were 2011–12 and 2021–22.

### UN system — structure details examiners test

- The Charter was signed on 26 June 1945 at San Francisco and came into force on 24 October 1945; India signed as an original member before independence.
- General Assembly: one state, one vote; 'important questions' need a two-thirds majority (Art 18). The Uniting for Peace resolution (377A, 1950) lets the GA act when the Security Council is deadlocked. Since 2022 (resolution 76/262) the GA meets within 10 working days whenever a veto is cast.
- Security Council: Chapter VI covers peaceful settlement and Chapter VII enforcement (Arts 39–51); the veto is in Art 27(3); Art 51 preserves self-defence. The Charter's only size change came in 1965, from 11 to 15 members.
- Reforming the Council needs a Charter amendment under Art 108: two-thirds of the GA plus ratification by two-thirds of members, including all five permanent members.
- ECOSOC: 54 members elected for three years; it coordinates specialised agencies. The Trusteeship Council suspended work in 1994 after Palau became independent.
- ICJ: 15 judges with 9-year terms, a third elected every three years by both the GA and the Council. Indian judges: B.N. Rau, Nagendra Singh (President 1985–88), R.S. Pathak, Dalveer Bhandari.
- Secretary-General: appointed by the GA on the Security Council's recommendation (Art 97); five-year terms by convention; regional rotation is informal.
- Peacekeeping: the first mission was UNTSO (1948). India is among the largest cumulative troop contributors and has lost the most peacekeepers; it sent the first all-women Formed Police Unit (Liberia, 2007) and runs the Centre for UN Peacekeeping in New Delhi.

### Bretton Woods and development finance

- IMF: a member's quota decides its votes, how much it can borrow and its SDR allocation. The US vote share is above 15%, which gives it a veto over decisions needing 85%. The 16th General Review (December 2023) agreed a 50% quota increase without shifting shares, so the Global South's call for realignment is still open.
- SDR basket: US dollar, euro, Chinese renminbi (added 2016), Japanese yen, pound sterling. Lending tools: Stand-By Arrangement, Extended Fund Facility, Poverty Reduction and Growth Trust, and the Resilience and Sustainability Trust (2022) for climate and pandemic resilience.
- India and the IMF: borrowing in 1981 (EFF) and the 1991 balance-of-payments crisis; India now undergoes routine Article IV surveillance and contributes to IMF resources.
- World Bank Group: IBRD (1944), IDA (1960, concessional loans; India was its largest cumulative borrower and graduated in 2014), IFC (1956, private sector), MIGA (1988, investment guarantees), ICSID (1966, investment disputes — India is not a member). President: Ajay Banga (since 2023).
- Debt architecture: the G20 Common Framework (2020) for debt restructuring; Sri Lanka (2022–23) shows India acting as a bilateral creditor and backing the IMF programme early.

### WTO — agreements and India's positions

- The WTO was set up in 1995 by the Marrakesh Agreement. The Ministerial Conference meets every two years; the General Council runs it in between; the Dispute Settlement Body has had no functioning Appellate Body since December 2019. India has not joined the EU-led interim arrangement (MPIA).
- Agreement on Agriculture: amber box (trade-distorting support, capped at a 10% de minimis level for developing countries), blue box (production-limiting) and green box (minimally distorting). The Bali 2013 'peace clause' shields public stockholding for food security until a permanent solution; India invoked it for rice in 2020.
- Nairobi 2015: export subsidies on farm goods to be eliminated. MC12 (Geneva 2022): fisheries agreement, a TRIPS waiver for COVID-19 vaccines, food-security declarations.
- India's stands: a permanent solution on public stockholding, special and differential treatment, no inclusion of the China-backed Investment Facilitation for Development agreement as a plurilateral without consensus, and policy space on e-commerce duties.
- Director-General Ngozi Okonjo-Iweala (Nigeria) began a second term in September 2025.
- Punjab link: MSP-based procurement of wheat and paddy draws scrutiny under amber-box limits because external reference prices are frozen at 1986–88 levels; India argues for updating the method.

### Other institutions with India and Punjab relevance

- FATF (1989): 40 Recommendations and grey/black lists; India became a member in 2010; Pakistan was on the grey list from 2018 to 2022. Relevant to cross-border narco-terror finance through Punjab.
- UNODC (Vienna): the World Drug Report tracks Golden Crescent (Afghanistan–Pakistan–Iran) opiate routes that feed Punjab's drug problem. The International Narcotics Control Board monitors the drug control treaties.
- Interpol (Lyon): the CBI is India's National Central Bureau; the 'Bharatpol' portal (2025) speeds up requests for Red Corner Notices against fugitive gangsters, including Punjab-linked gang operators abroad.
- ILO (1919, Geneva, tripartite): India is a founding member but has not ratified core conventions 87 and 98 on freedom of association and collective bargaining.
- India-led bodies: International Solar Alliance (treaty-based, HQ Gurugram), Coalition for Disaster Resilient Infrastructure (2019), Global Biofuels Alliance (2023), International Big Cat Alliance (2024).
- Headquarters matching (frequent MCQ): FAO, IFAD and WFP in Rome; UNEP and UN-Habitat in Nairobi; IAEA and UNIDO in Vienna; UNESCO in Paris; ICAO in Montreal; IMO in London; UPU in Bern; ITU, WIPO, WMO, WHO and ILO in Geneva.
- Commonwealth: 56 members; India hosted CHOGM in 1983. India is an observer in the Arctic Council (2013) and an association country of the IEA.

### Reform debates and answer frames

- UNSC reform groups: G4 (India, Brazil, Germany, Japan), the L.69 group of developing countries, the African Union's Ezulwini Consensus (two permanent seats with veto for Africa), and Uniting for Consensus (Italy, Pakistan and others; against new permanent seats). Talks happen in the GA's Intergovernmental Negotiations.
- India's case: population, economy, peacekeeping record, democratic legitimacy, and voice of the Global South (Voice of Global South summits since 2023).
- Counter-arguments to address: regional rivals' opposition, the veto question, the difficulty of the Charter amendment, and China's position.
- Why institutions are weakening: great-power rivalry and veto paralysis (Ukraine, Gaza), funding cuts and withdrawals, a WTO without an appeals body, the shift to minilaterals (Quad, I2U2) and plurilaterals.
- Frame: mandate and structure → performance → legitimacy deficit → India's stake → reform proposals (UNSC expansion, IMF quota realignment, restoring WTO appeals, climate finance, AI governance).
- Trap: the Trusteeship Council still exists on paper; only its operations are suspended.
- Trap: India is in the MTCR but not the NSG; China is in the NSG but not the MTCR.

**3.** Compare the mandates of the IMF and the World Bank. Why do developing countries demand their reform? (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: Bretton Woods twins (1944).
- IMF: macro stability, BoP support, surveillance, SDR.
- World Bank: long-term development finance (IBRD, IDA).
- Reform demands: quota and voting shares, leadership convention, conditionality, climate finance.
- Alternatives: AIIB, NDB.
- Conclusion: fair voice for emerging economies.

</details>
