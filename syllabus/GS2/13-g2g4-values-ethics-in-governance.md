## Short notes

- Ethical concerns in government: conflict of interest, nepotism and favouritism, misuse of discretion, policy capture by interest groups, leakage of information, partisan behaviour, abuse of power.
- Ethical concerns in private institutions: corporate fraud (Satyam 2009, IL&FS 2018), unfair labour practices, data misuse, environmental violations, greenwashing, insider trading, cartelisation.
- Dilemmas: public interest vs political directions, loyalty to superiors vs conscience, rules vs compassion, transparency vs confidentiality, efficiency vs equity.
- Sources of ethical guidance — laws (e.g. PCA 1988, IPC/BNS), rules (CCS Conduct Rules 1964), regulations (SEBI codes), and conscience (inner voice).
- Laws are minimum standards and externally enforced; ethics is a higher, internalised standard — legal is not always ethical (e.g. historical untouchability practices were once 'legal').
- Conscience: Gandhi's 'inner voice' and talisman; Kant's categorical imperative; conscience can be shaped by bias, so must be informed by reason and constitutional values.
- Constitutional morality (Ambedkar; used in Naz Foundation/Navtej Johar 2018, Sabarimala 2018) as an ethical guide for public servants.
- Accountability and ethical governance: Nolan Committee (UK, 1995) seven principles — selflessness, integrity, objectivity, accountability, openness, honesty, leadership.
- 2nd ARC 4th Report 'Ethics in Governance': code of ethics for ministers and civil servants, Lokpal, protection for whistleblowers, ethics audits.
- Ethical governance tools: codes of conduct, conflict-of-interest declarations, asset disclosure, social audit, RTI, whistleblower protection, ethics training.
- Ethics in international relations: realism (national interest, power) vs idealism (moral norms); Panchsheel (1954); NAM; responsibility to protect (R2P, 2005); humanitarian intervention debates.
- Indian examples: Vaccine Maitri (2021), Operation Dost (Türkiye earthquake 2023), Operation Brahma (Myanmar earthquake 2025), evacuation of foreign nationals alongside Indians.
- Refugees and migrants: India is not party to the 1951 Refugee Convention; handles refugees through executive policy (Tibetans, Sri Lankan Tamils) — ethical tension with security concerns.
- Climate ethics: common but differentiated responsibilities, climate justice, loss and damage fund; India's LiFE (Lifestyle for Environment) initiative.
- Ethical issues in funding: foreign aid conditionality, debt-trap lending, tied aid, IMF austerity conditions, foreign funding of NGOs (FCRA), ESG and ethical investing, sovereign wealth fund choices.
- Way forward: ethics infrastructure — leadership by example, transparent processes, protection to honest officers, value-based training.

## Deep dive

### Ethical frameworks — what each tells a district officer

- Kant (duty ethics): act only on rules you could will for everyone; never treat people merely as means — no lying to the public 'for their own good', no using a poor complainant as a pawn.
- Utilitarianism (Bentham, J.S. Mill): choose the action with the best overall consequences — useful for resource allocation (where to place a relief camp), but must be checked against rights of minorities.
- Virtue ethics (Aristotle): ask what a person of good character would do; integrity, courage, temperance and practical wisdom (phronesis) matter more than rule-books in grey areas.
- Rawls' justice as fairness (1971): arrange benefits to help the least advantaged most — supports targeting subsidies and priority to vulnerable groups.
- Care ethics: attention to relationships and the vulnerable — elderly flood victims, drug-dependent youth, migrants' families.
- Gandhian ethics: means must be as pure as ends; the talisman of the poorest person; trusteeship of wealth and power.
- Sikh ethical ideals: kirat (honest work), seva (selfless service), vand chhakna (sharing), sarbat da bhala (welfare of all), equality symbolised by langar and pangat.
- Kautilya: officials are to be tested for integrity and watched, since embezzlement is as hard to detect as fish drinking water — the realist case for institutional checks.
- Exam method: in case studies, show at least two frameworks reaching the same answer, or explain why you prefer one when they conflict.

### Seven-step dilemma resolution (write it as a flow in case studies)

- 1. Identify stakeholders and their legitimate interests.
- 2. State the ethical issues and the values in conflict (e.g. compassion vs rule of law; transparency vs privacy).
- 3. List options — at least three, including a creative middle path.
- 4. Test each option: legality (Constitution, Acts, rules), ethics (frameworks above), feasibility (time, money, staff), public trust.
- 5. Choose and justify; mention what you will do immediately and what over the medium term.
- 6. Act transparently: record reasons in writing, inform seniors, communicate with those affected.
- 7. Review: learn, fix the system so the dilemma does not recur (SOPs, technology, training).

### Law, rules and conscience — when they collide

- Legal but unethical: using a loophole to favour a relative in allotment; obeying a lawful order that will clearly cause avoidable harm without recording dissent.
- Ethical but illegal: releasing confidential data to expose wrongdoing without using protected channels — the motive may be good, but process matters; use whistleblower and vigilance routes.
- 'Following orders' is no defence for manifestly illegal acts — principle established at the Nuremberg trials (1945–46); Hannah Arendt's 'banality of evil' (1963) warns of thoughtless obedience.
- Indian whistleblower cases: Satyendra Dubey (NHAI engineer, murdered 2003 after exposing road-contract corruption) led to the government's 2004 whistleblower resolution and later the Whistle Blowers Protection Act, 2014; S. Manjunath (IOC officer, murdered 2005 for acting against fuel adulteration).
- Civil disobedience tradition: Thoreau (1849) and Gandhi — open, non-violent breach of an unjust law while accepting the penalty; a civil servant, however, must work through institutional dissent, not public defiance.
- Constitutional morality (B.R. Ambedkar) — fidelity to constitutional values (dignity, equality, liberty) above personal or popular morality; the Supreme Court used it in Navtej Johar (2018).
- Practical rule for officers: if an instruction seems wrong, seek it in writing, record your objection on file, and escalate; obey lawful orders, refuse clearly illegal ones.

### Accountability map — who holds the public servant to account

- Political: ministers answerable to the legislature (question hour, committees, no-confidence); officers answerable to ministers.
- Administrative/hierarchical: superiors, annual performance appraisal (APAR), departmental inquiries under conduct and discipline rules.
- Financial: CAG audit, Public Accounts Committee, internal audit, General Financial Rules.
- Judicial: writs (Articles 32 and 226), judicial review of administrative action, contempt for non-compliance.
- Quasi-judicial and statutory: Lokpal and Lokayuktas, vigilance commissions, information commissions, human rights commissions.
- Social: RTI, social audit (pioneered by MKSS public hearings in Rajasthan in the 1990s; mandatory for MGNREGA works under Section 17 of the 2005 Act), media, citizen charters.
- Distinguish: responsibility (duty assigned), answerability (duty to explain), accountability (answerability plus consequences).

### Ethics in private institutions — cases and safeguards

- Enron (2001): accounting fraud led to the US Sarbanes–Oxley Act (2002) on auditor independence and CEO certification of accounts.
- Satyam (2009): founder confessed to inflated cash balances — exposed weak boards and auditors in India; strengthened independent-director and audit rules in the Companies Act, 2013.
- IL&FS (2018): default of a large infrastructure lender showed governance failures in complex group structures.
- Volkswagen 'dieselgate' (2015): software to cheat emission tests — ethics of compliance vs culture.
- Bhopal gas tragedy (1984, Union Carbide plant): the defining Indian case on corporate responsibility for safety and on delayed justice to victims.
- Indian safeguards: vigil mechanism (Companies Act Section 177), CSR spending (Section 135), SEBI's Business Responsibility and Sustainability Report (BRSR) for the top 1,000 listed companies (mandatory from 2022-23).
- Ethical issues in private service delivery: private hospitals' billing, private schools' fees, gig workers' rights, data privacy (Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023).

### International relations and funding — extra cases

- Just war theory: jus ad bellum (just cause, last resort, proportionality) and jus in bello (distinction between combatants and civilians) — frame questions on terrorism and conflict.
- Sanctions dilemma: pressure on regimes vs suffering of ordinary citizens (food and medicine shortages).
- Vaccine equity: COVAX and vaccine nationalism during COVID-19; India's Vaccine Maitri as soft-power plus humanitarian action.
- Aid effectiveness: Paris Declaration (2005) principles — ownership, alignment, harmonisation, managing for results, mutual accountability.
- Political funding ethics: the Supreme Court struck down the Electoral Bond Scheme on 15 February 2024 (ADR v. Union of India) for violating voters' right to information.
- NGO funding: FCRA, 2010 as amended in 2020 — ban on transferring foreign funds to other NGOs, administrative expenses capped at 20%, mandatory FCRA account at SBI's New Delhi main branch; debate between transparency and civil-society space.
- Ethics of migration diplomacy: dignity of deportees, regulation of recruitment and travel agents, protection of overseas workers.

## Mains practice questions (3)

**1.** "Laws, rules and regulations are necessary but not sufficient sources of ethical guidance for a civil servant." Discuss the role of conscience with examples. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: distinguish legality from morality.
- Laws/rules: clarity, uniformity, enforceability, minimum standard.
- Limits: grey areas, outdated rules, unjust laws, discretion.
- Conscience: inner voice; Gandhi, Kant; constitutional morality as anchor.
- Risks: subjective bias — needs reasoning, consultation.
- Example: SDM facing pressure on flood relief allotment or land demolition.
- Conclusion: law sets the floor, conscience lifts the ceiling.
