## Short notes

- Probity: adherence to the highest standards of honesty and integrity in public life, going beyond legal compliance to avoid even the appearance of impropriety.
- Philosophical basis: Kautilya's Arthashastra (listed many ways officials embezzle; officials compared to fish drinking water unseen), Gita's nishkama karma, Plato's guardians, Gandhi's trusteeship and 'seven social sins', Kant's duty ethics.
- Governance vs probity: probity is the ethical foundation that makes governance legitimate; components — integrity, impartiality, accountability, transparency, fairness in use of resources.
- Corporate governance: Kumar Mangalam Birla Committee (1999) led to Clause 49; Narayana Murthy Committee (2003); Uday Kotak Committee (2017); SEBI (LODR) Regulations 2015.
- Companies Act 2013: independent directors (one-third on listed boards), at least one woman director for specified companies, audit committee, vigil mechanism, CSR (Sec 135), auditor rotation.
- RTI Act 2005: Sec 4 proactive disclosure; Sec 6 request; Sec 7 reply within 30 days (48 hours for life or liberty); Sec 8 exemptions; Sec 11 third-party information; Sec 24 intelligence/security bodies exempt except corruption and human rights allegations.
- RTI appeals: first appeal within the department, second to Information Commission; penalty on PIO up to ₹25,000 (Sec 20).
- RTI concerns: 2019 amendment on tenure/salary, DPDP Act 2023 amending Sec 8(1)(j) to exempt all personal information, pendency in commissions, attacks on RTI activists.
- Codes of ethics vs codes of conduct: ethics codes state values (broad, aspirational); conduct codes list specific dos and don'ts (CCS (Conduct) Rules 1964, AIS (Conduct) Rules 1968, Code of Conduct for Ministers).
- Citizens' charters and service delivery: Sevottam; Right to Service laws; grievance redress (CPGRAMS) — probity in delivery means timeliness, non-discrimination, no rent-seeking.
- Work culture: punctuality, merit-based promotion, performance appraisal (APAR), team ethic; problems — red tapism, risk aversion, 'file-pushing', fear of vigilance cases.
- Use of public funds: Art 266/267 funds, CAG audit, PAC scrutiny, General Financial Rules 2017, PFMS, GeM e-procurement, outcome budgets; avoid wasteful expenditure and fiscal opacity.
- Anti-corruption laws: Prevention of Corruption Act 1988 (2018 amendment made bribe-giving an offence and added Sec 17A prior approval for investigating officials), Lokpal Act 2013, PMLA 2002, Benami Act (amended 2016), Fugitive Economic Offenders Act 2018, Whistle Blowers Protection Act 2014 (not fully operational).
- India ratified the UN Convention against Corruption (UNCAC) in 2011.
- Causes of corruption: discretion plus monopoly minus accountability (Klitgaard), election funding, low transparency, complex procedures, social tolerance, delays in trial.
- Measures: reduce discretion via technology (DBT, e-procurement, online land records), faster trials, asset disclosure, protection to whistleblowers, ethics training, citizen vigilance.

## Punjab angle

- Punjab Vigilance Bureau is the state anti-corruption agency; the Anti-Corruption Action Line (March 2022) accepts citizen complaints with evidence.
- PPSC 'cash-for-jobs' scam (2002) — classic illustration of probity failure in recruitment and its long-term institutional cost.
- Punjab's 2018 Transparency and Accountability in Delivery of Public Services Act (successor to the 2011 Right to Service Act) and Sewa Kendras reduce discretion and face-to-face rent-seeking.
- Punjab State Information Commission handles RTI appeals; online land record (fard) access improves transparency at tehsil level.
- Fiscal probity issue: high committed expenditure (salaries, pensions, interest, power subsidy) in Punjab's budget limits development spending.

## Deep dive

### RTI Act 2005 — section map beyond the basics

- Sec 2(f) 'information' includes records, e-mails, opinions, advice, contracts, samples and data in electronic form; Sec 2(h) 'public authority' covers bodies set up by the Constitution or law and bodies 'substantially financed' by government; Sec 2(j) right includes inspection and certified copies.
- Sec 4(1)(b): 17 categories of proactive disclosure (functions, powers, norms, budgets, subsidy beneficiaries, officers' directory and pay).
- Sec 5: every public authority designates PIOs and APIOs; Sec 6(2): an applicant need not give reasons; Sec 6(3): transfer to the right authority within 5 days.
- Sec 7: 30 days (48 hours for life or liberty; +5 days via APIO; 40 days if third party involved); Sec 7(6): information free if the deadline is missed; Sec 7(8): rejection must state reasons and appeal details.
- Sec 8(1)(a)–(j): exemptions (sovereignty and security, contempt, privilege, commercial confidence, fiduciary, foreign confidence, endangering life, impeding investigation, cabinet papers until decision, personal information).
- Sec 8(2): public-interest override even for exempt information; Sec 8(3): most information about events 20 years old must be given; Sec 10: severability — release the non-exempt part.
- Sec 12–13 and 15–16: Central and State Information Commissions (Chief + up to 10 Commissioners); Sec 18: complaints; Sec 19(1): first appeal within 30 days; Sec 19(3): second appeal within 90 days.
- Sec 20: penalty Rs 250 per day up to Rs 25,000 on the PIO, plus recommendation of disciplinary action for persistent default; Sec 22: overriding effect over the Official Secrets Act and other laws; Sec 25: annual reports by Commissions.

### Landmark RTI rulings

- CBSE v. Aditya Bandopadhyay (2011): evaluated answer sheets are 'information'; examinees can inspect them.
- Girish Ramchandra Deshpande v. CIC (2012): service records, assets and tax returns of an employee are personal information, exempt unless larger public interest is shown.
- Thalappalam Service Co-operative Bank v. State of Kerala (2013): a body is a public authority only if 'substantially financed' or controlled by government — co-operatives are not automatically covered.
- RBI v. Jayantilal N. Mistry (2015): RBI cannot refuse bank inspection reports by citing a fiduciary relationship with banks.
- CPIO, Supreme Court v. Subhash Chandra Agarwal (November 2019): the office of the Chief Justice of India is a public authority; judges' asset details can be disclosed, balancing privacy and judicial independence.
- CIC order (June 2013) held six national political parties to be public authorities; the parties did not comply and the issue has stayed before the Supreme Court.
- Legislative changes: RTI (Amendment) Act, 2019 lets the Centre prescribe Commissioners' tenure and salary (3-year term under 2019 rules); DPDP Act, 2023 amended Sec 8(1)(j) to exempt personal information without the earlier public-interest test inside the clause.

### Codes of conduct — rules officers are tested on

- CCS (Conduct) Rules, 1964 — Rule 3: absolute integrity, devotion to duty, nothing unbecoming of a government servant; 2014 amendment added values such as political neutrality, accountability, transparency and courtesy.
- Rule 3A: promptness and courtesy; Rule 3C: prohibition of sexual harassment at the workplace.
- Rule 4: no use of position to secure jobs for family members in private firms; Rule 5: no participation in politics or elections; Rule 7: no strikes or demonstrations affecting public order.
- Rule 9: no public criticism of government policy; Rule 11: no unauthorised communication of official information.
- Rule 13: limits on accepting gifts; Rule 15: no private trade or employment; Rule 16: no speculative investment; Rule 18: periodic returns of movable and immovable property.
- All India Services (Conduct) Rules, 1968 apply to IAS/IPS/IFoS; PCS officers in Punjab are governed by the state's Government Employees (Conduct) Rules, 1966.
- Code of ethics (values) vs code of conduct (enforceable rules): the 2nd ARC recommended a statutory Public Services Bill with a code of ethics; the draft Public Services Bill (2007) lapsed.

### Citizens' charters and right to service — evolution

- UK Citizen's Charter launched in 1991 under John Major; India adopted the idea at the Chief Ministers' conference of 1997.
- Sevottam model (DARPG, 2006) linked to Bureau of Indian Standards IS 15700:2005 — charter, grievance redress and service-delivery capability.
- Madhya Pradesh Public Services Guarantee Act, 2010 was the first right-to-service law; Punjab Right to Service Act, 2011 followed with time-limits, designated officers and penalties.
- Punjab Transparency and Accountability in Delivery of Public Services Act, 2018 underpins Sewa Kendras' single-window delivery.
- Central Bill on time-bound delivery of services and grievance redress (2011) lapsed in 2014; CPGRAMS remains the central grievance portal.
- Weaknesses: charters drafted without consultation, no penalties, low awareness; fixes — consultation, compensation for delay, independent review, published performance data.

### Anti-corruption architecture — dates and details

- Santhanam Committee (1962–64) → Central Vigilance Commission set up by executive resolution (1964); CVC made statutory by the CVC Act, 2003 after Vineet Narain v. Union of India (1997).
- CBI functions under the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, 1946; state consent is needed for investigations in states.
- Prevention of Corruption (Amendment) Act, 2018: bribe-giving is an offence (Sec 8), liability for commercial organisations (Sec 9), prior approval to probe decisions taken in official functions (Sec 17A), trial to be completed ideally within 2 years.
- Lokpal and Lokayuktas Act, 2013: Lokpal operational from 2019 (first chairperson Justice Pinaki Chandra Ghose); states required to set up Lokayuktas; Maharashtra passed the first Lokayukta law (1971).
- Punjab: Vigilance Bureau (state anti-corruption agency); Punjab Lokpal Act, 1996 provides a state ombudsman for public functionaries; Punjab State Information Commission hears RTI appeals.
- International: UNCAC (2003; India ratified 2011); OECD-style asset recovery and beneficial-ownership disclosure as emerging norms.
- Measure: Transparency International's CPI 2025 ranked India 91st of 182 countries (released February 2026).

### Corporate governance extras

- Companies Act, 2013: independent directors (Sec 149, Schedule IV code), audit committee (Sec 177), mandatory auditor rotation (Sec 139: individual 5 years, firm two terms of 5 years), class-action suits (Sec 245).
- National Financial Reporting Authority (NFRA) notified in 2018 to regulate auditors of large companies.
- Kotak Committee (2017): stronger board independence and disclosure; separation of Chair and MD/CEO for top listed companies was later made voluntary by SEBI (2022).
- Insolvency and Bankruptcy Code, 2016: creditor-in-control resolution reduced promoter impunity in defaults.
- Stewardship codes for institutional investors and related-party transaction approvals by minority shareholders.
- Public-sector link: governance of state PSUs — Punjab had 49 State PSUs (16 inactive) as of March 2023 per CAG, with large losses concentrated in power and procurement bodies (PRS, 2026).

### Work culture, public funds and technology

- Mission Karmayogi (2020) — competency-based capacity building through the iGOT platform; shift from rules to roles.
- e-Office and file-tracking cut delay and discretion; Government e-Marketplace (GeM, 2016) for transparent procurement; PFMS and DBT reduce leakage.
- Outcome budgeting and social audit link spending to results, not just to expenditure.
- Use of public funds in Punjab: committed spending of about 72% of revenue receipts and a Rs 15,550 crore power subsidy (Budget 2026-27, PRS) leave little for capital spending — a probity-in-priorities issue.
- Red-tape remedies: delegation, time-limits, single-window clearances (Punjab Right to Business Act, 2020 for MSMEs), and protection for bona fide decisions so honest officers are not paralysed by fear of vigilance cases.
- Ethical work culture indicators: punctuality, fair transfers and postings, merit-based promotions, respectful treatment of citizens, zero tolerance for harassment.

**2.** "The Right to Information Act has been a powerful tool for transparency, but recent amendments threaten its spirit." Critically examine. (250 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: RTI 2005 as 'master key' (2nd ARC).
- Achievements: exposure of scams, entitlement tracking, empowerment.
- Concerns: 2019 amendment, DPDP Act 2023 on Sec 8(1)(j), pendency, non-compliance with Sec 4, attacks on activists.
- Counter: privacy rights after Puttaswamy, misuse of RTI.
- Way forward: robust Sec 4 disclosure, timely appointments, public-interest override.
- Conclusion: balance privacy and transparency.

</details>

**3.** Differentiate between a code of ethics and a code of conduct. Why does India need a comprehensive code of ethics for civil servants? (150 words)

<details><summary>Answer outline</summary>

- Intro: values vs rules.
- Code of ethics: integrity, impartiality, empathy; aspirational.
- Code of conduct: CCS (Conduct) Rules 1964 — specific prohibitions.
- Need: grey areas, changing roles, public trust; 2nd ARC 4th report and draft Public Services Bill ideas.
- Conclusion: combine rules with internalised values.

</details>
